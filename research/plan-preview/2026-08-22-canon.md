## 1. Horvitz (1999) — Principles of mixed-initiative user interfaces
Citation: Horvitz, E. (1999). Principles of mixed-initiative user interfaces. Proc. CHI '99, Pittsburgh, pp. 159–166. doi:10.1145/302979.303030
Confirmed: CHI 1999 (Crossref record for the DOI); PDF from author site.
URLs: https://erichorvitz.com/chi99horvitz.pdf ; https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/302979.303030
Status: full-text.
Abstract (verbatim, p.1): "Recent debate has centered on the relative promise of focusing user-interface research on developing new metaphors and tools that enhance users' abilities to directly manipulate objects versus directing effort toward developing interface agents that provide automation. In this paper, we review principles that show promise for allowing engineers to enhance human—computer interaction through an elegant coupling of automated services with direct manipulation. Key ideas will be highlighted in terms of the LookOut system for scheduling and meeting management."
Quotes (section "Principles for mixed-initiative UI", p.1–2):
- "(2) Considering uncertainty about a user's goals. Computers are often uncertain about the goals and current the focus of attention of a user."
- "(4) Inferring ideal action in light of costs, benefits, and uncertainties. Automated actions taken under uncertainty in a user's goals and attention are associated with context-dependent costs and benefits."
- "(5) Employing dialog to resolve key uncertainties. If a system is uncertain about a user's intentions, it should be able to engage in an efficient dialog with the user, considering the costs of potentially bothering a user needlessly."
- Also (8): "A preference for 'doing less' but doing it correctly under uncertainty can provide user's with a valuable advance towards a solution and minimize the need for costly undoing or backtracking." and (9): "We should design agents with the assumption that users may often wish to complete or refine an analysis provided by an agent."
Contribution: gives the decision-theoretic rationale for showing a plan and asking rather than acting: when the expected cost of a wrong autonomous action exceeds the cost of a dialog, the system should surface its guess for review.

## 2. Suchman (1985/1987/2007) — Plans and Situated Actions
Citation: Suchman, L. A. (1985). Plans and Situated Actions: The problem of human-machine communication. Xerox PARC report ISL-6, February 1985. Book edition: Cambridge University Press, 1987. 2nd ed.: Human-Machine Reconfigurations: Plans and Situated Actions, Cambridge University Press, 2007 (ISBN 9780521675888).
Confirmed: ISL-6 title page (Xerox PARC, February 1985, ISL-6) read from the scanned PDF. 1987 and 2007 editions confirmed only from search-result listings (Cambridge page returned HTTP 500); not fetched.
URLs: https://bitsavers.trailing-edge.com/pdf/xerox/parc/techReports/ISL-6_Plans_and_Situated_Actions.pdf ; https://cs.colby.edu/courses/J19/cs267/papers/Suchman-PlansAndSituatedActions.pdf
Status: full-text (ISL-6 report, OCR layer). Book editions: located-but-not-extractable.
Abstract (verbatim, ISL-6): "This thesis considers two alternative views of purposeful action and shared understanding. The first, adopted by researchers in Cognitive Science, views the organization and significance of action as derived from plans, which are prerequisite to and prescribe action at whatever level of detail one might imagine. ... The second view, drawn from recent work in social science, treats plans as derivative from situated action. Situated action as such comprises necessarily ad hoc responses to the actions of others and to the contingencies of particular situations. Rather than depend upon the reliable recognition of intent, successful interaction consists in the collaborative production of intelligibility through mutual access to situation resources, and through the detection, repair or exploitation of differences in understanding. As common sense formulations designed to accomodate the unforseeable contingences of situated action, plans are inherently vague."
Quotes:
- Ch. 4 opening (PDF p.44): "common sense notions like plans are not faulted versions of scientific models of action, but rather are resources for people's practical deliberations about action. As projective and retrospective accounts of action, plans are themselves located in the larger context of some practical activity."
- Ch. 4 (PDF p.44): "rather than build a theory of action out of a theory of plans, the aim is to investigate how people produce and find evidence for plans in the course of situated action."
- Ch. 4 five propositions (PDF p.44): "(i) plans are derivative from actions in situ; (ii) in the course of situated action, deliberation arises when otherwise transparent activity becomes in some way problematic"
- Ch. 3 (PDF p.30): "The coherence of situated action is tied in essential ways not to a priori prescriptions, but to the action's particular circumstances."
Contribution: the caution on the pattern — a previewed plan is a resource for the actor's deliberation and for repair, not a script that guarantees what execution will do; holding execution "to the plan" must allow for situated deviation and its repair.

## 3. Sarter & Woods (1995); Sarter, Woods & Billings (1997)
### 3a. Sarter & Woods (1995) — How in the world did we ever get into that mode?
Citation: Sarter, N. B. & Woods, D. D. (1995). How in the World Did We Ever Get into That Mode? Mode Error and Awareness in Supervisory Control. Human Factors 37(1), 5–19.
Confirmed: TRID record (Human Factors, vol. 37 no. 1, pp. 5–19, 1995). Sage page returned 403.
URLs: https://trid.trb.org/view/426376 ; https://resilienceroundup.com/issues/how-in-the-world-did-we-ever-get-into-that-mode/ (secondary)
Status: located-but-not-extractable (no PDF on disk; TRID gives a paraphrased abstract only).
Abstract (TRID paraphrase): new technology "is flexible in the sense that it provides practitioners with a large number of functions and options," but operators must now track which mode the automation is in and what it is doing; empirical data from pilot–automation studies show mode proliferation without cognitive support produces mode errors.
Quote reproduced on the resilienceroundup page and attributed to the paper (secondary source, verify before citing): "New attentional demands are created as the practitioner must keep track of which mode the device is in so as to select the correct inputs when communicating with the automation and to track what the automation is doing, why it is doing it, and what it will do next."
Contribution: names the three questions a plan preview answers in advance — what it is doing, why, and what it will do next — and shows the cost when they are answered only by opaque mode indications.

### 4b. Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000)
Citation: Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T. B., & Wickens, C. D. (2000). A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation. IEEE Trans. Systems, Man, and Cybernetics—Part A 30(3), 286–297.
Confirmed: running header in the PDF: "IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON SYSTEMS, MAN, AND CYBERNETICS—PART A: SYSTEMS AND HUMANS, VOL. 30, NO. 3, MAY 2000", p. 286.
URL: http://www.cs.uml.edu/~holly/91.549/readings/sheridan-autonomy.pdf
Status: full-text (Table I itself did not survive text extraction; the prose restating it did).
Abstract (verbatim, p.286): "Technical developments in computer hardware and software now make it possible to introduce automation into virtually all aspects of human-machine systems. Given these technical capabilities, which system functions should be automated and to what extent? We outline a model for types and levels of automation that provides a framework and an objective basis for making such choices. Appropriate selection is important because automation does not merely supplant but changes human activity and can impose new coordination demands on the human operator. We propose that automation can be applied to four broad classes of functions: 1) information acquisition; 2) information analysis; 3) decision and action selection; and 4) action implementation. ..."
Quotes (p.287):
- "At level 4, the computer suggests one decision alternative, but the human retains authority for executing that alternative or choosing another one. At a higher level 6, the system gives the human only a limited time for a veto before carrying out the decision choice."
- "a conflict detection and resolution system that notifies an air traffic controller of a conflict in the flight paths of two aircraft and suggests a resolution would qualify as level 4 automation. Under level 6 or higher, the system would automatically execute its own resolution advisory, unless the controller intervened."
- (p.293) "conflict resolution automation can provide alternatives to the controller but should not select one of them without controller involvement" and "having some human involvement at the action stage provides a 'last chance'" for error trapping.
Contribution: places plan preview precisely on the levels scale — the agent proposes (level 4/5), the actor approves before execution — and argues that this is where error-trapping by the human still works.

## 5. Billings (1991) — Human-centered aircraft automation: a concept and guidelines

Confirmed: NTRS record 19910022821 (NASA-TM-103885, 1991).

Status: full-text (scanned; OCR has artefacts, corrected only for obvious character errors inside quotes below).
Abstract (verbatim, NTRS): "Aircraft automation is examined and its effects on flight crews. Generic guidelines are proposed for the design and use of automation in transport aircraft, in the hope of stimulating increased and more effective dialogue among designers of automated cockpits, purchasers of automated aircraft, and the pilots who must fly those aircraft in line operations."
Quotes:

Contribution: "management by consent" is the aviation name for plan preview with approval gates; Billings also supplies the failure mode the pattern must design against — consent becoming perfunctory.

## 6. Bainbridge (1983) — Ironies of automation
Citation: Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. Automatica 19(6), 775–779. doi:10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8
Confirmed: PDF header "Automatica, Vol. 19, No. 6, pp. 775–779, 1983 ... Pergamon Press"; Wikipedia entry lists the same.
URL: https://ckrybus.com/static/papers/Bainbridge_1983_Automatica.pdf
Status: full-text.
Abstract (verbatim, p.775): "This paper discusses the ways in which automation of industrial processes may expand rather than eliminate problems with the human operator. Some comments will be made on methods of alleviating these problems within the 'classic' approach of leaving the operator with responsibility for abnormal conditions, and on the potential for continued use of the human operator for on-line decision-making within human-computer collaboration."
Quotes:
- §1.1.3 Monitoring (p.776): "A more serious irony is that the automatic control system has been put in because it can do the job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively."
- §1.1.3 (p.776): "One can therefore only expect the operator to monitor the computer's decisions at some meta-level, to decide whether the computer's decisions are 'acceptable'. ... The human monitor has been given an impossible task."
- §1.1.1 (p.775): "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one."
- §2 (p.777): "If the human operator must monitor the details of computer decision making then, ironically, it is necessary for the computer to make these decisions using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow"
Contribution: plan preview moves the actor's check from real-time monitoring of execution (which Bainbridge shows is an impossible task) to a meta-level review of intended decisions before they run, at a pace the actor can follow.

## 7. Parasuraman & Manzey (2010) — Complacency and bias in human use of automation
Citation: Parasuraman, R. & Manzey, D. H. (2010). Complacency and bias in human use of automation: an attentional integration. Human Factors 52(3), 381–410. doi:10.1177/0018720810376055
Confirmed: Sage journal page (Human Factors vol. 52 issue 3, 2010).
URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055
Status: abstract-only (TU Berlin repository PDF redirected to an HTML shell; not retrieved).
Abstract (Sage page, summarised by the fetcher; one sentence returned verbatim): automation complacency emerges under multiple-task load when manual tasks compete with the automated task for attention, is found in both naive and expert participants, and is not overcome by simple practice; automation bias produces omission and commission errors with imperfect decision aids. Verbatim: "Complacency and automation bias represent different manifestations of overlapping automation-induced phenomena, with attention playing a central role."
Contribution: warns that a review step is itself vulnerable — actors under load will rubber-stamp a proposed plan (automation bias) — so the preview must make the risky parts of the plan demand attention.

## 8. Vermeulen et al. (2013); Djajadiningrat et al. (2002)
### 8a. Vermeulen, Luyten, van den Hoven & Coninx (2013)
Citation: Vermeulen, J., Luyten, K., van den Hoven, E., & Coninx, K. (2013). Crossing the Bridge over Norman's Gulf of Execution: Revealing Feedforward's True Identity. Proc. CHI 2013, pp. 1931–1940. doi:10.1145/2470654.2466255
Confirmed: Semantic Scholar record (CHI 2013); PDF page footer 1931.
URL: http://www.elisevandenhoven.com/publications/vermeulen-chi13.pdf
Status: full-text.
Abstract (verbatim, p.1931): "Feedback and affordances are two of the most well-known principles in interaction design. Unfortunately, the related and equally important notion of feedforward has not been given as much consideration. Nevertheless, feedforward is a powerful design principle for bridging Norman's Gulf of Execution. We reframe feedforward by disambiguating it from related design principles such as feedback and perceived affordances, and identify new classes of feedforward. In addition, we present a reference framework that provides a means for designers to explore and recognize different opportunities for feedforward."
Quotes:
- Introduction (p.1931): feedforward "tells users what the result of their action will be. ... A simple example of feedforward is a label on a button: the label tells users what happens if they push the button."
- "Djajadiningrat – Going Beyond Affordances" (p.1934): "while feedback is communicated during or after the action, feedforward is information that is offered before the action takes place. Whereas feedback informs the user about the action that is carried out, feedforward informs the user about what the result of their action will be."
- (p.1932) "Note that, unlike feedforward, perceived affordances do not communicate the purpose of an action."
- (p.1934) "Djajadiningrat et al. [6] argue that inviting the appropriate action is a prerequisite for feedforward, but it is not sufficient."
Contribution: plan preview is feedforward at the level of a whole course of action — information about what the result will be, offered before execution — and this paper supplies the vocabulary to separate it from feedback and from affordance.

## 9. Coppers et al. (2019) — Fortunettes
Citation: Coppers, S., Luyten, K., Vanacken, D., Navarre, D., Palanque, P., & Gris, C. (2019). Fortunettes: Feedforward about the Future State of GUI Widgets. Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact. 3 (EICS), Article 20, pp. 1–20. doi:10.1145/3331162
Confirmed: Semantic Scholar record (Proc. ACM Hum. Comput. Interact., 2019); OATAO repository record.
URLs: https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3331162 ; https://oatao.univ-toulouse.fr/24921/
Status: abstract-only (both open PDF mirrors returned empty files).
Abstract (verbatim): "Feedback is commonly used to explain what happened in an interface. What if questions, on the other hand, remain mostly unanswered. In this paper, we present the concept of enhanced widgets capable of visualizing their future state, which helps users to understand what will happen without committing to an action. We describe two approaches to extend GUI toolkits to support widget-level feedforward, and illustrate the usefulness of widget-level feedforward in a standardized interface to control the weather radar in commercial aircraft. In our evaluation, we found that users require less clicks to achieve tasks and are more confident about their actions when feedforward information was available. These findings suggest that widget-level feedforward is highly suitable in applications the user is unfamiliar with, or when high confidence is desirable."
Contribution: empirical evidence that showing the future state "without committing to an action" raises confidence and reduces wasted actions — the micro-scale version of previewing an agent's plan.

## 10. Dragan, Lee & Srinivasa (2013) — Legibility and predictability of robot motion
Citation: Dragan, A. D., Lee, K. C. T., & Srinivasa, S. S. (2013). Legibility and predictability of robot motion. Proc. 8th ACM/IEEE Int. Conf. on Human-Robot Interaction (HRI '13), Tokyo, pp. 301–308. doi:10.1109/HRI.2013.6483603
Confirmed: Crossref record (HRI 2013, pp. 301–308); CMU RI publication page.
URLs: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1109/HRI.2013.6483603 ; https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/legibility-and-predictability-of-robot-motion ; PDF https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/storage/publications/pub_files/2013/3/legiilitypredictabilityIEEE.pdf
Status: full-text.
Abstract (verbatim): "A key requirement for seamless human-robot collaboration is for the robot to make its intentions clear to its human collaborator. A collaborative robot's motion must be legible, or intent-expressive. Legibility is often described in the literature as and effect of predictable, unsurprising, or expected motion. Our central insight is that predictability and legibility are fundamentally different and often contradictory properties of motion. We develop a formalism to mathematically define and distinguish predictability and legibility of motion. We formalize the two based on inferences between trajectories and goals in opposing directions, drawing the analogy to action interpretation in psychology. We then propose mathematical models for these inferences based on optimizing cost, drawing the analogy to the principle of rational action. Our experiments validate our formalism's prediction that predictability and legibility can contradict, and provide support for our models. Our findings indicate that for robots to seamlessly collaborate with humans, they must change the way they plan their motion."
Quotes (abstract, p.301): "A collaborative robot's motion must be legible, or intent-expressive."; "predictability and legibility are fundamentally different and often contradictory properties of motion."; "We formalize the two based on inferences between trajectories and goals in opposing directions".
Contribution: the legibility/predictability split transfers to plans — a preview should let the actor infer the goal from the steps (legible) and, once approved, the steps should match what the actor expects (predictable); these two can pull against each other.

### 11b. Chakraborti, Sreedharan, Zhang & Kambhampati (2017) — Plan explanations as model reconciliation
Citation: Chakraborti, T., Sreedharan, S., Zhang, Y., & Kambhampati, S. (2017). Plan Explanations as Model Reconciliation: Moving Beyond Explanation as Soliloquy. Proc. IJCAI 2017, pp. 156–163.
Confirmed: IJCAI proceedings page.
URLs: https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/23 ; https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/0023.pdf
Status: full-text.
Abstract (verbatim, p.1): "When AI systems interact with humans in the loop, they are often called on to provide explanations for their plans and behavior. Past work on plan explanations primarily involved the AI system explaining the correctness of its plan and the rationale for its decision in terms of its own model. Such soliloquy is wholly inadequate in most realistic scenarios where the humans have domain and task models that differ significantly from that used by the AI system. We posit that the explanations are best studied in light of these differing models. In particular, we show how explanation can be seen as a 'model reconciliation problem' (MRP), where the AI system in effect suggests changes to the human's model, so as to make its plan be optimal with respect to that changed human model. We will study the properties of such explanations, present algorithms for automatically computing them, and evaluate the performance of the algorithms."
Quotes:
- §1 (p.1): "When the robot's plan is different from what the human would expect given his model of the world, the robot will be called on to 'explain' its plan. We posit that such explanations should be seen as the robot's attempt to move the human's model to be in conformance with its own."
- §1 (p.1): the MRP "aims to make minimal changes to the human's model to bring it closer to the robot's model, in order to make the robot's plan optimal".
Contribution: when the actor reads a previewed plan and objects, the disagreement is usually a model gap; explanation attached to the plan should target that gap, minimally.

## 12. Mayer et al. (2015) — User interaction models for disambiguation in PBE
Citation: Mayer, M., Soares, G., Grechkin, M., Le, V., Marron, M., Polozov, O., Singh, R., Zorn, B., & Gulwani, S. (2015). User Interaction Models for Disambiguation in Programming by Example. Proc. UIST '15, Charlotte NC, pp. 291–301. doi:10.1145/2807442.2807459
Confirmed: PDF copyright block "UIST '15, November 08-11, 2015, Charlotte, NC, USA"; Semantic Scholar record.
URL: https://alexpolozov.com/papers/uist2015-disambiguation.pdf
Status: full-text.
Abstract (verbatim, p.1): "... examples, though often easy to provide, are an ambiguous specification of the user's intent. Because of that, a key impedance in adoption of PBE systems is the lack of user confidence in the correctness of the program that was synthesized by the system. We present two novel user interaction models that communicate actionable information to the user to help resolve ambiguity in the examples. One of these models allows the user to effectively navigate between the huge set of programs that are consistent with the examples provided by the user. The other model uses active learning to ask directed example-based questions to the user on the test input data over which the user intends to run the synthesized program. Our user studies show that each of these models significantly reduces the number of errors in the performed task without any difference in completion time."
Quotes (§1, p.2):
- "A typical PBE interface would pick the top-ranked program and use it to automate the user's task; possibly this top-ranked program can even be shown to the user. We propose a novel user interaction model, called Program Navigation, that allows the user to navigate between all programs synthesized ... Furthermore, these programs are paraphrased in English for easy readability."
- "Conversational Clarification, wherein the system asks questions to the user to resolve ambiguities in the user's specification with respect to the available test data."
- "Conversational Clarification is a proactive interface that asks clarifying questions, whereas Program Navigation is a reactive interface that expects an explicit correction of a mistake."
Contribution: two concrete shapes for reviewing a synthesised plan before it runs over all data — read the paraphrased program, or answer targeted questions on the cases where candidate plans diverge.

## 13. Kandel, Paepcke, Hellerstein & Heer (2011) — Wrangler

### 17b. Anderson, Kirwan, Jenkins, Eargle, Howard & Vance (2015)
Citation: Anderson, B. B., Kirwan, C. B., Jenkins, J. L., Eargle, D., Howard, S., & Vance, A. (2015). How Polymorphic Warnings Reduce Habituation in the Brain: Insights from an fMRI Study. Proc. CHI 2015, Seoul. doi:10.1145/2702123.2702322

Status: abstract-only (BYU PDF download returned an empty file).
Abstract (verbatim): "Research on security warnings consistently points to habituation as a key reason why users ignore security warnings. ## 20. Norman (1990) — The 'problem' with automation
Citation: Norman, D. A. (1990). The 'problem' with automation: inappropriate feedback and interaction, not 'over-automation'. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London B 327(1241), 585–593. doi:10.1098/rstb.1990.0101
Confirmed: PubMed record PMID 1970904 (Philos Trans R Soc Lond B Biol Sci. 1990 Apr 12;327(1241):585-93).
URL: https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=1970904&rettype=abstract&retmode=text
Status: abstract-only (Royal Society page 403; NTRS mirror returned HTML).
Abstract (verbatim, PubMed): "As automation increasingly takes its place in industry, especially high risk industry, it is often blamed for causing harm and increasing the chance of human error when failures do occur. I propose that the problem is not the presence of automation, but rather its inappropriate design. The problem is that the operations under normal operating conditions are performed appropriately, but there is inadequate feedback and interaction with the humans who must control the overall conduct of the task. When the situations exceed the capabilities of the automatic equipment, then the inadequate feedback leads to difficulties for the human controllers. The problem, I suggest, is that the automation is at an intermediate level of intelligence, powerful enough to take over control that used to be done by people, but not powerful enough to handle all abnormalities. Moreover, its level of intelligence is insufficient to provide the continual, appropriate feedback that occurs naturally among human operators. This is the source of the current difficulties. To solve this problem, the automation should either be made less intelligent or more so, but the current level is quite inappropriate. The overall message is that it is possible to reduce error through appropriate design considerations. Appropriate design should assume the existence of error, it should continually provide feedback, it should continually interact with operators in an effective manner, and it should allow for the worst situations possible. What is needed is a soft, compliant technology, not a rigid, formal one."
Quotes (abstract): "there is inadequate feedback and interaction with the humans who must control the overall conduct of the task."; "Appropriate design should assume the existence of error, it should continually provide feedback, it should continually interact with operators in an effective manner".
Contribution: the problem is not that the agent acts but that it acts without telling the actor what it intends; plan preview is the "continual interaction" Norman asks for, placed before execution.
