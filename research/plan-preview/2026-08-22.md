# Research: previewing an agent's intended actions before it executes them — 2026-08-22

Query: what showing a plan before execution does to outcomes and to trust, what makes a plan reviewable, what binds execution to it, when reviewing becomes ritual, and where the move ends and its neighbours begin. Generated via `/research-gate`, run while maturing the `plan-preview` seed.

## Context

*Topic*: previewing an agent's intended actions before it executes them.

*Interaction*: an actor asks for something consequential; before the system does the work it states what it intends to do — steps, targets, expected effects, cost — as an object the actor can read, correct, or refuse. Execution follows only after that, and is held to what was shown.

*Setting*: delegated and agentic work (coding agents, research agents, automation tools, data-transformation tools) where acting straight away would spend real work on a misread request. Approval gate, change review, bounded autonomy, cost transparency, and transparent reasoning are the neighbours; dry runs, refactoring previews, feedforward, and flight-plan programming are the pre-AI lineage.

*Questions driving retrieval*:

1. Does showing a plan before execution change outcomes — fewer misreadings caught early, better final results — or mainly change how the actor feels about the agent?
2. What makes a plan reviewable: granularity of steps, effects versus intentions, a sample of output versus a description of it, cost alongside scope?
3. What binds execution to the previewed plan, and what happens to trust and oversight when the two drift apart?
4. When does reviewing plans become ritual — how fast do actors stop reading, and what keeps a plan review a judgement rather than a click?
5. Where is the boundary with neighbouring moves — approving an action, reviewing a diff, showing reasoning during execution, bounding authority in advance?

## Retrieved papers

1. *An Experimental Comparison of Cognitive Forcing Functions for Execution Plans in AI-Assisted Writing* (Ghosh, Sarkar, Lindley, Poelitz — 2026). Controlled experiment, N=214, plus 12 think-aloud interviews. [arXiv:2601.18033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18033)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q4. Full-text read. Defines an execution plan as "a structured description of the steps the system proposes to follow in order to fulfill a user's request", shown so users can "verify that the system has correctly interpreted their intent". Plans of four to six steps, three of five tasks carrying one planted error; overreliance operationalised as accepting a plan "despite the presence of errors". The premise the study confirms: "AI-generated plans are themselves complex artifacts that users may accept uncritically, particularly when they appear coherent and plausible." A forcing function aimed at the plan's assumptions raised the rate at which participants revised their readiness rating (χ²=7.89, p=.03) without raising cognitive load; the variant participants *preferred* (what-if) was not the one that worked.
   - Transfer: *strong*. The only controlled study that treats the plan itself as the object of overreliance.

2. *Plan-Then-Execute: An Empirical Study of User Trust and Team Performance When Using LLM Agents As A Daily Assistant* (He, Demartini, Gadiraju — 2025). N=248, six tasks at different risk levels, 2×2 on user involvement in planning and in execution. [arXiv:2502.01390](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01390)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q2, Q4. Full-text read. Plan quality governs outcome: execution accuracy was 1.8% with low-quality plans, 59% with plans that could mislead action prediction, 66.7% with high-quality plans. Letting users edit, add, delete, and split steps "fails to calibrate user trust", and plan quality *decreased* with user involvement in every task but the one whose initial plan was flawed. "The plausible plans generated by the LLMs can mislead users into trusting the LLM agents when they are in fact wrong." Calibrated trust on high-risk tasks with imperfect plans was 0.11–0.21 against 0.81–0.94 on low-risk tasks with good plans. Participants found plans "too detailed"; involvement "poses a high cognitive load on users and decreases user confidence in their decisions".
   - Transfer: *strong*. Same interaction, with the clearest negative result on plan editing.

3. *Comparing Human Oversight Strategies for Computer-Use Agents* (Chen, Zhang, Chen, et al. — 2026). Mixed-methods, N=48, live web environment, four oversight strategies. [arXiv:2604.04918](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04918)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q3, Q4, Q5. Full-text read. Frames oversight on two axes — *delegation structure* (where default authority sits during execution) and *engagement level* (plan versus action trace). The plan-first strategy (users "authorize and can revise the task structure before execution begins, after which the agent proceeds autonomously") had the lowest rate of problematic-action occurrence (60.4% against 88.5% for per-action confirmation and 90.1% for risk-gated), and the lowest intervention success once a problem was visible (9.2% against 26.4%). "The main bottleneck in oversight is not the absence of control but the failure of recognition"; breakdowns "frequently involved rationalization: participants normalized questionable behavior." Trust favoured per-action confirmation on low-consequence tasks and reversed on high-consequence ones.
   - Transfer: *strong*. The one direct comparison of plan-level against action-level oversight.

4. *Cocoa: Co-Planning and Co-Execution with AI Agents* (Feng, Pu, Latzke, August, Siangliulue, Bragg, Weld, Zhang, Chang — CHI 2026). System plus lab study (n=16) and week-long deployment (n=7). [arXiv:2412.10999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10999)
   - Speaks to: Q2, Q3. Full-text read. Interactive plans the agent proposes and "the user can edit to their liking", each step assignable to user or agent; planning and execution interleaved "so that plans can adjust after partial execution". 32.6% of steps were edited and 17.4% reassigned; "most participants toggled all plan steps … to the agent". "Plans are bound to shift as the user reviews agent outputs and becomes inspired with new ideas"; "the length of the plan should be calibrated depending on the task"; backtracking to a step "was more helpful than digging through a chat conversation".
   - Transfer: *strong*. Argues that the preview and the run should not be separate stages, which is the designed form of drift.

5. *Magentic-UI: Towards Human-in-the-loop Agentic Systems* (Mozannar, Bansal, Tan, Fourney, Dibia, et al. — 2025). Open-source agent interface; benchmarks, simulated-user and real-user studies. [arXiv:2507.22358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22358)
   - Speaks to: Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5. Full-text read. A plan is "a sequence of natural language instructions" with agent, title, and details per step, "displayed in an interactive UI component that users can edit directly" before execution; steps become collapsible banners during the run and the orchestrator re-plans on failure. Action guards are a separate mechanism keyed to an irreversibility heuristic (always / maybe / never). A simulated user holding side information the agent lacked raised task completion from 30.3% to 51.9% while being asked for help in only 10% of tasks. Real users "often edited the plan before execution, usually to incorporate subjective preferences"; "Plan editing may become too cost-prohibitive as the number of steps to review increases." Finished runs can be saved as reusable plans.
   - Transfer: *strong*. Separates plan review from per-action approval in one design, and shows what the actor's edits are for.

6. *Human oversight of agentic systems in practice* (Dhanorkar, Passi, Vorvoreanu — 2026). Interviews with 17 developers using software agents. [arXiv:2606.05391](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05391)
   - Speaks to: Q3, Q4. Full-text read. Finds four forms of oversight work — a priori control, co-planning, real-time monitoring, post hoc review — with co-planning defined as "preliminary goal-setting activities that agents and developers jointly perform to establish common ground". Drift is reported as routine: "sometimes [the agent] follows your instructions, sometimes it tries to go on a tangent and do its own stuff" (P11). Among the heuristics developers adopt to cope with review cost, the paper names treating "an agent's plan [as] a faithful proxy for its actual working" — reviewing the plan instead of the code. Design recommendation for co-planning: "recommend sub-tasks with resource costs and execution previews".
   - Transfer: *strong*. Field evidence for both the drift force and the plan-as-proxy shortcut.

7. *Reframing LLM Agent Security as an Agent-Human Interaction Problem* (Wang, Li, Tian — 2026). Analysis of 59 papers, 21 production agent systems, 26 security plugins. [arXiv:2605.24309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24309)
   - Speaks to: Q4, Q5. Full-text read. Runtime approval is deployed in 15 of 21 systems and is "the only category that pauses agent execution to defer a decision to the human"; a single task "can trigger dozens of approval prompts", users become "OK-button machines", and the paper imports the browser-security finding that users click through 90%+ of SSL warnings. "Intent anchoring" — validating every action against the stated request — has zero production deployment, partly because "users cannot perceive the safeguard". Proposed direction: "proactive ambiguity detection (resolving ambiguities during planning rather than interrupting at runtime)".
   - Transfer: *strong* for Q4 and Q5; the security framing is wider than the pattern.

8. *How to Steer Your Multi-Agent System: Human-LLM Collaborative Planning* (He, Kim, Zhang, et al. — 2026). Design space plus prototype (AMBIPOM), user study (N=13) and benchmark. [arXiv:2605.23023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23023)
   - Speaks to: Q2. Full-text read. Plan edits vary on *mode* (structural direct manipulation versus natural-language feedback), *scope* (global versus targeted to a selected subgraph), and *level* (add/edit/delete versus merge/split/replan). Users "assembled hybrid workflows per iteration", treated manual editing as high-control but high-effort and global text feedback as "rolling a dice", and favoured LLM-assisted direct manipulation as the middle. "Trust increased in-task but verification declined later in sessions." In the benchmark, targeted feedback preserved plan stability but the LLM's structural correctness suffered.
   - Transfer: *strong* for the granularity question; multi-agent DAG plans are more elaborate than most product plans.

## Convergent findings

### 1. The preview's value lands upstream, in what gets executed — not in the moment of review

*Synthesis*. Chen et al.: authorising the plan first cut the occurrence of problematic actions (60% against ~90%) but left users *worse* at stopping a problem once it appeared. Mozannar et al.: a user who supplied side information at planning raised completion from 30% to 52% while being consulted in 10% of tasks. He et al.: the plan's quality set execution accuracy (1.8% to 66.7%). HULA: 82% of plans approved and the approved plans carried through to merged PRs. The common shape: the plan is where the actor's knowledge that the agent lacks — preferences, context, scope — gets in; once in, the run benefits whether or not the actor watches it. The preview is not primarily a catch-net for the run; it is the point where the run is set.

*Papers*: Chen et al. 2026; Mozannar et al. 2025; He, Demartini & Gadiraju 2025; Takerngsaksiri et al. 2024.

*Disagreement*: He et al. found plan quality *fell* when users edited plans that were already good; Cocoa and Magentic-UI report edits as mostly beneficial. The difference is probably who holds the missing knowledge: researchers editing plans for their own projects versus crowd participants editing plans for unfamiliar tasks.

### 2. A plausible plan is accepted the way a plausible answer is

*Synthesis*. Ghosh et al. set out from "users may accept uncritically, particularly when they appear coherent and plausible" and needed a forcing function on the plan's assumptions to raise revision rates. He et al.: "plausible plans … can mislead users into trusting the LLM agents when they are in fact wrong", and editing rights did not calibrate trust. AMBIPOM: "verification declined later in sessions." Chen et al. name the mechanism — rationalisation rather than inattention; users "normalized questionable behavior". Dhanorkar et al. record the shortcut as a working heuristic: the plan stands in for the work. The seed's "ritual" force is real, but the literature locates it in plausibility and session length as much as in prompt frequency — the same overreliance the library already documents for answers and reasoning trails.

*Papers*: Ghosh et al. 2026; He, Demartini & Gadiraju 2025; He, Kim et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2026; Dhanorkar et al. 2026.

### 3. Plan and run come apart in two different ways, and current tools hold neither together

*Synthesis*. One kind of drift is designed: Cocoa interleaves planning and execution because "plans are bound to shift as the user reviews agent outputs"; Magentic-UI re-plans on step failure and shows the step list updating. The other is silent: Dhanorkar's developers report agents going "on a tangent", Khan finds that approval gates in five of six frameworks let side effects through while paused, and Wang et al. find that no production system anchors execution to the stated intent. The first kind keeps the preview honest by updating it; the second defeats it. The design question is therefore not "prevent drift" but "make every departure from the shown plan visible as one" — and, for side effects, enforce the hold outside the model.

*Papers*: Feng et al. 2026; Mozannar et al. 2025; Dhanorkar et al. 2026; Khan 2026; Wang, Li & Tian 2026; Piet et al. 2026.

### 4. Review cost scales with the plan, and attention runs out before the plan does

*Synthesis*. He et al.: plans "too detailed", involvement raising load and lowering confidence. Mozannar et al.: "Plan editing may become too cost-prohibitive as the number of steps to review increases." Feng et al.: plan length "should be calibrated depending on the task". Wang et al. and Li et al. give the per-action version — dozens of prompts per task, 81% using "Always Allow" to dismiss, 3 of 16 reading carefully. Tang et al.: oversight itself brings "attentional tunneling". The lever is granularity: preview at the grain the actor can judge, and preview only when there is something to judge.

*Papers*: He, Demartini & Gadiraju 2025; Mozannar et al. 2025; Feng et al. 2026; Wang, Li & Tian 2026; Li et al. 2026; Tang et al. 2025.

### 5. What makes a plan reviewable is a structure the actor can act on at the right grain

*Synthesis*. The usable designs share a shape: discrete steps that can be edited, reordered, removed, or reassigned (Cocoa; Magentic-UI), a named target per step (HULA's file list and per-file change plan), and more than one way to intervene (AMBIPOM's structural versus semantic, global versus targeted). AMBIPOM's users found whole-plan text feedback a gamble and preferred targeted edits with LLM help; Cocoa's users valued backtracking to a step over scrolling a chat. Min and Xia add the spatial form for agents acting on interfaces — showing where the agent will click — and the three dimensions any preview varies on: representation, level of detail, manipulability. RADAR-X supplies the older move: let the actor propose a foil and have the system explain the difference.

*Papers*: Feng et al. 2026; Mozannar et al. 2025; He, Kim et al. 2026; Takerngsaksiri et al. 2024; Min & Xia 2025; Valmeekam et al. 2020.

### 6. Plan-level review and action-level approval are different moves with different effects

*Synthesis*. Chen et al.'s two axes — where authority sits during the run, and whether oversight is organised at plan or action level — separate the two cleanly, and the outcomes differ: plan-first prevents more and intervenes less; per-action confirmation intervenes more and prevents less, and is trusted more only on low-consequence tasks. Magentic-UI implements both as separate mechanisms (co-planning; action guards keyed to irreversibility). Wang et al. sort production mechanisms into runtime approval, scope configuration, and policy specification, and argue for moving ambiguity resolution from runtime into planning. This is the argument for keeping plan preview, approval gate, and bounded autonomy as separate patterns joined by edges rather than one "human in the loop" page.

*Papers*: Chen et al. 2026; Mozannar et al. 2025; Wang, Li & Tian 2026.

### 7. Management by consent: the aviation name for the move, and its failure mode named in 1991

- *Human-Centered Aircraft Automation: A Concept and Guidelines* (Billings — NASA TM 103885, 1991). Full text. "Management by consent implies a situation in which automation, once provided with goals to be achieved, operates autonomously, but requires consent from its manager before instituting successive phases of flight, or certain critical procedures. … The consent principle has important potential advantages, in that it keeps pilots involved and aware of system intent, and provides them the opportunity to intervene if they believe the intended action is inappropriate at that point in time." And the caveat: "it must be informed consent. More fundamental human factors research is needed to identify how to implement it without the consent becoming perfunctory." Management by exception is the contrast — the automation "performs them unless the pilot takes exception" — and under management by delegation the aircraft "will not deviate from these directions unless it is incapable of executing them." Also: "Automated systems must be predictable."
- *A Model for Types and Levels of Human Interaction with Automation* (Parasuraman, Sheridan, Wickens — IEEE SMC-A 2000). Full text. "At level 4, the computer suggests one decision alternative, but the human retains authority for executing that alternative or choosing another one. At a higher level 6, the system gives the human only a limited time for a veto before carrying out the decision choice." "Having some human involvement at the action stage provides a 'last chance'" for error trapping. The original ten-level scale (Sheridan & Verplank 1978) could not be retrieved; its wording is taken from this restatement.
- *Ironies of Automation* (Bainbridge — Automatica 1983). Full text. "The automatic control system has been put in because it can do the job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively." "One can therefore only expect the operator to monitor the computer's decisions at some meta-level, to decide whether the computer's decisions are 'acceptable'. … The human monitor has been given an impossible task." And the condition for it to work at all: "it is necessary for the computer to make these decisions using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which the operator can follow".
- *The 'Problem' with Automation* (Norman — Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 1990). Abstract only. "There is inadequate feedback and interaction with the humans who must control the overall conduct of the task"; design "should continually interact with operators in an effective manner".
- *How in the World Did We Ever Get into That Mode?* (Sarter, Woods — Human Factors 1995) and *Automation Surprises* (Sarter, Woods, Billings — Handbook of Human Factors 1997). Located, not extractable. The secondary quote, to be verified before use: the practitioner must "track what the automation is doing, why it is doing it, and what it will do next." The flight-management-system case — a plan entered into the machine and executed without the crew keeping sight of it — is the canonical pre-AI plan-drift story.
- *Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation* (Parasuraman, Manzey — Human Factors 2010). Abstract only. Complacency under multi-task load, in novices and experts alike, "not overcome by simple practice"; "attention playing a central role".
- Implication: the seed's three forces were all stated by this literature thirty years ago — consent keeps the actor aware of intent (clause 1), the system must not deviate from the directions given (clause 2), and consent must not become perfunctory (clause 3). Billings's "informed consent" is the standard the pattern's solution has to meet, and Bainbridge's "at a rate which the operator can follow" is the argument for moving the check from the run to the plan. Timed auto-approval in shipping products (practice note: Jules, Devin) is level 6 on Sheridan's scale, not level 4, and the page should say so.

### 8. Mixed initiative: the plan as a turn in a dialogue

- *Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces* (Horvitz — CHI 1999). Full text. "(4) Inferring ideal action in light of costs, benefits, and uncertainties"; "(5) Employing dialog to resolve key uncertainties. If a system is uncertain about a user's intentions, it should be able to engage in an efficient dialog with the user, considering the costs of potentially bothering a user needlessly"; "(8) A preference for 'doing less' but doing it correctly under uncertainty … minimize the need for costly undoing or backtracking"; "(9) We should design agents with the assumption that users may often wish to complete or refine an analysis provided by an agent."
- *Mixed-Initiative Interaction* (Allen, Guinn, Horvitz — IEEE Intelligent Systems 1999). Full text. Levels of initiative from "unsolicited reporting" through "subdialogue initiation" to "negotiated mixed initiative"; a system that only "responds to commands from the human, who specifies the plan using the tools available … is not a mixed-initiative system".
- *TRIPS* (Ferguson, Allen — AAAI 1998). Full text. Collaborative plan construction through a "propose-evaluate-confirm protocol".
- Implication: the decision-theoretic rationale for when to preview — when the expected cost of acting on a misread exceeds the cost of asking — and the protocol shape. A plan that can only be approved or refused is the lowest rung; Horvitz's principle 9 and the Cocoa/Magentic-UI edit findings (cluster 5) are the same claim forty years apart.

### 9. Feedforward: what the result will be, before the action

- *Crossing the Bridge over Norman's Gulf of Execution: Revealing Feedforward's True Identity* (Vermeulen, Luyten, van den Hoven, Coninx — CHI 2013). Full text. Feedforward "tells users what the result of their action will be"; quoting Djajadiningrat et al. (DIS 2002, not retrievable): "while feedback is communicated during or after the action, feedforward is information that is offered before the action takes place. Whereas feedback informs the user about the action that is carried out, feedforward informs the user about what the result of their action will be." "Unlike feedforward, perceived affordances do not communicate the purpose of an action."
- *Fortunettes: Feedforward about the Future State of GUI Widgets* (Coppers, Luyten, Vanacken, Navarre, Palanque, Gris — EICS 2019). Abstract only. Widgets "visualizing their future state, which helps users to understand what will happen without committing to an action"; users "require less clicks to achieve tasks and are more confident about their actions".
- *Side Views: Persistent, On-Demand Previews for Open-Ended Tasks* (Terry, Mynatt — UIST 2002). Full text. "On-demand previews of the effects of commands"; persistent previews "can also be chained together to view the effect of multiple commands" — "multiple, simultaneous views of potential future states".
- *Direct Manipulation* (Shneiderman — IEEE Computer 1983) and *Direct Manipulation vs. Interface Agents* (Shneiderman, Maes — interactions 1997). Full text. "Rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose impact on the object of interest is immediately visible." Maes: "when the information load gets too high, there is a point where a person has to delegate"; "using agents implies giving up all control. That's incorrect."
- Implication: plan preview is feedforward at the scale of a course of action, and Min & Xia (above) extend the term to generative systems explicitly. Against Shneiderman's standard, a delegated run is neither incremental nor immediately visible; the preview restores visibility of impact before the operation, and is the agent-era answer to the 1997 debate. Terry & Mynatt's chained previews are the ancestor of a step list with a previewed effect per step.

### 10. Plans as resources, and plans made legible

- *Plans and Situated Actions* (Suchman — Xerox PARC ISL-6 1985; Cambridge UP 1987; 2nd ed. 2007). Full text of the 1985 report. Plans "are resources for people's practical deliberations about action. As projective and retrospective accounts of action, plans are themselves located in the larger context of some practical activity"; "plans are derivative from actions in situ"; "as common sense formulations designed to accommodate the unforeseeable contingencies of situated action, plans are inherently vague."
- *Legibility and Predictability of Robot Motion* (Dragan, Lee, Srinivasa — HRI 2013). Full text. "A collaborative robot's motion must be legible, or intent-expressive"; "predictability and legibility are fundamentally different and often contradictory properties".
- *Plan Explicability and Predictability for Robot Task Planning* (Zhang, Sreedharan, Kulkarni, Chakraborti, Zhuo, Kambhampati — ICRA 2017). Full text. "Explicability is concerned with the association between human-interpreted tasks and agent actions, while predictability is concerned with the connections between these abstract tasks."
- *Plan Explanations as Model Reconciliation* (Chakraborti, Sreedharan, Zhang, Kambhampati — IJCAI 2017). Full text. "When the robot's plan is different from what the human would expect given his model of the world, the robot will be called on to 'explain' its plan"; explanation "aims to make minimal changes to the human's model".
- Implication: Suchman is the theoretical form of cluster 3 — a plan is a resource for deliberation and repair, not a script, and holding execution "to the plan" has to leave room for situated deviation *and make it visible*. The planning literature gives the page its vocabulary for a reviewable plan: steps that map to tasks the actor recognises (explicable), each following from the last (predictable), with explanation attached where the plan departs from what the actor would expect.

### 11. Reviewing a synthesised procedure before it runs over everything

- *User Interaction Models for Disambiguation in Programming by Example* (Mayer, Soares, Grechkin, Le, Marron, Polozov, Singh, Zorn, Gulwani — UIST 2015). Full text. "A key impedance in adoption of PBE systems is the lack of user confidence in the correctness of the program that was synthesized"; two models: "Program Navigation … programs are paraphrased in English for easy readability" and "Conversational Clarification, wherein the system asks questions to the user to resolve ambiguities … with respect to the available test data"; each "significantly reduces the number of errors in the performed task without any difference in completion time."
- *Wrangler* (Kandel, Paepcke, Hellerstein, Heer — CHI 2011). Full text. "To reduce this gulf of evaluation, Wrangler provides natural language descriptions and visual transform previews"; "Wrangler displays these previews in the source data, and not as a separate visualization"; "the user clicks the green add button to execute the transform."
- Implication: this is the evidence base for the seed's "sample of the output before the full run" form, which the agent-era studies do not cover. Two shapes: read the procedure paraphrased, or answer targeted questions on the cases where candidate procedures diverge. Both reduce errors at no time cost — the one place in this run where a preview is shown to be free.

### 12. Approval screens that nobody reads

- *Android Permissions: User Attention, Comprehension, and Behavior* (Felt, Ha, Egelman, Haney, Chin, Wagner — SOUPS 2012). Full text. "17% of participants paid attention to permissions during a given installation"; "only 3% of Internet survey respondents could correctly answer three comprehension questions."
- *Alice in Warningland* (Akhawe, Felt — USENIX Security 2013). Full text. 25 million warning impressions; "users click through more-frequent errors more quickly"; 70.2% click-through on Chrome's SSL warnings against a tenth to a third elsewhere, so "the user experience of a warning can have a significant" effect.
- *How Polymorphic Warnings Reduce Habituation in the Brain* (Anderson, Kirwan, Jenkins, Eargle, Howard, Vance — CHI 2015). Abstract only. "A dramatic drop in the visual processing centers of the brain after only the second exposure to a warning"; a warning that "changes its appearance" resists it.
- *Trained to Accept?* (Böhme, Köpsell — CHI 2010). Located, not extractable; do not quote until verified.
- Implication: the base rate for a pre-action screen is that most actors neither look at it nor understand it, habituation starts on the second exposure, and frequency drives click-through speed. A plan preview that is always the same shape, fires for trivial work, or lists capabilities rather than consequences inherits all of this. Wang et al.'s "OK-button machines" is the same finding on agent prompts.

## What this challenges

- *"Misreadings surface while they are still cheap."* Only when the actor holds knowledge the agent lacked. Against a plausible plan for an unfamiliar task, actors accept the misreading (Ghosh; He et al.), and giving them edit rights can make the plan worse. The resulting clause should say what the preview is for: getting the actor's context into the run before it starts, not catching the agent out.
- *The ritual force is framed as a frequency problem.* Frequency matters for per-action prompts (Wang; Li), but for plans the literature puts the cause in plausibility, length, and session fatigue. The pattern's forces should carry plan granularity as the lever, and the page should set up cognitive forcing functions the way citation and transparent reasoning do — a plan is an artefact that invites reliance.
- *"The preview only means something while the system is held to what it showed."* Half right. Cocoa and Magentic-UI argue that a plan which never changes after partial execution is the wrong design; what has to hold is that departures are shown as departures. The page should distinguish visible re-planning from silent deviation, and say that enforcement of the hold — for side effects — cannot be left to the model (Khan).
- *The seed treats plan preview as a delegation set-up touchpoint.* Chen et al. make it a *delegation structure*: choosing plan-level over action-level oversight changes what the actor is exposed to throughout the run, not only at the start. The `serves: delegation` note can say so.
- *The seed's disclosure says the pattern is attested only in AI catalogues.* It has a thirty-year lineage under other names: management by consent (Billings 1991), level 4–5 automation (Sheridan), feedforward (Djajadiningrat 2002; Vermeulen 2013), previewed transforms (Wrangler 2011), program navigation (Mayer 2015). The `evidence` field can carry `literature`, and the lead can stop presenting the move as new.
- *The seed's forms (step list, output sample, scope statement, dry run) are listed flat.* The evidence sorts them: step lists are the form studied; scope and effect statements are what the drift incidents and the security papers ask for; output samples have no agent-era study behind them and rest on the programming-by-example and data-wrangling lineage in the Addendum.
