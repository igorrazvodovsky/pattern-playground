## Convergent findings

### C1. The workflow artifact is bidirectional — authored to delegate, read to supervise

ReUseIt's synthesised workflows "incorporate execution guards … while keeping users informed of progress and issues"; PromptChainer's chains are valued because they are "perceived to be more transparent and controllable" than an opaque single run; AIPOM and XAgen exist purely so users can "inspect, understand, and control" workflows that already run. The build surface and the monitoring surface converge on one representation. Building vs display (Q2) is not two patterns — it is one artifact's two faces, and systems increasingly ship both on the same graph view. Orchestration (Q3) does not break this: AIPOM and XAgen apply the identical inspect-refine contract to multi-agent plans, which reads as the same move at a higher composition, not a new move.

Papers: ReUseIt, PromptChainer, AIPOM, XAgen (near-miss corroboration: Graph of Trace, OrchVis).

*Draft project takeaway*: a workflow representation earns its keep twice — authored to delegate, read to supervise; the pattern should be one entry with two faces, not builder/display twins.

### C2. Authorship is inverting: system-proposed, actor-repaired

InstructPipe's premise is that "existing systems typically require users to build pipelines from scratch" and that the fix is an LLM drafting the pipeline "for further human-AI collaboration"; ReUseIt synthesises the workflow *from the agent's own attempts* with no authoring step at all; AwareAuto (near-miss) generates multi-branch automations from natural expression. The blank canvas is a dying assumption. This is the strongest correction to the strawman: "the actor externalises a procedure" is only half the pattern — increasingly the *system* externalises (from instructions, demonstrations, or its own runs) and the actor's move is *review and repair*. The externalisation itself is executor-agnostic; who drafts first is a dimension, not a definition.

Papers: InstructPipe, ReUseIt (near-miss: AwareAuto).

*Draft project takeaway*: name inspection-and-repair, not construction, as the pattern's core interaction — the draft may come from either side of the delegation boundary.

### C3. The repair loop is the hard part; it needs effect-preview and step-level attribution

ModSandbox's moderators could not "estimate the actual effect of a rule in advance" nor figure out "how the rules should be updated"; PromptChainer found chain authoring "requires … debugging the chain at multiple granularities"; XAgen's practitioners needed to "attribute [failures] to specific agents or steps". Authoring interfaces systematically underweight the after-deployment life of the procedure. The two load-bearing affordances are *rehearsal against real data before commitment* (ModSandbox's sandbox — evidence for delegation's *test drive* touchpoint) and *failure localisation to a step* (XAgen, PromptChainer).

Papers: ModSandbox, PromptChainer, XAgen.

*Draft project takeaway*: what makes automation hard is not writing the rule but foreseeing its effect and locating its failures — effect-preview and per-step attribution are the affordances the entry must name.

### C4. The instruction genre is executor-agnostic

Natural Notation's reminders "might be performed either by an automated agent or by the author" — the same sticky-note genre, addressed to self, another person, or a machine, with only linguistic register shifting. ReUseIt closes the loop from the other end: a workflow induced from *agent* behaviour is read and reused by *humans*. The near-miss occupations study (How Do AI Agents Do Human Work?) induces one workflow representation from either species of worker. Q1 resolves: user workflow vs agent workflow is not a pattern boundary; the executor is a dimension of one move. What varies by executor is trust apparatus (guards, monitoring), not the representational move.

Papers: Natural Notation, ReUseIt (near-miss: How Do AI Agents Do Human Work?).

*Draft project takeaway*: instructing a system is continuous with leaving a note for yourself or a colleague — one move, executor as a dimension; don't fork user-workflow and agent-workflow entries.

### C5. Workflows are grown, not specified

Situated Live Programming's users "iteratively create, edit, and refine a reactive robot program *while executing partial programs*", authoring in the live task space rather than an abstract canvas; Natural Notation's authors drift spontaneously into "graphical resources as an informal visual language"; AgentSPEX (near-miss) positions an explicit workflow language precisely between implicit reactive prompting and code-coupled orchestration frameworks. Formality is entered gradually and situationally — the entry should hook into the formality spectrum and incremental formalisation rather than assume upfront specification.

Papers: Situated Live Programming, Natural Notation (near-miss: AgentSPEX).

*Draft project takeaway*: the workflow representation is grown while partially running, sliding along the formality spectrum — link the entry to incremental formalisation and formality, and treat upfront full specification as the degenerate case.

## Addendum (same day): organisational process modelling — the Q5 gap, elaborated and read

Prompted by a scoping question from practice: does the pattern cover modelling an *organisational* process — e.g. a sales process with states, rules, transitions in a CRM? The morning run could not answer (Q5 drew nothing). This addendum records the gap analysis and a targeted read of the CSCW canon the arxiv pipeline structurally cannot reach.

> *Provenance (addendum)*. Direct reads, not API retrieval. Dourish (2001) read in full (open PDF, author's site). Suchman (1994), Bowers/Button/Sharrock (1995), and Schmidt (1997/1999) grounded via publisher/web abstracts — and BBS additionally via Dourish's extended in-paper account of the same fieldwork. Winograd's (1994) response located but its PDF resisted extraction — cited from secondary grounding only, no quotes. van der Aalst's BPM corpus remains unread; named as the discipline-side boundary, not synthesised.

### The gap, stated

The morning corpus assumes throughout that the workflow's author is its owner-beneficiary and that the *system executes* the procedure. The organisational case breaks three of those assumptions: the author models a procedure *other actors* work inside (author ≠ subject); the system *enforces and records* rather than executes (humans do the selling); and the artifact holds a second job as measurement/reporting infrastructure. The canon below speaks to exactly these three.

### The canon

- *Process Descriptions as Organisational Accounting Devices: The Dual Use of Workflow Technologies* — Dourish, GROUP 2001. Full read. [dourish.com/publications/2001/group01-orgaccounts.pdf](https://www.dourish.com/publications/2001/group01-orgaccounts.pdf)
- *Workflow From Within and Without* — Bowers, Button & Sharrock, ECSCW 1995. The Establishment Printers (PF2) fieldwork: a shopfloor workflow system that disrupted the workers' own flow-management yet persisted because its use was *contractually mandated* — the client required PF2's monthly reports. Workers ran workarounds (fictitious worker IDs, paper records) and someone retroactively cleaned the data so the report stayed readable.
- *Do Categories Have Politics?* — Suchman, CSCW journal 2(3), 1994. Speech-act-encoded categories in The Coordinator "carr[y] with [them] an agenda of discipline and control over organization members' actions." Winograd (1994) replies in the same volume; Suchman (1995) responds, clarifying she does not demonise management.
- *Of Maps and Scripts* — Schmidt, GROUP 1997 / IST 1999. The corrective to the pure situated-action line: the empirical evidence "is not as strong as we may have believed"; formal constructs serve "not only as 'maps' but also as 'scripts'" — their status is differentiated, not uniformly resource-not-prescription.

### Convergent findings (continued)

#### C6. A process representation is dual-use: coordination technology *and* organisational accounting device

Dourish's answer to why workflow systems succeed commercially despite damaging the work they coordinate: they do two jobs at once — "it acts – at one and the same time – both as a *coordination technology* and as an *organisational accounting device*." PF2 failed at the first and excelled at the second (the client's contractual window onto the print shop), and that was enough to keep it deployed. Crucially, "no single account of working action can adequately serve both purposes" — the tension is constitutive, and the mismatch between representation and lived work "should not be eliminated, but creatively colonised."

Papers: Dourish 2001, Bowers/Button/Sharrock 1995.

*Draft project takeaway*: a procedure representation always has an audience beyond its executors — it renders work accountable, and who the account is *for* is a force the entry must name; a sales pipeline's stages exist as much for the forecast reader as for the seller.

#### C7. Enforce vs reveal — and the 2001 proposal the 2025 agent tools built

Dourish's "reimagining": treat workflow as a *visualisation* technology — "the role of the system is to *find and present* the order in the work, rather than, as traditionally conceived, to *prespecify and enforce* it" — with multiple concurrent process descriptions over the same activity, and process descriptions as organising *resources* (Grinter's software engineers reading config-management state as a view onto group activity). This is precisely what the morning corpus's agent-trace tools do a quarter-century later: ReUseIt induces the workflow from the agent's runs; Graph of Trace renders execution as an emergent directed graph. The cross-era convergence is the strongest single argument that one representational pattern spans the organisational and the agentic cases.

Papers: Dourish 2001 ↔ ReUseIt, Graph of Trace, XAgen (morning run).

*Draft project takeaway*: the entry's deepest axis is prescribe-then-enforce vs observe-then-reveal — the same graph artifact serves both ends, and the agentic tools rediscovered the CSCW resolution.

#### C8. Formal constructs are maps *and* scripts — resist the pure situated-action reading

Schmidt's corrective bounds C7 from the other side: the ethnographic evidence for "plans are mere resources" is weaker than received wisdom held, and formal constructs demonstrably also script behaviour, legitimately. For the pattern this means neither pole is the honest default: a deployment pipeline genuinely scripts; a sales methodology mostly maps; the entry should carry the differentiation rather than a doctrine.

Papers: Schmidt 1997/1999.

*Draft project takeaway*: whether the representation is a map (resource for situated action) or a script (prescription) is a property of the deployment, not the artifact — the entry should name the axis and refuse a default.

#### C9. Categories discipline: author ≠ subject is a named political force

Suchman: encoding members' actions into system categories "carries with it an agenda of discipline and control"; Dourish sharpens it — a process representation is a "cleaned up" view, so "the question must be asked, to whom are these details irrelevant? Who defines the essence?" In the EP case the enshrined view belonged to management and the client, not the shopfloor. Star's boundary-object lens names the failure mode: the process description is *meant* to serve all stakeholders but "fall[s] down in [its] plasticity." This force simply does not exist in the morning corpus's self-owned automations — it activates when mediation leaves the individual.

Papers: Suchman 1994 (Winograd 1994 response noted, unread in full), Dourish 2001, Bowers/Button/Sharrock 1995.

*Draft project takeaway*: when the workflow's author and its subjects diverge, the representation becomes an instrument of ordering — a force that switches on at coordination/networking mediation and that the delegated-agent case never surfaces.

### Scope verdict this supports

One entry remains defensible, with the addendum's forces named as conditional: the representational move (a procedure made explicit, inspectable, editable) spans self-notes, agent delegation, and organisational modelling — Dourish's own definition ("a *process representation* that drives the functioning of the system") covers the CRM case without strain, and C7's cross-era convergence argues the move is one thing. What the organisational case adds is not a different move but three forces that switch on with mediation: the accounting audience (C6), the map/script status question (C8), and author≠subject discipline (C9). The project's scope boundary lands cleanly: the *modelling and inspection interfaces* are in scope (pipeline editors, state/transition rule builders, trace views); BPM as organisational-change methodology is not. The Suchman thread already on wizard.mdx connects here.
