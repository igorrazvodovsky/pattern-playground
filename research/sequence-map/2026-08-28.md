## Context

A design-pattern corpus (human↔AI interaction) is being reorganised as named, ordered
walks ("sequences") over its patterns: each step acts on what previous steps produced;
sequences hand off to one another and converge at shared stations. `precedes` is
make-time-only and acyclic, so precedes-chains are candidate spines. Questions, from
`query.yml`: do sequence-adopting pattern communities confirm that authoring sequences
rewrites patterns, and what representation did they settle on; is a fixed authored
order defensible; what do staging models in HCI/design methods say about ordering
guidance; is the artifact/process register split precedented; what failure modes are
reported.

## Papers

- *Sequences as a basis for pattern language composition* (Porter, Coplien & Winn,
  Science of Computer Programming 56(1), 2005) — full-text read (user-supplied PDF,
  2026-08-28; upgraded from abstract-grounded).
  The founding formalisation on the software side. Definitions: "A sequence is the
  ordering of patterns in a given architecture — or perhaps in a family of
  architectures — that tells the order in which the patterns should be applied. A
  sequence is a sort of architect's tour of the artifact being built" (§4). The
  language and the sequence carry *different registers of order* and must not be
  conflated: "A pattern language should reflect more than an encoding of order; it is
  primarily an encoding of system structure … the time ordering is the domain of
  sequences, not of pattern languages" (§5) — even when a Building Edge exists before
  a Terraced Slope, the language records the structural relation, the sequence the
  temporal one. Each carries information the other loses: "A sequence may contain
  information that is lost in a pattern language: for example, the preferred ordering
  of two patterns at the same level of scale … a pattern language may contain
  information that is lost in a sequence: for example, the relationship between a
  pattern and which larger pattern it refines" (§4). Languages give "only a weak
  notion of the order … neither Alexander's theory nor language diagrams indicate how
  to order adjacent patterns" (§4). The two are mutually derivable with domain
  knowledge: a language "provides the dynamics for generating sequences, just as the
  grammar of a natural language provides the dynamics for generating sentences" (§6.2),
  and from a collection of observed sequences one can reconstruct a rough language,
  the grammar-induction move (§6). Composition of multiple languages = interleave one
  sequence from each, guided by context, then read the combined language off the
  interleaved sequence; formally, languages are posets under an application-order
  relation, composition is pairwise, order-sensitive, valid when both posets are
  preserved without reversals (§7). Cautions: some languages should not be combined
  (composition only makes sense over a shared space, and combining can invalidate the
  original modularisation of forces, §7.2); the "main sequence order" — the order if
  every pattern were applied — is recommended as the best presentation order for a
  language (§4). Ancestor reference: Harrison & Coplien, "Pattern sequences",
  EuroPLoP 2001.
  Speaks to: representation, interconnection, what a sequence adds to the graph.
  Transfer: strong — this is the plan's exact move, one register over (software
  architecture).
- *Pattern languages in HCI: a critical review* (Dearden & Finlay, Human–Computer
  Interaction 21(1), 2006) — full-text read (Sheffield Hallam OA copy).
  On generativity (§5.3): one reading of A Pattern Language is "generating designs by
  implicit sequencing of decisions, derived by traversing the network of links";
  Beck & Cunningham's version is that the language "helps designers to ask and answer
  the right question at the right time". But "generative" also legitimately means
  indirect actionable advice (Gabriel), constraint solving, or generating an option
  space of alternatives — and some languages contain *alternative* patterns for the
  same problem (Tidwell's TILED vs STACK), i.e. choice points, not steps. On
  organisation (§8.2): "Unlike architectural patterns, where 'scale' provides a useful
  organising principle, in HCI the problem is multi-dimensional. Scale is important,
  but designers also address problems in terms of technology, task, information, and
  time." Also: "attention must be paid to the differences between the sequence and
  pacing of activities in architectural production and in interactive systems
  development." On use (§6): as of 2006, no observational studies of designers using
  patterns in practice; the walked-vs-consulted question was empirically open. §6.5
  notes a pattern's context has two parts — its position in the language and the
  environmental pre-conditions for applying it.
  Speaks to: ordering-by-scale, choice points, presuppositions. Transfer: strong — the
  corpus under study is HCI pattern languages.
- *The structure of pattern languages* (Salingaros, Architectural Research Quarterly 4,
  2000) — full-text read.
  Two legitimate orders over one corpus: decreasing size "is the correct ordering when
  one is using them for design, since decisions on the largest scale have to be made
  first", but "in order to validate the above patterns, they have to be read in the
  opposite order: small to large" — the evolving order is how a reader *learns* the
  language. Order is a property of the activity performed on the corpus, not of the
  corpus. The language's structure proper is its connective map, which most users never
  see: "it is difficult to visualize [connections] without a connective map".
  Speaks to: default ordering, per-activity entry. Transfer: partial — architectural
  corpus, but the claim is about pattern languages as such.
- *The Nature of Pattern Languages* (Falkenthal, Breitenbücher & Leymann, PURPLSOC
  2018) — full-text read.
  Formalises a pattern language as a node-coloured, edge-weighted directed multigraph,
  and distinguishes *pattern sequences* (authored navigation structures; "the relations
  between the patterns establish so-called pattern sequences, which comprise the
  generative power of the whole pattern language") from *solution paths* (the patterns
  a user actually selected for a concrete problem, recorded after the fact, including
  which branch was taken at a choice). Sequences and solution paths are both subgraph
  selections over one graph — the derived-projection reading.
  Speaks to: representation (phase 3). Transfer: strong on structure, weak on content
  (IT patterns).
- *Context Encapsulation: Three Stories, a Language, and Some Sequences* (Henney,
  EuroPLoP 2006) — abstract-grounded plus the Falkenthal et al. account.
  Sequence authoring rewrote the corpus in the small: working from recurring problem
  *stories*, one pattern (Kelly's Encapsulate Context) split into a brief pattern
  language, with sequences through it easing application. Stories are the nucleus from
  which sequences are found.
  Speaks to: authoring forces splits; story-first authoring method. Transfer: strong on
  method.
- *Systematic pattern selection using pattern language grammars and design space
  analysis* (Zdun, Software: Practice and Experience 37(9), 2007) — full-text read
  (user-supplied PDF, 2026-08-28; upgraded from abstract-grounded).
  Sequences formalised as a grammar: patterns are terminal symbols, relationships
  production rules, derived sequences the language's words; order is generated per
  problem from an authored grammar rather than stored as walks. Three precisions
  matter beyond the abstract. First, a step is a *decision event*, not a build step:
  "a pattern … can be seen as an event in a creative design process … the sequence
  A, B, C means that pattern A is selected before pattern B" — and selection order is
  not implementation order: patterns in one sequence may be "applied in parallel, or …
  a sequence needs to be applied as a whole" (the client/server request-handler pair
  is selected in some order but implemented together) (§4.1). Second, the grammar is
  curated and living, not exhaustive: it records "only a number of successful solution
  paths that have proven to work in the past" and "should be seen as a living document"
  as combinations are mined and technology changes (§4.1). Third, the relation
  vocabulary: required / optional / variants / alternatives, non-exclusive by default
  with an explicit `(xor)` marker for genuinely exclusive choices; consecutive
  duplicates collapse because steps are selections, not instance counts; every
  dependency is annotated with expected quality-goal effects, and complex choice
  points get a Questions-Options-Criteria design-space analysis (§§4.2, 5). The
  representation is two-layer: a human-readable grammar overview diagram for
  discussing decisions, and the formal grammar for tooling (§4.1). Validation: five
  non-expert students mapping a messy 49-pattern security corpus went from >70% wrong
  pattern summaries to <10% after adopting sequence/grammar diagrams, correctly
  identified eight undocumented relationships, and surfaced real gaps in the pattern
  landscape (§7.5).
  Speaks to: representation (phase 3), choice points, gap-finding. Transfer: strong
  on structure, partial on content.
- *Creating Software Architecture using Pattern Sequences* (Siddle, EuroPLoP 2007) —
  full-text read.
  The practitioner account of a real sequence, with its deviations recorded: some
  patterns were "recognised in the architecture after implementation took place"; two
  were "actually applied concurrently and independently, they are presented in sequence
  here because the extra step introduced by separating the patterns in the sequence is
  a logical one"; a sub-sequence's patterns were applied collectively "but not in a
  discernable order". Claimed benefits: coherence from dependency order, and — for the
  main sequence only — "each pattern application results in a workable architecture"
  (Alexander's each-step-whole property, empirically partial). Reported failure mode:
  patterns drawn from diverse sources had inconsistent contexts, which "is thought to
  have derailed a number of pattern application attempts on the project".
  Speaks to: what a pilot walk will actually look like; failure modes. Transfer:
  strong on process, weak on domain.
- *Generative methods in urban design: a progress assessment* (Mehaffy, Journal of
  Urbanism 1(1), 2008) — located-but-unextractable (T&F 403); abstract-grounded.
  Twenty-year assessment of A New Theory of Urban Design's stepwise generative line:
  the method specifies "less … a final form through schematic planning and more …
  the stepwise process by which a form might emerge from the evolutionary actions of a
  group of collaborators". Cited here as the bridge from pattern language to generative
  codes; see `2026-08-28-sources.md` for the Generative Codes paper itself (full-text
  read).
  Speaks to: how the sequence idea evolved after patterns. Transfer: partial.

Also located, not read: *Pattern Views: Concept and Tooling for Interconnected Pattern
Languages* (Leymann group, arXiv:2003.09127) — tooling for cross-language pattern
views; a phase 3 pointer.

## Convergent findings

### Sequences are how pattern languages became usable for composition — the plan's move is precedented on both flanks
Porter/Coplien/Winn, Henney, Zdun, Siddle, and Falkenthal et al. form a continuous
line (2005–2018) in which the pattern community concluded that the language graph
alone does not compose designs, and authored sequences are the missing instrument.
Alexander's own system precedent is Book 2 ch. 20 — the network of calling sequences,
snippable sizing, and public repository (see the companion sources note; a first pass
missed it and wrongly reported the precedent as software-only). The two flanks are
connected: ch. 20 §9 cites the software-patterns community as the working model of a
shared medium for evolving such material.
Papers: Porter et al., Henney, Zdun, Siddle, Falkenthal et al.

### The settled representation is a projection over the graph, not a new content kind
Where the line formalises, sequences are partial orders / paths / grammar derivations
*over* the language graph — authored selections, with the graph remaining the single
source of structure. Falkenthal et al. additionally separate authored sequences from
after-the-fact solution paths. This weights the plan's phase 3 toward the
derived-projection option (authored order seeding a projection), which the plan
already leans to.
Papers: Porter et al., Zdun, Falkenthal et al. Disagreement: Zdun generates order per
problem from a grammar instead of storing walks — the run-time horn, formalised.

### A single fixed order is repeatedly rejected; order is per-activity and per-problem
Salingaros: design walks large-to-small, learning walks small-to-large — same corpus,
two orders. Dearden & Finlay: scale alone under-determines order in HCI because the
space is multi-dimensional (technology, task, information, time), and HCI's sequence
and pacing differ from architecture's. Zdun: order is derived per problem. This
converges with the primary-source finding (companion note) that Alexander himself
holds fixed-artifact and derived-live positions simultaneously.
Papers: Salingaros, Dearden & Finlay, Zdun.

### An authored sequence orders decisions, not construction — and honest ones say so
Zdun makes it formal: a sequence step is a selection event, and patterns selected in
order may be implemented in parallel or as a block. Siddle shows the same from
practice (retrospective recognition, concurrent application presented serially,
unordered collective sub-steps): the authored walk is a rationalised serialisation of
messier work — valuable *because* it is the teachable order of decisions, so long as
it does not claim to be the build's history. Henney's story-first method reaches the
same place from authoring: the sequence serialises a story, not a log. Alexander's
make-time framing ("which other centers need to be in position before that center can
be formed") sits closest to the decision reading.
Papers: Zdun, Siddle, Henney.

### Choice points are native to pattern languages
Alternative patterns for the same problem (Dearden & Finlay §6.5), user-taken branches
recorded in solution paths (Falkenthal et al.), grammar alternatives with an explicit
exclusive/non-exclusive distinction plus per-decision Questions-Options-Criteria
analysis (Zdun). The plan's choice-point steps (navigation model) are precedented; no
sequence tradition forces choices to resolve outside the sequence. Zdun's default is
worth noting: alternatives are non-exclusive unless marked `(xor)` — most pattern
choices combine.
Papers: Dearden & Finlay, Falkenthal et al., Zdun.

### A sequence carries order the graph cannot — the two registers stay separate
Porter et al. answer the plan's open question about what a sequence contributes:
the preferred ordering of same-scale patterns under one parent, which no refinement
structure encodes — while the graph keeps what a sequence loses (which larger pattern
a step refines). Their insistence that temporal order is "the domain of sequences,
not of pattern languages" is independent support for this corpus's register
separation (make-time `precedes` distinct from the structural edges), and their
grammar-induction claim (a rough language is recoverable from a collection of
sequences, and vice versa, with domain knowledge) is the formal basis for the
derived-projection reading of phase 3. Zdun's two-layer representation (readable
overview diagram + formal grammar) and his non-expert experiment — error rates
collapsing and corpus gaps surfacing once sequences were drawn — say the projection
is also the corpus's legibility instrument.
Papers: Porter et al., Zdun, Falkenthal et al.

## What this challenges

- *The scale-based default order.* Dearden & Finlay state directly that scale is not a
  sufficient organising principle in HCI, and Salingaros shows order is per-activity
  even in architecture. The plan's default order (structuring space → … → finishing
  the whole) should be presented as one authored reading order among several the
  system supports — which its own per-product-entry paragraph already half-concedes —
  not as the system's order.
- *"Sequence" as a new content kind.* The formal line consistently lands on sequences
  as authored projections over the graph; nothing in the literature supports a
  free-standing sequence artifact detached from the edge structure. Phase 3's
  derived-projection lean is corroborated; the new-content-kind option is not.
- *Steps as patterns only.* Henney's sequences serialise stories; Siddle's serialise
  design acts; Alexander's steps are mostly acts (companion note). The strict
  patterns-as-steps rule has weaker support than the plan assumes; expect pilot steps
  to be moves that *deposit* patterns.
- *The pilot's success criterion.* Siddle's evidence says a clean end-to-end walk is
  not what happens: record where the walk had to rationalise (concurrency, unordered
  clusters, retrospective steps) as findings, not as authoring failures. The
  each-step-whole property held for only one of his two sequences — worth testing
  per-sequence here too, alongside the read-aloud test.
- *Cross-source context inconsistency* is the one empirically reported way sequence
  application derails (Siddle). The corpus is single-author, which mitigates it, but
  sequences that mix patterns from different territories (the AI cluster crossing into
  spatial patterns) should check that initiating situations actually chain.
