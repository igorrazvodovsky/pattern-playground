# Review: the first thirteen IxD sequences against Alexander's

Read: all thirteen files in `apps/patterns/src/content/sequences/`, `docs/specs/sequences.md`, the sequence-map plan, the read-aloud and test-walk sections of three sitting plans, the diffs to `content.config.ts`, the validator, the walk renderer, nav and stack store; on the Alexander side, the patternlanguage.com sequences page and its office-layout sequence, and from the vault the frontmatter of all ~100 `type: sequence` notes plus nine in full (Herman Miller, kitchen, tea house, Guasare litany, fundamental process, garden-before-house, Mexicali, winnowing, four-colour game), the `generative sequence` and `backtrack-free sequence` concept notes, and *Ordering the pattern language*. The build could not be run on the local machine's VM (platform-specific native bindings), so the code is reviewed by reading.

## Numbers first

Thirteen sequences, 49 sub-sequences, 132 numbered positions, 163 leaf steps, 75 connections, 107 distinct patterns invoked (of 141 pages). Half the steps carry a qualifier: 22 optional, 18 weak (no rule), 20 aspect re-invocations, 12 clusters, 8 choice points. Glosses average 59 words a step, 9,700 words in all. The Herman Miller office sequence, Alexander's longest published one, runs 26 steps at ten words each. All 75 connections are authored as `to`; none as `from`.

## What Alexander's sequences are, structurally

Reading the published ones side by side (kitchen, office, Mexicali, tea house, Guasare, Pasadena), five things hold across all of them. A step places a centre: a noun in the world, a table, a window place, an entrance transition, a lot line. The list is flat and numbered continuously, nine to twenty-five steps, and the only escape hatch is global (Herman Miller step 0: drop any later step that fights the centres you already have). Each sequence has a signature surprise, which is where its content is: the counter eighth, the tea house at step 15, the lot lines last, the garden before the house. The first step is nearly always a perceiving or inventory step, inside the sequence, not before it: "formulate the activities as generic centres", "feel the natural centres", "rank your fourteen work activities". And a sequence is handed to strangers with a model and a page of successful examples; its mode, in your base's vocabulary, is *published*.

## Where the IxD sequences match, and where they depart

The theoretical frame is faithful and in places sharper than the source. The vault's argument that backtracking is the one criterion that needs no eye, and that morphological impact cannot transfer to a medium whose wholeness is not perceivable, is the right pick, and the spec encodes it. The debt-and-collection device (footing owes an exit, the grant owes a return, arrival owes the clearing of scaffold) is a real invention: Alexander never needs it because his whole is on the table at every step, and yours is not, so the walk keeps a ledger instead. That is an honest adaptation, and it is also the clearest sign of the underlying difference: his steps differentiate a whole, yours accumulate obligations.

Four departures are worth deciding on rather than letting stand.

**The head is cut off.** The spec bans process-register steps and pushes them into `presupposes`. Alexander does the opposite: the perceiving step is *the* step, and the kitchen sequence you cite as the paradigm opens with a translation of the household's life into generic centres before anything is placed. Thirteen sequences now open with a paragraph saying, in effect, assume someone else did the hard part; seven of them add that nothing in the corpus discharges it. The reasoning (the perceivability precondition, the paired process project) is defensible, but the result is that every sequence begins one step after where Alexander's begins, and the reader arrives with no whole in mind. Question: is a one-step inventory head (in Alexander's mode, not the process project's) really process-register, or is it the step that gives the rest something to act on?

**The mode is forward, and the site publishes it as published.** Your base separates *forward* (designed ahead), *retrospective*, *traditional* and *published* (handed to strangers). Every IxD sequence is forward mode: two thought-walks by the author who wrote the order, on the same two imagined products (an issue tracker, a research assistant) reused across all thirteen sittings, each walk finding exactly one backtrack, which then got fixed. The tea-house note in your own vault faults Alexander for never running the scrambled-order control; that fault applies here twice over, since nobody has yet run any of these on a product they did not also author. This is not a complaint about the sittings, which were authoring, and good authoring. It is a labelling point: the sequence pages carry no mode, no walk count, no example, while Alexander's every published sequence carries a "Sequence in Action" and a page of results. The two test walks already exist in the plan files; the cheapest fix is to render them.

**Lifecycle order is re-entering as region order.** *Ordering the pattern language* diagnosed use-time order wearing a make-time label at the edge level, and the register sweep pulled it out. At the sub-sequence level it is back: governing autonomy runs grant, gate, transfer, account; delegating work runs framing, watch, receive, iterate; memory runs keeping, return, forgetting. Those are the actor's chronology. The encounter's plan admits "design order and run order coincide here", and the vocabulary doc licenses that for protocol families. Whether it holds for the others is the open question; the honest test is whether any region order would reverse under the backtrack criterion (must the correction move be decided before the watching surface?), and if not, the region order is a narrative, not a claim, and should say so. The within-region orders are where the real make-time claims live, and several are Alexander-grade with reasons attached: explanation deliberately after the evidence marks "a convincing account would talk the actor out of opening"; tag before attribute-visibility; suggestion (the contract) before ai-completion (its inline form); the working/showing declaration pulled into the grant. Those are your counters-eighth. There are perhaps eight of them across 163 steps, and nothing on the page marks them.

**The line has become an annotated graph.** Half the steps carry a qualifier, 41 of 49 sub-sequences have three or fewer positions (the spec's own bar is five to twenty), and the numbering restarts per region, so no reader can say "undo comes at step 13 of 16". Alexander's sequence is a line with one escape hatch; yours is a partial order with a numbering, which the spec says plainly and honestly. The cost is that the artefact converges on what the `precedes` graph already was. The value a sequence adds over the graph is exactly the strict A-before-B claims between same-scale steps that no edge carries. It would be worth counting those per sequence, and the count may be smaller than the apparatus suggests. Attention and tempo has six weak steps in twelve: on doctrine (ch. 11 §11, building a sequence forces new patterns), but the page shows a walk that is half unbacked without saying so.

Two smaller things. The read-aloud paragraph in each plan file is the closest thing in the repository to an Alexander sequence: things forming, in order, in prose. The spec currently says the body lead stops before the regions, so that paragraph lives in a plan file nobody visits. Consider making it the body. And Alexander numbers continuously across sections (Herman Miller 0–25 over four headings), which is what makes "eighth" sayable; a global count alongside the per-region number costs nothing.

## Code

The schema, validator and renderer are clean and match the spec. Three findings.

Incoming connections are not rendered. All 75 connections are outgoing, `SequenceWalk.astro` reads only its own file's `connections`, and nothing computes the reverse. So calibrating reliance, which the map calls "a called sequence over every AI surface" and which eleven connections point at, shows four outgoing links and none of the eleven arrivals; acting on material likewise hides eleven. The spec's "each kind reads from either end" is true of the labels and false of the page. The fix mirrors `appearancesBySlug`: index `to` across the collection at build time and render the incoming set on the target page. This is the one change I would make before anything else, because it is the interconnection inventory made visible.

Deep far addresses are not validated. `checkSequenceRefs` exempts `to`/`from` wholesale, which is right for unauthored targets, but thirteen connections address a sub-sequence or step in a sequence that does exist (`acting-on-material/safety/3`, `the-encounter/trouble/2`, `composing-views/splitting/4` and so on). All thirteen resolve today; a renumbering in the target will silently break them. Exempt only the heads that resolve to no file.

Lint and build did not run on the local VM (esbuild and rolldown want macOS bindings), so I have not confirmed the tree builds; the validator logic reads correctly, and a `content.indexOf(slug)` line-number heuristic is the only sloppiness I saw (it reports the first occurrence, which may be a different field).

## The one question

Alexander's sequence is a thing a stranger runs on a model and gets a unique result from. Yours, as they stand, are things the author reads to check the corpus's order, and they have done that job well: thirteen sittings, over a hundred recorded corpus changes, a system map. Whether they are also meant to be run, and by whom, decides most of the above: the head step, the mode label, the gloss length, and whether the walks get published. If the answer is that they are the corpus's own ordering instrument and not a hand-to-strangers artefact, then the site should say so and the apparatus is about right. If they are meant to be run, the Herman Miller page is the target shape and these are roughly five times too long per step and one step short at the front.
