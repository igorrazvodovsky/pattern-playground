## The transformation under the step (ch. 2)

A sequence step is, underneath, a structure-preserving transformation, and
ch. 2 is where that concept gets its content:

- *The next step is already hinted at.* Each transformation intensifies a
  center that is latent in what exists — the "faint fuzz" at the middle of the
  empty circle, there "for describable mathematical reasons" (§1). This is why
  a step is not arbitrary: "Relatively few acts intensify the structure which
  exists, while there are thousands of random acts which would not" (§1A).
- *Conservative and innovative at once* (§1E): "nothing entirely new has been
  injected — the newness has been created by intensification of what exists."
- *One bad step is nearly unrecoverable* (§4). A run of structure-preserving
  transformations is "almost always beautiful"; a single structure-destroying
  one in the middle of good ones makes things "ugly very quickly," and "the
  effect of this one bad transformation is very hard to recover from." This is
  ch. 11 §7's backtrack criterion seen from the other side: backtracking is
  expensive because a committed step's product soaks into everything after it.
- *Minimum new structure* (§10): at each step, "introduce the minimum new
  structure which is absolutely necessary, nothing more."
- *The fifteen properties double as the transformations* (§8): each property is
  both a characteristic of living structure and a transformation that injects
  it. This is Alexander's move palette under his steps. This corpus does not
  import the fifteen as its move set — its generic rules are patterns, and the
  properties' translation to the interaction medium is its own track
  (`docs/language/properties/`).

## What a step is (ch. 7)

Ch. 7 defines living process ("any adaptive process which generates living
structure, step by step, through structure-preserving transformations," §1) and
gives the cycle every step is an instance of — the fundamental differentiating
process (§7): attend to the wholeness as it is; find where it is weakest; find
the latent centers; choose one; apply a transformation; check that life
actually increased; check it was the simplest differentiation that could have
done it; repeat, and stop "when no further step can be taken that intensifies
the life of the whole."

Read against that cycle, an authored sequence step is a packaged cycle. For a
recurring type of work, the sequence answers "which latent center next" in
advance — that is the knowledge a sequence stores. The step keeps the rest of
the cycle live: the latitude of how, the per-step check, the stop rule. A step
that also fixed the *how* would be storing what must stay responsive.

Three more holdings:

- *Three shapes of living process* (§8): a single application of the cycle; "a
  string of steps … strung together one after the other"; and "a nested
  hierarchy of steps … carried out not in a single precise order, but in a
  general broad order, each one then calling upon further steps, until the
  whole is finished." The corpus's architecture — sequences of sub-sequences,
  approximate order, typed calls — is the third shape, stated here before
  ch. 20 builds the society-scale network out of it.
- *Individual inside accretive* (§2): every act of making is both a locally
  complete process and a contribution to a larger accretion it does not
  control. A product team adopting one sub-sequence is an individual process
  inside that product's accretion; the sequence should read correctly from
  both directions, which is what a sub-sequence's own initiating situation
  provides.
- *Content, not vocabulary* (§9): a process is living "in content, not
  necessarily in outward verbal form"; the garden advice "do one small good
  thing; then do another" is a living process with no theory words in it. This
  licenses the corpus's plain practitioner voice: fidelity to the method is
  carried by what the steps do, and Alexander's vocabulary does not need to
  appear in a sequence page at all.

## Step-by-step adaptation is the ground the sequence stands on (ch. 8)

Ch. 8's point for sequence authoring is structural: sequence is feature 4 of
living process, and it sits *inside* feature 1, step-by-step adaptation. The order of
steps is only half of the method. The other half is per-step feedback: each
step must be "checked at once for the increase of life which will occur,
accepted if it has it, rejected if it does not" (§5). A sequence followed
without per-step testing is not a living process, however good its order.

- *The Ashby argument* (§4): thirty coins set one at a time take a minute;
  spun all at once until all show heads takes centuries. Adaptation is
  possible only one decision at a time — the reason a walk of small decisions
  beats a scheme, and why each step's decision content must be separable
  (ch. 9 §6 makes separability explicit).
- *The open end-state* (§6): "adaptation itself means nothing if changes
  cannot be made in response to the process of adaptation. By definition, such
  changes cannot be foreseen." A sequence fixes the order of decisions and
  must leave the result unpredictable; ch. 11 §9's "fixed sequence, infinite
  variety" is this requirement met, not a paradox.
- *Feedback needs a medium* (§5). Drawings do not give feedback about the real
  thing ("a polite fiction," §8); Alexander's answer is cheap rough models and
  the real site. In this corpus's terms the medium is the design material the
  adopting team tests in — prototypes, the running product. That is a
  process-register condition, which is why it lands at sequence heads as
  presupposition-shaped, not as a step.

## The whole that governs a step (ch. 9)

- *Latent centers reconcile preserving with enhancing* (§4). The whole that
  governs a step is not an end-state vision: St Mark's Square took 900 years
  of four-move cycles (configuration → latent centers → chosen action →
  construction) with no possible picture of the final square. "The whole, at
  each moment, is discerned by paying attention to the whole configuration as
  it is together with the centers latent in that wholeness."
- *The notation problem* (§5): a sketch is "invariably over-specific" — if 20%
  of it is decided, the other 80% is arbitrary information the notation forced
  in. Alexander's answer is the word picture: a vision "defined only in those
  aspects you want to define, and undefined in others." A sequence's readable
  body is exactly this instrument, and the formal layer's discipline — clusters
  that decline a false ordering, the marked weak form, glosses that state the
  decision rather than the outcome — is the same rule: record only what is
  actually decided.
- *Decide only what you know* (§6): small steps in decision content ("limited
  to a particular subject … floating, to an extent, by itself"), large in
  scale early. And the scarcity argument: of a hundred candidate next steps,
  "there may easily be as many as 90 or 95 … which will make the thing worse."
  First ideas are probably among the bad ones. This scarcity is why a found
  ordering is worth publishing at all — and why ch. 11 §7 says winnowing one
  is "very hard work."

## What a step deposits (ch. 10)

- *Centers are labile, not building blocks* (§1): "they are not things, but
  regions … which as they change, as they are improved, are ideally suited to
  enhance and enlarge and extend the whole." The igloo snow block (§2) starts
  rectangular and is pushed, trimmed, and squashed as the vault rises: the
  deposit of an early step is *reshaped* by later steps.
- This grounds a distinction the backtrack criterion needs: *deformation is
  not backtracking*. A sequence fails when a later step forces an earlier
  *decision* to be revoked (ch. 11 §7); it is healthy — expected — for a later
  step to reshape an earlier step's *product* while the decision stands. The
  authored sequences already practise this (a rule re-invoked at a later site
  through `aspect`; acting-on-material's reverse path revising the grading "once,
  not walked again") and the distinction deserves stating so revision is not
  misread as a failed order.
- *The four defects* (§6, the Berlin library) are the failure register for
  invoking a pattern as a step: the centers made have no smaller centers; they
  are image-like copies "cut and pasted" rather than grown; they "do not
  emerge from the surrounding wholeness"; they "do not help form any larger
  centers." Transferred: a step that merely names a pattern invites cut-and-
  paste application of it. The gloss's job is the binding — saying what, in
  the walk's accumulated product, this invocation acts on and what larger
  thing it serves.
- *A necessary intrusion is placed where it heals* (§3, the Alpine power
  station): even a step the situation forces can be phrased so its placement
  repairs the most damaged part of the whole.
- *A short sequence of large-impact steps forms the whole* (§9, Julian Street
  Inn): "the pattern of the building as a whole was formed by a fairly short
  sequence of steps," each taken correctly and then "taking the consequences
  morphologically." The corpus's five-to-twenty-step sub-sequences are the
  right size for the same reason ch. 20 wants them snippable.

## Patterns seen from the process side (ch. 13, full read)

The earlier sources note settled the pattern↔sequence relationship from
§§2–4 and 11. The full chapter adds:

- *Pattern-finding is the fundamental process applied to functions* (§9, the
  farmhouse kitchen). Defining a new generic center is not "a redefinition of
  function" but "rearranging the functions, redefining the nature and meaning
  of the way the thing works." The centers in people's minds (living room,
  dining room) differed from the centers in their actions; the new pattern
  resolved the conflict. This corroborates ch. 11 §11 from the pattern side:
  ordering work and pattern work are the same work at two grains — which the
  authoring sittings have observed empirically, seven to nine corpus changes
  per sitting.
- *Essential centers, not gimmicks* (§10): "the essential centers are those
  whose presence is already latent in the field — which go to the heart of the
  living structure that is already there." The step-selection test: a step
  earns its place when its decision is already latent in what the previous
  steps made, not when the pattern inventory has an entry to place.
- *The list alone* (§11): a good language "emanates as a whole" — the
  Samarkand poem conveys the place before any design exists, and the list is
  "transformed as a whole" as understanding matures. The test questions given
  there (does it form a coherent whole in the mind? does reading it shed light
  on the project?) apply to the sequence map as the corpus's whole-vision
  instrument.
- *Observation from inside* (§§7–8): the Peru patterns came from living in
  four households, not from ethnographic reports ("none of it seemed even
  remotely useful"); an observer asks "what would I do if I were one of the
  people living here" and cross-checks against the other observers. Practice
  evidence over reports — the corpus's existing stance — with the caveat that
  this grounding work is process-register and enters sequences only as
  presuppositions.

## The missing stratum: form language (ch. 16)

Ch. 16 names a third thing that is neither the pattern language nor the
sequence: the *form language* — "the box of tricks, the elements, rules, ways
of making roofs, edges, windows" (§2), the combinatory schemata a maker
actually builds with. Note 1 records the failure that makes it matter: people
used *A Pattern Language* and produced incoherent buildings, because they
poured the patterns "into the then popular, known receptacle of available
geometric forms." "People simply cannot create buildings without a
form-language … the real stuff, the core geometry of the created form lies at
a level deeper than the level of the pattern language." And §3: traditional
sequences carried all three at once — content, order, *and* form.

This corpus splits Alexander's fused system into three strata with named
seams:

- *Patterns* are the generic rules (ch. 13).
- *Sequences* are the order of invoking them (ch. 11).
- *The component catalogue and the foundations* are the form language — the
  schemata a product team actually builds with. A sequence touches this
  stratum at two seams: constituents riding inside steps, and the
  foundation-substrate rule (foundations are material, never steps).

The warning that comes with the split: a step whose rule has no realised
substrate will be poured into whatever form is lying around, and the walk's
product will not consolidate — the 1970s failure, replayed. `realised_by`
coverage along a sequence's rules is the corpus-shaped check; a watch item,
not tooling.

The fifteen transformations as a form-language alphabet (§§7–8, the Mardin
tower reconstruction) remain the aspirational track already recorded in
`docs/language/vision.md`.

## The ten features as an import ledger

Ch. 7 §13 lists ten features every living process must have, each expanded by
one chapter, claimed necessary and expressly not sufficient. The corpus's
position, feature by feature:

1. *Step-by-step adaptation* (ch. 8) — the ground assumption. The ordering
   half is imported; the feedback half lives only in authoring
   practice (test walks) — see the findings below.
2. *The whole governs* (ch. 9) — carried by initiating situations, resulting
   contexts, and the latent-center reading of what a step acts on.
3. *Always making centers* (ch. 10) — carried by the pattern definition
   (a pattern is a rule for making a center) and the psychosemiotic reading of
   centers in `docs/language/design-theory.md`.
4. *Sequence* (ch. 11) — imported: the sequences collection.
5. *Every part unique* (ch. 12) — implied by fixed-order/variable-result but
   nowhere stated; unimported. Ch. 12 derives it from feature 4 (modularity is
   a timing fault: the unit designed too early), so it may come along free.
6. *Patterns as generic rules* (ch. 13) — imported; the corpus's founding
   reading (the generative-move framing).
7. *Deep feeling* (ch. 14) — deliberately not imported. The per-step verdict
   in this corpus belongs to the actor's judgement under the suggestion-grade
   stance; feeling as an instrument is Alexander's answer to the same need.
8. *The aperiodic grid* (ch. 15) — building-specific by its own chapter's
   admission; no analogue claimed.
9. *Form language* (ch. 16) — present as the component catalogue and
   foundations; the three-strata division above is the import.
10. *Simplicity* (ch. 17) — not imported as a feature; present only as
    authoring discipline (minimum decision content per step).

The posture this supports: import a feature when a sequence sitting runs into
its absence, not wholesale — the list is necessary-not-sufficient even for
Alexander, and features 5, 7, 8, and 10 have no forced move today.

## Tested against the four authored sequences

The four sequence files (composing-views, structuring-the-space,
acting-on-material, the-encounter) read against the holdings above.

What holds, with evidence:

- *Operand chains are explicit.* Glosses name what a step spends and what
  consumes its product ("the representation choice spends the ladder step's
  product"; "every later act consumes this grammar"; "every activity below
  consumes the aligned topic"; "later regions consume this surface"). This is
  ch. 11's every-operand-manufactured-by-the-preceding-operation, stated in
  the artifact rather than merely true of it.
- *Initiating situations state latency.* Each sub-sequence opens from a
  pressure already present ("narrowing has stopped being enough"; "the space
  has outgrown its model"; "they do not cost alike") — the essential-center
  test of ch. 13 §10 passed at the sub-sequence grain.
- *Latitude and the open end-state are respected.* Glosses state decision
  dimensions, not outcomes; optional steps carry their skip conditions;
  clusters decline false ordering; choices are non-exclusive unless marked.
  Ch. 8 §6 and ch. 18 §1 are satisfied by construction.
- *Deformation is distinguished from backtracking in practice.* `aspect`
  re-invocations at later sites, and acting-on-material's "the grading is
  revised once, not walked again," treat reshaping an earlier deposit as
  normal course — the ch. 10 lability reading, though nothing yet names the
  distinction.
- *The obligation device matches the method.* The-encounter records debts when
  a step creates them and discharges them later ("a way out before any
  activity starts is owed — an obligation the trouble pass collects"). That is
  each-step-enhances-the-whole in the debt direction, and it is a device worth
  keeping.
- *The leads are ch. 9 word pictures.* Each body opens with a vision of the
  walk's product in words, defined only in the aspects the walk decides.

Findings:

1. *The feedback half of feature 1 is not in the artifact.* No step, gloss, or
   resulting context says what would show a step has failed — the "rejected if
   it does not" branch (ch. 8 §5). Authoring-time feedback exists (the test
   walks); use-time per-step testing is implied by the suggestion-grade stance
   but stated nowhere a sequence reader would meet it. The proportionate fix
   is one statement in the sequences spec — a sequence orders decisions that
   each remain to be tested in the adopting product's material. Per-step test
   clauses would bloat every gloss.
2. *Thin cluster-member glosses lean on the cluster gloss for their binding*
   ("Persist their choice"; "Along a chosen attribute"). Acceptable where the
   cluster gloss carries the binding, but the binding job (ch. 10's four
   defects read as a step-writing rule) should be stated as the criterion, so
   thinness is a choice and not an omission.
3. *A step's decision content is one pattern invocation, not one atomic
   decision.* Composing-views' framing step packs query, representation,
   arrangement, and defaults into one step; that is right — the inner
   dimensions belong to the pattern page — but unstated. Recording the
   boundary keeps steps from proliferating into pattern-internal dimensions
   (Zdun's selection-event reading; ch. 9 §6's "limited to a particular
   subject", where the subject is the rule invoked).

## What this settles for the docs

- The pattern language and the generative sequence share one ancestry: both
  are instruments of the fundamental differentiating process — patterns store
  *what may be made* (generic rules for centers), sequences store *in what
  order to invoke them*, and both presuppose step-by-step adaptation with
  per-step feedback as the ground they operate on. The third stratum, the
  form language, is in this corpus the component catalogue and foundations.
- The living-process frame belongs in `docs/language/design-theory.md` (the
  Book 2 bridge between the two phases already recorded there), the working
  terms in the conceptual glossary, and the authoring bar — read-aloud test,
  backtrack-free test walks, one-invocation steps, binding glosses, the
  deformation/backtrack distinction, the use-time feedback statement — in the
  sequences spec.
