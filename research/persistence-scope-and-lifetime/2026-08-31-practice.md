# Practice survey: persistence — scope and lifetime — 2026-08-31

Query: what shipping products and published design systems actually decide about what state is kept, what it is attached to, and how long it lives. Companion to the same-day literature note; practice evidence only.

## 1. Design systems specify the save decision, and they do not start where the library does

Two published systems carry the save question as a named pattern, and both
treat the arrangement as a per-control decision rather than a product-wide
default.

Primer (GitHub) starts from the explicit step: "When designing a form, start
with an explicit saving pattern" ([Primer](https://primer.style/product/ui-patterns/saving/), vendor, fetch).
Automatic saving is scoped to imperative controls — toggles, segmented
controls — where instant feedback is expected, and explicit saving is
*mandatory* for text inputs, checkbox groups, radio groups, native selects, and
multi-select menus (fetch), on the stated grounds of preventing accidental submission of
sensitive data or unintended selections.

Primer also states the mixing rule outright: "Avoid mixing explicit and
automatic save patterns on a single page with multiple forms, and never mix save
patterns in a single form."

GitLab's Pajamas carries the same two arrangements — manual, and "auto-save
where changes are saved automatically with a short delay after the user's last
action" — and adds the failure branch: when a change fails to save, the guidance
is to raise a toast with a retry action rather than retry automatically
([Pajamas](https://design.gitlab.com/patterns/saving-and-feedback/), vendor,
summary).

NN/g's position is that the two are not alternatives at all: autosave "does not
need to replace save: they can both coexist", with visible feedback required
either way to preserve the actor's sense of system status
([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/efficiency-vs-expectations/), summary).

## 2. Where storage is automatic, every source requires it to be reported

No surveyed source lets silent storage stand on its own. Primer: "If the change
is not obvious within the user's viewport, provide feedback to let the user know
that the change occurred successfully", delivered as an inline message or a
banner, with toasts rejected on github.com for accessibility reasons (vendor, fetch).
Pajamas specifies the states as "Saving…" with a spinner and "Saved" with a
timestamp (summary). Figma's manual save is documented as producing a *named
version* rather than a file — the naming and description exist for version
history, not for storage (summary).

That last point is the practice form of the library's own claim in
`draft-and-publish`: once storage is continuous, the surviving explicit act is
about marking a moment, not about persisting.

Primer also names the pending-state obligation on failure: "If there is an error
saving the data, the user's data should be preserved in the form and they should
be given feedback about the failure" (vendor, fetch).

## 3. Session scope is a named, regulated pattern — and it is the one lifetime actors are forced to meet

The clearest case of a deliberately designed lifetime in shipped practice is the
session timeout, and it is designed under legal constraint rather than
preference. The DWP Design System ships "Manage a session timeout" as a pattern
whose job is to help actors "extend their session when it is about to end" and
to "explain what has happened after a session has timed out"
([DWP Design System](https://design-system.dwp.gov.uk/patterns/manage-a-session-timeout), vendor, fetch).

The timing rule is stated: "You should warn a user at least 2 minutes before the
end of this time" (fetch). The driver is WCAG 2.2 success criterion 2.2.1, *Timing
Adjustable*, which requires warning before a timeout and an option to extend
(vendor, summary).

GOV.UK's own design system has no published pattern for this — it sits in the
backlog with a related modal component at prototype stage
([alphagov backlog](https://github.com/alphagov/govuk-design-system-backlog/issues/207), summary).

Two things follow for the library. Session lifetime is the one persistence
decision an accessibility standard forces into the interface, which is a strong
argument that it is designed rather than incidental. And the DWP pattern
overview does not say what happens to the actor's work at timeout — the fetched
page states the pattern explains what happened, without specifying whether data
is preserved or discarded. That gap is where `disengage-without-closing` and
`saving` meet, and practice at this level of documentation does not close it.

## 4. Retention is administered on the record, separately from the material, and defaults to keep

Slack's data retention is set by workspace admins, not by the actors whose
messages are retained, and the default is indefinite: unless a custom policy is
set, all data is kept
([Slack help](https://slack.com/help/articles/203457187-Customize-data-retention-in-Slack), vendor, summary).
The policy choices are keep forever or delete after a set number of days, with
the options varying by plan — on the free plan, either keep everything without
tracking edits and deletions, or let Slack delete after 90 days; on paid plans,
keep with revision tracking, keep without it, or delete on a schedule (summary).

Two details bear on the library directly. Retention applies going forward from
when the setting is saved, not retroactively as a single act (summary) — the
same asymmetry `selective-memory`'s page describes for an erased fact that must
not resurface. And the policy is per-conversation as well as workspace-wide
([Slack help](https://slack.com/help/articles/115005393586-Edit-message-retention-settings-for-specific-conversations), vendor, summary),
so the retention grain in practice is the container, not the item.

This is the practice-side counterpart to the literature note's cluster 2: the
record has its own lifetime, set by a different person, on a different grain,
from the material it describes.

## Convergence

Practice agrees that the save arrangement is a per-control decision with a
stated driver, not a product-wide default, and it agrees that automatic storage
owes a visible report with a timestamp (§1, §2). It agrees that where an
explicit step survives alongside continuous storage, that step is naming a
version rather than persisting work (§2). Session lifetime is the one
persistence decision that reaches the actor by obligation, through an
accessibility criterion, and it comes with a specified warning interval (§3).
Record lifetime is administered separately from material lifetime, by a
different party, at container grain, and defaults to keeping everything (§4).

One difference worth recording rather than treating as a conflict: Primer's
starting point for forms is the explicit save, where `saving.mdx` starts from
automatic and treats the explicit step as needing a driver. Both name the same
driver — a transaction that only makes sense as a unit, and Primer's stated
reason of preventing accidental submission is that driver under another name.
They set the default in opposite directions for the same reason.
