## Context

A pattern language for interaction design, with a focus on human-AI
collaboration. Persistence currently has no node: its forces are distributed
across `saving`, `draft-and-publish`, `disengage-without-closing`,
`selective-memory` and `ai-tuning`, `undo` and `deletion`, `provenance-marking`,
`activity-log`, `shareability`, and the `memory-and-continuity` sequence that
walks all of it. The `temporality` quality mentions persistence once, filed
under Duration. `docs/language/decision-dimensions.md` names the absence: "no
branch reasons about whether state is ephemeral, session-scoped, or durable."

Questions:

1. Is persistence one design territory or several? Does what state is attached
   to — a session, an actor, an object, a record — determine how the move is
   designed?
2. Does the lifetime axis branch design decisions, or is it an implementation
   fact the actor never meets?
3. Resumption after a long absence and across devices — a distinct move from
   remembering, or the reading half of the same one?
4. What do actors believe survives?
5. Does the save/commit boundary carry human meaning beyond storage?
6. Which disciplines already own this word?

Deliberately not re-run: the actor-scoped AI-memory channel
(`research/selective-memory/`, 2026-08-25), interruption-scale resumption
(`research/attention-and-tempo/`, 2026-08-29), and the catch-up move, gated
separately. Findings there are inputs.

## Convergent findings

### 1. The unit state attaches to is a design choice, and "session" is not the only candidate

The strongest single finding, and it challenges the scope axis this run was
built on. Bardram's activity-based computing makes the *activity* — not the
session, not the document, not the actor — the thing state attaches to: "Work is
organized into activities, which are higher-level computational constructs that
encapsulate all resources, tools, and communication mechanisms into one
goal-oriented interaction model." Persistence then attaches to that construct
rather than to any of the four scopes drafted here: "Suspending an activity means
its state is stored and removed from the active work space, while resuming an
activity restores it."

So the answer to Q1 is not "one territory or four" but that the attachment point
is itself the decision. ABC's activity spans applications, devices, and actors
at once — "Activities are stored in an infrastructure and hence can be accessed
from multiple devices" — so it cuts across session, object, and actor scope
rather than sitting alongside them as a fifth. Note what this does and does not
show: files still persist in ABC and preferences still persist, so the activity
construct does not replace the other scopes. What it shows is that the four-way
carve is not exhaustive and not the only available one.

Papers: Bardram et al. 2015 (partial); Bardram et al. 2006 (abstract).

### 2. The record of the past is separately persistent state, and formally so

Dix, Mancini & Levialdi separate two levels of state precisely: `S`, "the state
of the system if there were no undo whatsoever", from `S^a`, "this complete state
of all the system, including the bits needed for undo". The reason the
distinction matters is a proof: "you cannot satisfy the strong undo property with
respect to the full system state `S^a`" — undo returns the material to where it
was without returning the record to where it was, because the record now contains
the undo.

The authors flag it as the hard part: "the authors' experience of presenting
aspects of undo formally and informally suggests that it is one of the most
difficult concepts to grasp."

This converges with the practice side, where retention is administered on the
record independently of the material (see the practice companion, §4), and it
is the formal statement of a channel the library has already marked as
ownerless.

Papers: Dix et al. 2001 (partial).

### 3. Resumption is designed as its own thing, not as the reading half of storage

ABC treats suspension/resumption and cross-device roaming as separate named
principles from storage, and attaches its own interface obligations to the
return: "Users are presented with awareness cues and overviews on the distributed
state and accessibility of the activities." Resumption also carries an
adaptation obligation that storage does not — "Activities adapt to the
capabilities of the device(s) on which they are resumed. Hence, an activity might
look quite different whether it is resumed on a wall-sized display or on a
smartphone."

That last clause is the sharpest transfer to the library: what is restored is
not required to be what was stored. A restore is a rendering decision, which is
why it cannot be the passive reading half of the keeping.

This extends rather than repeats `research/attention-and-tempo/`, whose return
path finding is interruption-scale (seconds to hours, one device, one sitting).
The obligations here are absence-scale and cross-device.

Papers: Bardram et al. 2015 (partial); Bardram et al. 2006 (abstract);
Tam & Greenberg 2006 (placement only).

### 4. Actors expect benign neglect to work, and for digital things it does not

Marshall's answer to Q4 is direct. People know the risk and intend to act —
"Inevitably people realize that their valuable digital assets are at risk, and
they have every intention of addressing this risk" — and then rely on a strategy
carried over from physical things, which she names and rejects: benign neglect
"may be safer than naïve archiving" for physical objects but is "a dangerous
strategy for their digital equivalents."

Two of her four challenges are about scope directly. Distribution: "Individuals'
digital belongings are necessarily distributed over many different stores, some
overlapping, and some completely independent." And retrieval: "We don't remember
what we have; we may not remember the many places where we have stored things."

Whittaker & Hirschberg's earlier office-move study supplies the paper baseline
and a counterintuitive figure: expecting wholesale discarding, "instead we found
workers kept large, highly valued paper archives", and "only 49% of people's
archives were unique documents, the remainder being copies of publicly available
data and unread information."

Papers: Marshall 2008 (fetch summary); Whittaker & Hirschberg 2001 (abstract);
Bergman & Whittaker 2016 (abstract).

### 5. Lifetime designed as a feature is under-evidenced as a benefit

Q2's clearest case is ephemeral messaging, and the evidence is mixed rather than
supportive. Bayer et al. found Snapchat interactions "were perceived as more
enjoyable — and associated with more positive mood — than other communication
technologies", and in the same breath that they "were also associated with lower
social support than other channels."

Single study, one population (college students), and the outcomes are affective
rather than design-decision outcomes. It supports the weak claim that designed
ephemerality changes the character of what happens in the channel. It does not
support the strong claim that shortening a lifetime is a way to improve an
interaction.

Papers: Bayer et al. 2015 (abstract).

*Superseded in part by the addendum below (clusters 6–8): this cluster answers
the narrow question of whether shortening a lifetime is shown to improve an
interaction, not whether designed lifetime is a developed design territory. It
is.*

## What this challenges

- *The scope axis this run was built on may be the wrong carve.* The session /
  actor / object / record split was drafted in this session and is stated in the
  query context. Bardram's activity construct is a fifth candidate that
  dissolves the first three rather than joining them, and Card & Henderson's
  Rooms says the same thing fourteen years earlier. Nothing in the retrieved set
  endorses the four-way split; it survives this run as an unexamined assumption,
  not as a finding.

- *`temporality.mdx` files persistence under Duration* — "How the system
  communicates time passing and progress" — with the entry "*Persistence:* When
  and how changes are saved." Dix's `S` / `S^a` distinction and Marshall's
  distribution challenge are both about where state lives and what level it
  lives at, neither of which is communicated duration. The node's own *History*
  heading is the nearer home.

- *The memory-and-continuity sequence calls the record's expiry a channel
  "which no pattern holds and this cluster only marks."* That is right as far as
  ownership goes, and cluster 2 says the marking understates it: the record is
  not merely an unowned third channel but a formally distinct level of state
  whose behaviour under reversal provably differs from the material's.

## Noted, not a challenge

`saving.mdx` states "Automatic is the better default for open-ended work" and
treats a surviving explicit step as the exception needing a driver. Primer's
shipped guidance sets the opposite starting point for one large class — "When
designing a form, start with an explicit saving pattern" — and makes explicit
save mandatory for text inputs and grouped choices (practice companion, §1).
This is reconcilable through saving.mdx's own transaction-scope driver, and
Primer's stated reason (preventing accidental submission) is that driver under
another name. Recorded because the page's default reads as more general than
practice supports, not because the two disagree.

## Convergent findings (continued)

### 6. "Persistence by default" is a named position in the field, with a literature against it

The framing this run reached for is already the field's. Ankenbauer & Brewer
open on "a hegemonic design practice and a general social desire to retain
information", producing "unbounded, algorithmic, and emergent forms of
retention", and set forgetting against it as a counter-practice.

They name the wellspring — Bannon's *Forgetting as a Feature, Not a Bug* — and
quote its call: "Let us explore some scenarios where the technology might be
used to assist in forms of forgetting, rather than remembering." The temporal
branch runs through Mayer-Schönberger's *Delete*, where "data should be assigned
an expiration date", and Geambasu et al.'s Vanish.

The social finding is the sharpest one for the library's privacy neighbours,
quoting boyd: "a persistence-by-default-minded assumption is that anyone who
doesn't want their data to be persistent has something to hide." Designing a
shorter lifetime is read as an admission, which is a cost the keep-forever
default never pays.

Papers: Ankenbauer & Brewer 2025 (partial).

### 7. Forgetting has a six-practice taxonomy, and design almost never reaches the binary one

Ankenbauer & Brewer "identify six general forgetting practices: 1) pure
forgetting or an immediate and total loss of information; 2) performative
forgetting or a negotiated forgetting without the full commitment to pure
forgetting; 3) temporal forgetting or forgetting characterized by time; 4)
spatial forgetting or forgetting characterized by space and environment; 5)
visualized forgetting or forgetting as something that can be seen; 6)
unintentional forgetting."

The result that matters is what happens to the first one: "HCI and CSCW research
often seems skeptical of pure forgetting", and every design line they trace
stops short of it. They catch Vitale et al. doing exactly this — a paper mapping
the design space for forgetting that nonetheless concludes "it is essential that
(...) any action is reversible", preferring "softer actions over the more
radical concept of deletion". Sas et al. supply the reason: deletion is "a crude
binary" of existence and non-existence, "a binary not found in actual practice
or design." Even where forgetting is the product, retention wins — Snapchat's
ephemerality is bypassed by a screenshot, and the authors' summary is blunt:
"Even within systems where forgetting is a main feature, retention is king."

The practices that do get built are graded rather than binary: reducing salience
("systems should be designed to forget through reduc[ing] the salience of
certain information"), moving material to less accessible storage under
information lifecycle management, obscuring rather than removing — "one has a
better chance of forgetting not by deleting information but by obfuscating it" —
and visible decay, where a patina or a fade renders the forgetting itself.

Papers: Ankenbauer & Brewer 2025 (partial); Vitale et al. 2019 (partial).

### 8. Keeping and discarding is a design space with named dimensions

Vitale, Odom & McGrenere give the run the thing it was looking for and did not
find in the main pass: an explicit dimension set for this territory, derived by
"clustering and mapping insights from prior work into four key design dimensions
to probe: selection regime, automation, aggressiveness, temporality."

- *Selection regime* — "whether they consider one item at a time or a collection
  of items all together."
- *Automation* — "the tension between user-initiated selection and automation."
- *Aggressiveness* — open-ended concepts that "only inform of data to take care
  of ... letting them decide what to do and when" against ones "more forceful"
  that "push the user to decide."
- *Temporality* — "either retrospective, looking at items based on past use, or
  prospective, looking at items based on future use", with the prospective side
  named as "largely underexplored".

Note that none of the four is the lifetime axis this run set out with, and none
is a scope axis either. Their temporality dimension is the actor's *mindset*
when deciding, not the duration of the thing.

Two further results. Automation is contextually acceptable rather than
categorically rejected: "promising initial contexts to pursue automation ... are
mobile devices with limited storage space, media files, and distributed data
(i.e., data that is not unique or otherwise re-accessible)", and elsewhere "only
with proper safeguards." And they propose a grammar of discarding actions past
delete — "mirroring (for storing a copy of data from a central repository only
temporarily), distributing ..., warranting (to authorize automatic tools to act
only on specific items), locking (to mark items as protected by any discarding
action)", alongside Harper et al.'s "eradicating" and "withdrawing" and Bergman
et al.'s "demoting" — with the summary claim that "providing options for
discarding actions to be granular rather than binary will also allow for more
personalized solutions."

Papers: Vitale et al. 2019 (partial); Ankenbauer & Brewer 2025 (partial).

## What this challenges (addendum)

- *Main-pass cluster 5 is too weak, and was built on the wrong paper.* It
  concluded that "lifetime designed as a feature is under-evidenced as a
  benefit" from a single affective study of Snapchat. The claim about *benefit*
  survives, but the framing does not: designed lifetime is a developed research
  area with a named canon (Bannon, Mayer-Schönberger, Vanish) and a six-practice
  taxonomy. Read cluster 5 as answering a narrow question — is shortening a
  lifetime shown to improve an interaction — and clusters 6 and 7 as answering
  the one the run actually asked.

- *`deletion.mdx` and the sequence's forgetting region treat removal as an act
  with remains.* The literature goes further: the binary itself is the problem.
  "A binary representation of data as either present or deleted does not reflect
  the majority of our participants' mindset", and the proposed vocabulary —
  demote, withdraw, mirror, warrant, lock, obscure, decay — is a set of moves the
  library currently has no names for. If any single finding in this run is a
  candidate for authoring work, it is this one.

- *The scope axis takes a second hit, from a different direction.* Beyond
  Bardram's activity construct, Ankenbauer & Brewer record WeChat Moments
  operating on an "ephemerality for them, persistence for me" principle. The
  same object has two lifetimes at once, differing by who is looking. Lifetime
  is not a property of the thing; it is a property of the thing *per party*,
  which neither the scope axis nor the lifetime axis as drafted can express.

- *Q6 has an answer after all, and it is close to home.* The run recorded the
  discipline question as barely touched. Information lifecycle management is
  named directly — material moved "to less accessible digital storage elsewhere
  — out of sight and out of mind (but still existent)" — and explicitly likened
  to "how paper records eventually become consigned to large warehouses in rural
  areas." That is records management's retention schedule, reached from the HCI
  side without going to appraisal theory.
