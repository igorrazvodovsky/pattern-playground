## Context

Authoring a generative sequence ordering interruption design decisions: which events may
interrupt, when they arrive, in what shape, and how the actor resumes. The questions:

1. Is deferring interruption delivery to task boundaries or breakpoints (rather than
   immediate delivery) supported, and does batching help or hurt?
2. Is "push what cannot wait, keep the rest for a pulled catch-up" the right split, or
   does deferral itself carry costs — anticipation, self-interruption, checking habits?
3. Do actor-tuned controls (channels, quiet hours, focus and do-not-disturb modes)
   calibrate interruption effectively, or do people misconfigure and abandon them?
4. Does an interruption owe a return path — is resumption support (resumption lag, cues,
   state restoration) evidenced as a design obligation?
5. Is McFarlane's coordination taxonomy (immediate / negotiated / mediated / scheduled)
   the right shape for the timing decision, and which method wins?

## Retrieved papers

- *Comparison of Four Primary Methods for Coordinating the Interruption of People in
  Human-Computer Interaction* (McFarlane, Human–Computer Interaction 17(1), 2002,
  pp. 63–139) — full-text PDF located at
  [interruptions.net](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/McFarlane-HCI02_2.pdf);
  abstract and front matter read; venue confirmed via
  [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/hhci/McFarlane02.html) and
  [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S15327051HCI1701_2).
  Speaks to Q5. The taxonomy paper and its own experiment (36 participants, abstracted
  multitasking): "The results show important design tradeoffs for coordinating the
  interruption of people in HCI and support some UI design guidelines. Negotiation
  support is the best overall solution except where small differences in the timeliness
  of handling interruptions is critical and then immediate is best." Implications:
  default to announcing an interruption and letting the actor choose its moment;
  reserve forced immediate delivery for cases where seconds of timeliness matter; no
  single method dominates, so the timing decision is a genuine trade-off, not a policy
  constant. Transfer: strong — the setting is generic desktop multitasking, and
  "supervise background autonomous services" is named in the abstract as a motivating
  activity, which is exactly the background-agent case.
- *If not now, when? The effects of interruption at different moments within task
  execution* (Adamczyk & Bailey, CHI 2004) — abstract-grounded via
  [Illinois Experts](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/if-not-now-when-the-effects-of-interruption-at-different-moments-/)
  and the [ACM DL listing](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/985692.985727).
  Speaks to Q1. "Different interruption moments have different impacts on user emotional
  state and positive social attribution", and a system choosing moments "could enable a
  user to maintain a high level of awareness while mitigating the disruptive effects of
  interruption". Implications: delivery moment is a controllable design variable with
  measurable emotional consequences; picking boundary moments preserves awareness while
  cutting disruption. Transfer: strong on desktop tasks (the study used editing,
  reading, and search tasks), partial in that it is a lab study with experimenter-timed
  interruptions.
- *Oasis: a framework for linking notification delivery to the perceptual structure of
  goal-directed tasks* (Iqbal & Bailey, ACM TOCHI 17(4), 2010) — full-text read
  (abstract and introduction) from the
  [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/TOCHI-Oasis-final.pdf);
  venue confirmed via the [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/1879831.1879833).
  Speaks to Q1 and Q5 (it is the strongest mediated-coordination result). Summarising
  its own experimental lineage: "Empirical studies have shown that deferral of
  notifications until breakpoints are reached during task execution reduces interruption
  costs as measured by resumption lag and subjective assessments of frustration", and
  "coarser breakpoints correspond with successively larger reductions in interruption
  cost". The system contribution: "The value of our system is that it intelligently
  schedules notifications, enabling the reductions in interruption costs shown within
  prior controlled studies to now be realized by users in everyday desktop computing
  tasks." Also, against turning alerts off: "users prefer to continue the use of
  notifications to cue them to new information despite the purported interruption costs
  rather than to turn the notifications off and have to check for new information
  manually". Implications: breakpoint deferral is implementable by a system without
  knowing the task; prefer coarser boundaries for larger savings; deferral horizons in
  this line are short (to the next perceptual boundary), not indefinite holds.
  Transfer: strong — everyday desktop computing tasks, an actual scheduling system.
- *Disruption and recovery of computing tasks: field study, analysis, and directions*
  (Iqbal & Horvitz, CHI 2007) — full-text read from the
  [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/CHI_2007_Iqbal_Horvitz-1.pdf);
  venue confirmed via [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/chi/IqbalH07.html).
  Speaks to Q4, Q2. Two-week field study of 27 information workers: "participants spent
  on average nearly 10 minutes on switches caused by alerts, and spent on average
  another 10 to 15 minutes (depending on the type of interruption) before returning to
  focused activity on the disrupted task", and "27% of task suspensions resulted in more
  than two hours of time until resumption". On what alerts are for: "Users view alerts
  as an awareness mechanism rather than a trigger to switch tasks, but the alerts often
  cause them to do otherwise." On checking habits: "All users mentioned that they would
  habitually self interrupt themselves to access Outlook regardless of alerts." The
  paper's own design implications, verbatim headings: "Provide visual indicators of
  occluded application windows to assist with the recovery of suspended tasks",
  "Automatically save task context on suspension", "Provide easy access to suspended
  task context", "Provide playback of actions within task contexts". Also: "the
  visibility of windows may serve as a reminder to users to break out of the diversion
  chain and return to suspended applications". Implications: the expensive half of an
  interruption is the return, so the return path is an explicit obligation — visible
  cues to the suspended work, saved context, and replay of recent actions; self-
  interruption exists independently of alerts, so removing alerts does not remove
  checking. Transfer: strong — desktop knowledge work, email and IM alerts, directly
  the product context.
- *Notifications and awareness: a field study of alert usage and preferences* (Iqbal &
  Horvitz, CSCW 2010) — full-text first page read from the
  [Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/253n-iqbal.pdf);
  venue confirmed via the [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1718918.1718926).
  Speaks to Q2, Q3. Two-week field study (20 users) with notifications disabled in the
  second week: "Results showed that users view notifications as a mechanism to provide
  passive awareness rather than a trigger to switch tasks. Turing off notifications
  cause some users to self interrupt more to explicitly monitor email arrival, while
  others appear to be able to better focus on their tasks. Users acknowledge
  notifications as disruptive, yet opt for them because of their perceived value in
  providing awareness." (The "Turing" typo is in the original.) Implications: removing
  push does not remove the awareness need — for a subset of actors it converts delivery
  into polling; the effect is bimodal, so a single default is wrong for someone; people
  knowingly pay the disruption price for awareness, so the awareness function must
  survive any deferral design. Transfer: strong — workplace desktop email notifications.
- *The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress* (Mark, Gudith & Klocke, CHI
  2008) — first page read from the
  [UCI PDF](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf); venue confirmed via the
  [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1357054.1357072).
  Speaks to Q1, Q2 (what interruption actually costs). "We found that context does not
  make a difference but surprisingly, people completed interrupted tasks in less time
  with no difference in quality. Our data suggests that people compensate for
  interruptions by working faster, but this comes at a price: experiencing more stress,
  higher frustration, time pressure and effort." Implications: throughput metrics will
  not show interruption damage — the cost surfaces as strain, so timing design cannot be
  justified or dismissed on completion time alone; whether an interruption shares the
  task's context mattered less than expected. Transfer: partial — a lab simulation of
  office email work; the compensation-under-stress mechanism plausibly carries to any
  paced knowledge work.
- *Memory for goals: an activation-based model* (Altmann & Trafton, Cognitive Science
  26(1), 2002) with its interruption-paradigm test, *The effect of interruption duration
  and demand on resuming suspended goals* (Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis, Journal of
  Experimental Psychology: Applied 14(4), 2008) — Altmann & Trafton abstract-grounded
  via the [Wiley listing](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2)
  ("the priming constraint, which makes predictions about the role of cues in retrieving
  pending goals"); Monk et al. abstract read from the
  [interruptions.net PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Monk-JEPA08.pdf).
  Speaks to Q4. Monk et al., verbatim: "Three experiments using an interleaved tasks
  interruption paradigm showed that longer and more demanding interruptions led to
  longer resumption times in a hierarchical, interactive task. The resumption time
  profile for durations up to 1 min supported the role of decay in defining resumption
  costs, and the interaction between duration and demand supported the importance of
  goal rehearsal in mitigating decay." Implications: suspended goals decay, so
  resumption cost grows with the interruption's length and cognitive demand — keep
  interruptions short and shallow when they must happen; associative cues are the
  mechanism by which pending goals are retrieved, which is the theoretical warrant for
  resumption cues in the interface. Transfer: partial — cognitive lab paradigms; the
  decay-and-cues mechanism is task-general and underwrites the field studies above.
- *An in-situ study of mobile phone notifications* (Pielot, Church & de Oliveira,
  MobileHCI 2014) — grounded via the authors' published summary sheet
  ([pielot.org PDF](https://pielot.org/pubs/Pielot2014-MobileHCI-Notifications-InfoBites.pdf),
  read in full) and the [ACM DL listing](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2628363.2628364).
  Speaks to Q2, Q3. One week, 15 users, 6854 logged notifications: "63.5 notifications
  (median) per participant per day, which our participants considered to be 'the
  usual'"; "More emails: increased feelings of being overwhelmed, interrupted, stressed,
  and annoyed." The authors' own implication lines: "Reducing the number of
  notifications — not advisable: users will check their phones more frequently to not
  miss 'important' messages"; "Communicating availability: most promising approach for
  personal communication, as it would allow to manage expectations." Implications:
  cutting volume without addressing the awareness need breeds checking; making the
  actor's availability legible to senders manages expectation at the source — for agent
  work, the analogue is the agent knowing the actor's state rather than the actor
  polling the agent. Transfer: partial — mobile personal communication; the checking
  dynamic transfers, but much of the felt pressure comes from human senders expecting
  replies, which agent-originated events do not carry.
- *Productive, anxious, lonely: 24 hours without push notifications* (Pielot & Rello,
  MobileHCI 2017) — abstract-grounded via the
  [arxiv abs page](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02314); venue confirmed via the
  [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3098279.3098526).
  Speaks to Q2. 30 volunteers, all devices, 24 hours: "without notifications,
  participants felt less distracted and more productive. But, they also felt no longer
  able to be as responsive as expected, which made some participants anxious. And, they
  felt less connected with one's social group." Implications: silencing everything
  trades distraction for anticipation and anxiety — deferral has a felt cost even when
  it works; the cost is driven by responsiveness expectations, so its size depends on
  who or what is on the other end. Transfer: partial to weak — the anxiety mechanism is
  social (humans awaiting replies); a background agent awaiting nothing removes most of
  that pressure, though anticipation about pending results may substitute for it.
- *"Silence your phones": smartphone notifications increase inattention and
  hyperactivity symptoms* (Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn, CHI 2016) — abstract read from the
  [interruptions.net PDF](https://interruptions.net/literature/Kushlev-CHI16.pdf);
  venue confirmed via [dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/chi/KushlevPD16.html).
  Speaks to Q2, Q3. Week-long within-subjects field experiment (221 participants,
  alerts-on-and-near vs alerts-off-and-away): "Participants reported higher levels of
  inattention and hyperactivity when alerts were on than when alerts were off. Higher
  levels of inattention in turn predicted lower productivity and psychological
  well-being", and the findings "suggest how people can reduce these costs simply by
  adjusting existing phone settings." Implications: sustained push exposure measurably
  degrades attention, so quieting delivery is a real lever, not a comfort feature — a
  direct counterweight to the silence-backfires findings above. Transfer: partial —
  smartphones and general life, not desktop work; the attention mechanism is general.
- *Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being* (Fitz, Kushlev,
  Jagannathan, Lewis, Paliwal & Ariely, Computers in Human Behavior 101, 2019) —
  abstract read from the author-hosted proof PDF
  ([kushlev.com](https://www.kushlev.com/s/2019-Fitz-Kushlev-etal-2019.pdf), via its
  squarespace redirect); confirmed via the
  [ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219302596).
  Speaks to Q1, Q2. Randomised field experiment, n = 237, notifications as usual vs
  batched vs never: "participants whose notifications were batched three-times-a-day
  felt more attentive, productive, in a better mood, and in greater control of their
  phones", while "participants who did not receive notifications at all reaped few of
  those benefits, but experienced higher levels of anxiety and 'fear of missing out'
  (FoMO)". Secondary summaries of the results add that hourly batching produced little
  change relative to control — that cadence finding sits in the paper's results, which
  were not read here, so treat it as reported rather than quoted. Implications:
  batching to a few predictable daily deliveries beats both a continuous stream and
  total silence; cadence matters — batching that is too frequent buys nothing
  (reported, unquoted); predictability of the delivery moment is doing part of the
  work. Transfer: partial — smartphone notifications in daily life; the
  batch-versus-never contrast is the transferable shape for a pulled catch-up design.
- *NUGU: a group-based intervention app for improving self-regulation of limiting
  smartphone use* (Ko et al., CSCW 2015) — abstract-grounded via
  [Yonsei Pure](https://yonsei.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/nugu-a-group-based-intervention-app-for-improving-self-regulation)
  and the [ACM DL listing](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2675133.2675244).
  Speaks to Q3. From the abstract: "users often had difficulties in maintaining their
  chosen management strategies due to lack of self-regulation", and in the three-week
  study (n = 62), "compared with its non-social counterpart, the NUGU users' usage
  amount significantly decreased and their perceived level of managing disturbances
  improved". Implications: self-set limiting strategies are adopted but not sustained
  unaided — controls that depend on the actor's continued discipline will be abandoned;
  external scaffolding (here social, but plausibly any accountability structure)
  sustains them. Transfer: weak — smartphone overuse among Korean students, and the
  intervention is social; only the fragility-of-self-regulation finding is carried over.

## Convergent findings

- *Deferring delivery to task boundaries is well supported.* Interrupting at breakpoints
  rather than mid-action reduces resumption lag and frustration, coarser boundaries
  save more, and a system can find the boundaries automatically. Members: Adamczyk &
  Bailey 2004; Iqbal & Bailey 2010 (carrying Iqbal & Bailey 2005/2006). No
  disagreement, but note the studied deferrals are short — to the next perceptual
  boundary, not multi-hour holds.
- *Negotiated coordination beats forced immediacy; immediate delivery is for genuinely
  time-critical events only.* Members: McFarlane 2002; consistent with Pielot et
  al. 2014 (availability communication as the promising direction). Mild tension: the
  Iqbal/Bailey line shows mediated (system-decided) timing performing well in practice,
  whereas McFarlane's single experiment crowned negotiation — the two are reconcilable
  if the system mediates the moment and the actor keeps the negotiation controls.
- *Notifications are kept for passive awareness, not as switch triggers.* People
  knowingly pay the disruption price to avoid manual checking. Members: Iqbal & Horvitz
  2007; Iqbal & Horvitz 2010; Oasis introduction. No disagreement.
- *Removing push entirely backfires for many: self-interruption, checking, anxiety,
  FoMO.* Members: Iqbal & Horvitz 2010 (some users poll more); Pielot et al. 2014
  ("not advisable"); Pielot & Rello 2017; Fitz et al. 2019 (never condition).
  Disagreement: Kushlev et al. 2016 found a week with alerts off reduced inattention
  and improved well-being — the silence-costs finding is real but not universal, and
  Iqbal & Horvitz 2010 itself found the effect bimodal.
- *Batching works at a coarse, predictable cadence.* Three predictable deliveries a day
  improved attention, mood, and felt control; hourly batching bought little (reported,
  unquoted); never delivering raised anxiety. Members: Fitz et al. 2019; consistent
  with the coarser-breakpoints gradient in Iqbal & Bailey 2010. Single-study evidence
  for the specific cadence — thin.
- *The return path is an evidenced design obligation.* Suspended goals decay with
  interruption length and demand; field data shows ten-minute-plus diversions and 27%
  of suspensions lasting over two hours; visible cues to suspended work speed
  resumption; the papers' own implications are cues, saved context, and action
  playback. Members: Altmann & Trafton 2002 / Monk et al. 2008; Iqbal & Horvitz 2007.
  No disagreement.
- *Interruption costs hide from output metrics, and controls that need discipline
  decay.* Interrupted work finishes on time at the price of stress and strain; self-set
  limiting strategies lapse without external scaffolding. Members: Mark et al. 2008;
  Ko et al. 2015. No disagreement.

## What this challenges

- *Deferral to breakpoints is supported, but not the long hold the strawman implies.*
  The breakpoint studies deferred delivery by seconds to minutes, to the next
  perceptual boundary of the current task. Nothing in this set validates holding
  non-urgent items for hours; the one study of coarse batching (Fitz et al. 2019) is a
  single smartphone experiment, and its hourly condition reportedly bought nothing —
  the batching evidence is thinner and more cadence-sensitive than "batching helps"
  suggests.
- *"Push what cannot wait, pull the rest" ignores the awareness function and the cost
  of silence.* Users treat notifications as passive awareness and prefer them to
  manual checking (Iqbal & Horvitz 2007, 2010; Oasis); turning delivery off made some
  users self-interrupt more to poll (Iqbal & Horvitz 2010), and total silence produced
  anxiety, FoMO, and felt unresponsiveness (Pielot & Rello 2017; Fitz et al. 2019).
  A pulled catch-up must still leave a passive, glanceable signal that nothing needs
  attention, or it converts push into polling. Against this, Kushlev et al. 2016 found
  alerts-off improved attention and well-being — the direction of the silence effect
  is genuinely contested, and Iqbal & Horvitz 2010 found it differs by person, so the
  split cannot be a fixed default.
- *Evidence that actor-tuned controls calibrate interruption well is thin to absent in
  this set.* No retrieved study shows people configuring channels, quiet hours, or DND
  well; the closest evidence points the other way — self-set limiting strategies fail
  for lack of self-regulation unless externally scaffolded (Ko et al. 2015), and
  self-interruption persists regardless of alert settings (Iqbal & Horvitz 2007).
  Kushlev et al. 2016 shows settings *can* deliver benefits, not that people sustain
  them. A claim that controls suffice should be treated as unverified.
- *Resumption support survives challenge — it is the best-evidenced obligation here* —
  but with a sharpening: delayed and self-paced returns showed resumption phases no
  shorter than immediate ones (Iqbal & Horvitz 2007), so deferring an interruption
  does not by itself cheapen the return; cues and saved state are owed either way.
- *McFarlane's taxonomy holds as the decision's shape, but the strawman's preferred
  cell may be the wrong winner.* A deferral engine that unilaterally reschedules
  delivery is mediated/scheduled coordination — and in McFarlane's own comparison
  those did not win: "Negotiation support is the best overall solution except where
  small differences in the timeliness of handling interruptions is critical and then
  immediate is best." The later breakpoint work rehabilitates mediation for the
  *moment* of delivery, but the evidence favours keeping the actor's negotiation
  handles rather than replacing them.
- *Speed metrics will not show the damage or the benefit.* People absorb interruptions
  by working faster at the cost of stress (Mark et al. 2008), so a timing design
  evaluated on task completion alone can look like a no-op while shifting real costs.
