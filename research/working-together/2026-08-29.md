## Context

Several people work in the same material (a document, a board, a codebase, a plan); the design decides who can see and touch what, what each person knows of the others' activity, how contributions are made and attributed, and what happens when contributions collide. The corpus has one chain (annotation → commenting → status-feedback), attachments (shareability, saving, activity-log, notification, draft-and-publish), and four named absences: presence, concurrent conflict, shared views, human-to-human handoff. Six authored arrivals from other sequences await landing stations.

Questions:

1. Is presence one pattern or several graded mechanisms — does the literature give a structure the corpus should mirror?
2. Does visibility → awareness → accountability hold up as an ordering over collaboration capabilities?
3. Is synchrony a split into separate sequences, a choice point, or a rate at which one spine is walked?
4. What does the merge-model choice surface to actors — when does a person see a conflict at all?
5. Does the sharing grant precede the other capabilities, or does sharing grow incrementally from private work?
6. Which collaboration features fail from the work/benefit disparity, and does that mark optional steps?

## Canon reads (named-and-grounded; questions 1–3, 5–6)

- *A descriptive framework of workspace awareness for real-time groupware* (Gutwin & Greenberg, CSCW journal 2002) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1021271517844), [open preprint](https://collablab.northwestern.edu//CollabolabDistro/nucmc/GutwinGreenberg_FrameworkWorkspaceAwareness.pdf). *Full-text read* (pp. 1–10). Workspace awareness is "the up-to-the-moment understanding of another person's interaction with the shared workspace" — "knowledge about where others are working, what they are doing, and what they are going to do next", organised as a three-part framework: what information people track, how they gather it, and what they use it for ("for coordinating action, managing coupling, talking about the task, anticipating others' actions, and finding opportunities to assist one another"). Two facts for the sequence beyond the element structure. The setting is *mixed-focus collaboration* — "people shift frequently between individual and shared activities during a work session" — so awareness is what makes the shifts cheap, not a feature of constant togetherness. And the baseline condition is relaxed-WYSIWIS: once "different people can scroll to different parts of the workspace… any information about where the other person is working or what they are doing can only be gathered through verbal communication" unless the design supplies it — the awareness problem is created by the same relaxation that makes shared workspaces usable. Speaks to Q1. Transfer: strong — deliberately tool-generic, and the framework declines prescriptive order ("we do not give prescriptive rules and guidelines… each groupware application will have to operate within particular awareness requirements dictated by the task").
- *Awareness and coordination in shared workspaces* (Dourish & Bellotti, CSCW '92) — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/143457.143468). S2 abstract-grounded. Awareness support "commonly supported in CSCW systems by active, information generation mechanisms separate from the shared workspace" — and these "penalise information providers, presuppose relevance to the recipient, and make access difficult". Their shared-editor study argues instead for awareness "provided and exploited passively through the shared workspace", which "allows users to move smoothly between close and loose collaboration, and to assign and coordinate work dynamically". Speaks to Q1 and Q6 together: awareness information should be a by-product of doing the work, not a separate reporting duty — which is also the design answer to Grudin's disparity, since a by-product costs the provider nothing. Transfer: strong.
- *Social translucence: an approach to designing systems that support social processes* (Erickson & Kellogg, TOCHI 2000) — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/344949.345004), [author PDF](https://tomeri.org/TOCHI2000_SocialTranslucence.pdf). *Full-text read* (pp. 1–8). Visibility, awareness, and accountability are one causal chain from a single design act, the glass window in the door: making socially significant information *visible* produces *awareness* ("I don't open the door quickly because I know that you're on the other side") which produces *accountability* ("I know that you know that I know") — and the paper is explicit that the links are severable in software: "while accountability and awareness usually co-occur in the physical world, they are not necessarily coupled in the digital realm." Translucence, not transparence: "there is a vital tension between privacy and visibility", and constraints on visibility are themselves a resource for structuring interaction. Speaks to Q2: not an ordering over capabilities but the consequence structure of one capability — making activity visible — whose accountability face must be separately earned in digital systems. Transfer: strong.
- *Groupware: some issues and experiences* (Ellis, Gibbs & Rein, CACM 1991) — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/99977.99987), [open PDF](https://www.lri.fr/~mbl/ENS/CSCW/2013/papers/Ellis-CACM-91.pdf). Abstract/secondary-grounded. The field's framing paper and source of the time-space matrix (same/different time × same/different place) — a taxonomy of *systems*, not a staging of decisions. Speaks to Q3: the matrix classifies what a product is, and the corpus's collaboration page already carries its temporal variants; nothing in it orders one capability before another. Transfer: partial — taxonomy, not design guidance.
- *Groupware and social dynamics: eight challenges for developers* (Grudin, CACM 1994) — [ACM record via S2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9a87ce1fcddff62d714d103d847d740a586a190a), [open HTML](https://webpages.charlotte.edu/richter/classes/2006/6010/readings/cacm94.html). Abstract/secondary-grounded. The first challenge is the work/benefit disparity: "groupware systems often require work from people who do not benefit directly", and "a groupware application never provides precisely the same benefit to every group member". Speaks to Q6: any step whose information must be *authored* for someone else's benefit (a status update, a manual presence flag, a hand-written trail) is at risk of going unfed; steps whose information is captured as a by-product are safe. Transfer: strong.
- *WYSIWIS revised: early experiences with multiuser interfaces* (Stefik, Bobrow, Foster, Lanning & Tatar, TOIS 1987) — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/27636.28056), [open PDF](https://www.lri.fr/~mbl/ENS/CSCW/2013/papers/Stefik-TOIS87.pdf). Abstract/secondary-grounded. Strict what-you-see-is-what-I-see is "crucial, yet too inflexible when strictly enforced"; the relaxations run along four dimensions — display space, time of display, subgroup population, and congruence of view. Speaks to Q1 and Q4: the fully shared view is the limiting case, every real system relaxes it, and the four dimensions are the axes along which a design decides *how much* the collaborators' views may diverge — with awareness support and conflict exposure both consequences of that decision. Transfer: strong.
- *Distance matters* (Olson & Olson, Human–Computer Interaction 2000) — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/S15327051HCI1523_4), [open PDF](https://ics.uci.edu/~corps/phaseii/OlsonOlson-DistanceMatters-HCIJ.pdf). Abstract/secondary-grounded. Four factors decide whether distributed collaboration works: common ground, coupling of work, collaboration readiness, and collaboration technology readiness. Tightly coupled work — "closely collaborating… on highly interdependent tasks" — wants co-presence and synchrony; loosely coupled work tolerates distance and delay. Speaks to Q3: synchrony is keyed to the coupling of the task, which varies per stretch of work inside one product, not per product — the same answer at task scale that mixed-focus collaboration gives at session scale. Transfer: strong.
- *Local-first software: you own your data, in spite of the cloud* (Kleppmann, Wiggins, van Hardenberg & McGranaghan, Onward! 2019) — [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3359591.3359737), [author PDF](https://martin.kleppmann.com/papers/local-first.pdf). *Full-text read* (§2.4, §§3–4.2). The design-facing account of the merge substrate. Users "have come to expect this kind of seamless real-time collaboration" that cloud apps established by letting people edit "without worrying about conflicts"; the failure case is documented concretely (Evernote moves a concurrently changed note to a "conflicting changes" notebook with "nothing to support the user in resolving the situation — not even a facility to compare the different versions"; Git-style merges have tools only "for line-oriented text files"). On CRDTs: "the only type of change that a CRDT cannot automatically resolve is when multiple users concurrently update the same property of the same object; in this case, the CRDT keeps track of the conflicting values, and leaves it to be resolved by the application or the user." And granularity is a choice over the same substrate: changes "can be as small as a single keystroke, enabling Google Docs-style real-time collaboration. But you could also collect a larger set of changes and send them to collaborators as a batch, more like a pull request in Git." Their prototypes surface residual conflicts minimally (PixelPusher "highlights conflicted pixels in red"). Speaks to Q4 decisively, and to Q3 (real-time and asynchronous are batch sizes over one substrate, not different worlds). Transfer: strong.

## Retrieved papers (arxiv; question 4 mostly)

- *"Don't step on my toes": resolving editing conflicts in real-time collaboration in computational notebooks* (CHI 2024) — [arxiv 2404.04695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04695). Data scientists co-editing notebooks "may inadvertently interfere with each others' work by altering the shared codebase and runtime state if they do not set up a social protocol for working together and monitoring their collaborators' progress"; the proposed model introduces graded *levels of edit protection*. Speaks to Q4: even inside live co-editing, conflict handling is part social protocol (awareness and territory norms) and part mechanism, and protection is graded rather than binary. Transfer: strong — the domain is specialised but the finding is about co-editing generally.
- *Semantic conflict model for collaborative data structures* (2026) — [arxiv 2602.19231](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19231). CRDTs "ensure convergence through built-in conflict resolution", but "this resolution is typically implicit and opaque to users"; the paper's contribution is *explicit, local-first conflict resolution* — keeping the conflict as a first-class value a person can see and settle. Speaks to Q4: the systems community itself now argues that silent merge over-reaches, and that some collisions deserve surfacing. Transfer: partial (systems venue, design implication clear).
- *Time-position characterization of conflicts: a case study of collaborative editing* (2020) — [arxiv 2010.16153](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16153). Empirical study of collaborative editing sessions characterising conflicts as "edits from different users that occur close in time and position in the document", splitting work into single-authored and co-authored sessions. Speaks to Q4: collision is rare and localised in real behaviour — people mostly self-partition, which is what awareness enables. Transfer: partial (one corpus of editing traces).

Two further arxiv results corroborate weakly and are noted without extraction: *Collaborative real time coding or how to avoid the dreaded merge* (2015, [1504.06741](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.06741)) and *Avoiding conflicts is better than solving conflicts* (2009, [0911.0838](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.0838)) — both argue prevention over resolution from the systems side, agreeing with the behavioural finding.

## Convergent findings

### 1. Awareness is a graded information layer fed by the work itself, and presence is its "who" slice

Gutwin & Greenberg structure workspace awareness as multiple elements (who is present, where they are working, what they are doing, what they will do next) maintained continuously and used for several distinct jobs — coordinating, managing coupling, anticipating, assisting. Dourish & Bellotti add the delivery rule: the information must be generated and consumed *passively, through the shared workspace*, because separate active mechanisms penalise the provider — which is also the escape from Grudin's disparity, since a by-product of working costs its provider nothing. Together they answer Q1: presence is not one pattern but the visible face of an awareness layer with graded elements, and the grading practice ships (avatar stack, live cursor, selection, trail) matches the element structure the framework names. The design decision the sequence should carry is which elements a surface's coupling actually needs — the framework explicitly declines a prescriptive ordering.
Papers: Gutwin & Greenberg 2002; Dourish & Bellotti 1992; Grudin 1994.

### 2. Visibility → awareness → accountability is a consequence chain, not a staging; its accountability face must be separately earned

Erickson & Kellogg's three properties all flow from one design act — making socially significant activity visible — and the paper warns that the chain's links, bundled in physical space, "are not necessarily coupled in the digital realm". For Q2 this means the sequence gets one *making-activity-visible* decision whose consequences ripple, not three ordered steps; and it inherits the constitutive tension — translucence, not transparence, because "what we say and do with another person depends on who, and how many, are watching". The surveillance cost of visibility is not a side effect to mitigate later but part of the same decision.
Papers: Erickson & Kellogg 2000.

### 3. Synchrony is a rate, set by the coupling of the work, over one set of mechanisms

Three independent sources refuse the sync/async split as a structural boundary. Olson & Olson key synchrony to coupling of the task, which varies within one product. Gutwin & Greenberg's mixed-focus finding puts the shift between individual and shared work *inside a single session*. Kleppmann et al. show real-time and asynchronous collaboration as batch sizes over one change substrate — a keystroke or a pull request. Ellis/Gibbs/Rein's matrix, the one candidate for a split, classifies systems rather than ordering decisions. Answer to Q3: one spine, walked at tempos the work's coupling sets per surface and per moment; the sequence should not fork on synchrony, and steps should state how they degrade across tempo (a live cursor becomes a trail) rather than duplicating per tempo.
Papers: Olson & Olson 2000; Gutwin & Greenberg 2002; Kleppmann et al. 2019; Ellis et al. 1991.

### 4. Conflict is handled in three grades: prevented by awareness, absorbed by merge, surfaced only at deliberate divergence

The convergence runs from 1987 to 2026. Relaxing WYSIWIS created the possibility of divergent views and with it the awareness debt (Stefik et al.); awareness plus social protocol prevents most collisions (the notebooks study; the time-position study finds conflicts rare and localised because people self-partition); the merge substrate absorbs concurrent edits silently, with the sole irresolvable case being concurrent update of the same property (Kleppmann et al.); and what cannot be absorbed should be kept as a visible, first-class thing for a person to settle (the semantic conflict model's argument against opaque implicit resolution; Evernote's conflicted copy as the documented failure of surfacing without support). This matches the practice survey exactly: live surfaces never show conflict UI, and conflict appears only where divergence was deliberately permitted — the fork boundary — where it blocks reintegration until a person picks. Answer to Q4: the sequence's conflict step is not "choose a merge algorithm" but "decide where divergence is permitted, and give its return a surface"; the merge model below it is substrate.
Papers: Stefik et al. 1987; Kleppmann et al. 2019; CHI 2024 notebooks study; 2020 time-position study; 2026 semantic conflict model; with Dourish & Bellotti on the prevention grade.

### 5. The grant precedes; the private→public transition is a move inside an already-granted space

The literature contributes the frame (WYSIWIS's subgroup-population dimension — who is in the "we" at all — and Erickson & Kellogg's privacy/visibility tension), and the practice survey supplies the staging evidence directly: every surveyed product requires an explicit, graded grant before any other collaboration mechanism exists, and none grows sharing implicitly out of private work. What *does* grow incrementally is visibility within the granted space — the draft state, the suggestion mode, the branch — which is a different decision taken later and repeatedly. Answer to Q5: the grant heads the sequence; incremental-formalisation-style growth applies to the work's public face, not to the grant itself.
Papers: Stefik et al. 1987; Erickson & Kellogg 2000; with the practice survey carrying the staging evidence.

## What this challenges

- The map entry's "current extent: annotation → commenting → status-feedback" reads the contribution channel as the sequence's core. Both the literature and practice put two regions before it: the grant, and the awareness layer — the existing chain is the middle of the walk, not its opening.
- "Presence" as one missing pattern under-describes the gap: the evidence gives a graded awareness layer (several marks at different intensities plus an after-the-fact trail) whose elements are selected per surface — closer to how indication resolved in attention and tempo than to a single new pattern.
- "Concurrent conflict" as a missing *pattern* may also be mis-shaped: the evidenced design decision is where divergence is permitted and how its return is surfaced, with prevention (awareness) and absorption (merge substrate) doing most of the work. A conflict-resolution surface divorced from a fork boundary has no evidenced home.
