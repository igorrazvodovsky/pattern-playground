# Research: searching as a seeking move — 2026-08-27

Query: whether the "query in, result set out" model survives the evidence, what the search/browse boundary actually rests on, what query-formulation support does to the actor, and what changes when the system composes an answer instead of retrieving one. Run while maturing the `searching` seed.

> *Retrieval provenance*. Fan-out web search across five angles, 25 sources fetched, 118 claims extracted, 25 carried into three-vote adversarial verification, 8 survived. Verification was harsh by design: eight claims were killed, several on framing rather than on fact, so material from killed claims survives inside the evidence of neighbouring findings and is marked below as context rather than as finding. Two verifiers exhausted their search budget before running contradiction sweeps — for the Furnas claims and the autocomplete claims, "no contradiction found" means "not searched". Practice evidence is in the sibling note `2026-08-27-practice.md`, not folded in here.

## Context

*Topic*: searching as a seeking move — an actor expresses intent as a query and the system does the evaluation and discrimination.

*Interaction*: an actor wants something out of a collection they cannot or will not walk through by hand. They express intent as a query; the system returns an ordered set, or increasingly a composed answer, rather than the collection's own structure.

*Setting*: any surface holding more content than an actor can browse — application search, document and file systems, catalogues, and now generative search and assistant surfaces where the query is answered rather than matched.

*Questions driving retrieval*: the seven in `query.yml`.

## Findings

### 1. The one-shot model fails for exploratory search, not for search in general

This is the finding that decides the pattern's shape, and the honest verdict is narrower than "the model fails".

Exploratory search is defined by an information need that is unclear at the outset and changes during the session. Palagi, Gandon, Giboin & Troncy, *A Survey of Definitions and Models of Exploratory Search* (ESIDA'17 @ IUI), Table 2 #1, "An evolving search process": "The user adopts an opportunistic behavior, and will change or specify the objective or goals of search or even the strategies used to achieve them through multiple queries reformulation or refinement. During the search, the user can accomplish forward or backward steps."

The same paper rejects the result set as the evaluative object for exploratory search in its own voice: precision and recall "suppose a precisely identified search target and result set. They are not sufficient."

But it also preserves lookup as a real pole — "focused searches where the user has a specific goal in mind and also an idea of the expected result" — and rejects the dichotomy: "Many papers use this dichotomy to define ES… However, we believe that there is a continuum/spectrum between these two extremes, because we can find some lookup activities in an ES session."

The empirical weight sits one hop downstream. Athukorala, Głowacka, Jacucci, Oulasvirta & Vreeken, *Is exploratory search different?* (JASIST 2016, 67(11), 2635–2651, doi:10.1002/asi.23617) build a classifier that separates core exploratory from core lookup at 85% accuracy but degrades to 60.3% once borderline tasks are included. The blurred boundary is measured, not asserted.

*Limits*: Palagi is a workshop synthesis over a small reference base and reports no new user data.

### 2. The classic anchors are still live, not superseded

Shah, *Rethinking Human-AI Collaboration in Information Seeking* (CHIIR '26, pp. 151–161) builds its human-side model on "epistemic orientation (learning vs. pattern reproduction), temporal structure (processual vs. instantaneous), and agentic purpose (uncertainty resolution vs. task execution)", with Bates 1989, Kuhlthau's ISP, Dervin, Marchionini 2006, Pirolli 2009, White & Roth 2009 and Rieh et al. 2016 in its 54 references.

*Limits*: an argumentative perspective paper with no new data, and its only indexed citing work shares its first author. Contradiction sweeps found work *qualifying* berrypicking and the ISP but nothing claiming supersession — arguing with a model treats it as live.

Teevan et al.'s orienteering/teleporting and Pirolli & Card's information scent produced no surviving claim in either direction in this corpus. Not established either way here; do not write that they are dormant.

### 3. Query formulation is costly because naming is unreliable

Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais, *The vocabulary problem in human-system communication* (CACM 30(11):964–971, 1987): "We studied spontaneous word choice for objects in five application-related domains, and found the variability to be surprisingly large. In every case two people favored the same term with probability <0.20."

*Scope*: the p<0.20 figure is measured. The paper's better-known "80-90 percent failure rates" is a *simulation* projected from those distributions, modelling single-exact-keyword access — stemming, query expansion and embedding retrieval have since blunted exactly that failure mode. Cite Furnas for the naming gap between actor and designer, which nothing later overturns; do not cite it as evidence that modern search fails 80–90% of the time.

### 4. The search/browse hybrid wins for exploratory tasks, and one specific rule reduces dead ends

Yee, Swearingen, Li & Hearst, *Faceted Metadata for Image Search and Browsing* (CHI 2003). Within-subjects, counterbalanced, 32 art-history students, ~35,000-image fine arts collection, interfaces given neutral names.

The design rule, verbatim: "We do this by keeping results organized, by sticking to simple point-and-click interactions instead of imposing any special query syntax on the user, and by not showing any links that would lead to zero results. Every hyperlink that selects a new result set is displayed with a query preview (an indicator of the number of results to expect)."

Behavioural effect: "during the structured tasks participants actually did receive empty results in Baseline 82 times, while in FC, they received empty results only 26 times." Preference: "88% said that FC was more useful for the types of searching they usually do and 91% said they preferred FC to Baseline overall", with significance at p<.001 for easy to use, easy to browse, flexible, interesting, enjoyable.

*Split evidence on dead ends*: the 82-vs-26 count carries no significance test, and the only statistically tested dead-end measure was null (t(62)=1.41, p=.163; t(62)=.499, p=.619). The post-test comparative judgement was strongly asymmetric (28 vs 3). Attributing the whole difference to the zero-result rule overreaches — the faceted interface also differed on facets, matrix, grouping and keyword disambiguation.

*Costs*: the faceted interface was significantly slower (12m06s vs 9m30s on Task 2). On the one simple single-facet task ("find images of roses") preference split roughly evenly — which supports the "for exploratory tasks" scoping rather than undercutting it.

*Limits*: single study, n=32, one collection, one population, authors evaluating their own system, mostly subjective measures; an order effect inflated one arm (t(26)=2.67, p<.01). The Hearst book chapter on the same material is self-citation, not independent confirmation.

### 5. Faceted overviews buy subjective gains without demonstrated outcome gains, and bias what gets seen

Kules & Shneiderman, *Users can change their web search tactics* (Information Processing and Management, doi:10.1016/j.ipm.2007.07.014). 24 experienced searchers, 2×2 within-subjects, four ~12-minute exploratory web tasks.

Subjective wins: "helped me organize my search results" 7.4 vs 4.9 on a 9-point scale, F(1,88)=42.11, p<0.001. Outcome null: "A Wilcoxon rank sum test did not detect a significant difference in Idea Quality by System."

*Do not compress this to "facets feel better but don't help."* The paper's own scope sentence is "They do not show objective differences in *outcomes*" — significant objective *behavioural* differences were found: depth of results viewed 28.4 vs 22.3, F(1,919)=8.96, p<0.01; queries per search 3.0 vs 3.5, F(1,87)=7.15, p<0.01. The abstract's headline is "subjects explored deeper while feeling more organized". The null is also weak on its own terms — floor-bound ratings the authors blame on the time limit, idea quality "assessed blind by a single researcher" with no inter-rater reliability.

*Bias finding*: searchers collected a significantly higher proportion of categorised pages when the overview was present, 82.7% vs 75.4%, χ²(1, N=679)=5.11, p<.05. The authors' own word is bias. Three qualifications: topic beat system on the same measure (χ²=18.00, p<.001); the test pseudoreplicates 679 pages nested in 24 participants; and the stated mechanism is *structural availability* — the tree only surfaces categorised pages — with the remedy being "clearer indications to searchers that their results are being filtered", not better labels.

Comprehension trouble is separately documented: 19/24 participants commented on categorisation problems, though this is a prompted rate since the interview asked about problems.

*Limits*: single lab study, n=24, 2007, ODP/DMOZ taxonomy (shut down 2017). "Additional studies are needed to confirm these results."

### 6. Query-formulation support is not a neutral affordance

Suggestion ranking measurably changes which query gets issued. Cai & de Rijke, *A Survey of Query Auto Completion in Information Retrieval* (Foundations and Trends in IR 10(4), 2016), §7.2: "A strong position bias towards examining and using top-ranked query completions is identified, which is consistent with similar findings on user interactions with web search results… Due to this strong position bias, ranking quality does affect QAC usage." The underlying study is Hofmann, Mitra, Radlinski & Shokouhi, CIKM '14, pp. 549–558.

*Scope*: the CIKM study varied ranking quality within-subject. It did **not** compare autocomplete present vs absent, so "autocomplete changes task outcomes versus no autocomplete" is not licensed. A stronger framing — that selection is driven by position *rather than* quality — was voted down as contradicting the finding. Position bias survives; quality-irrelevance does not.

Yamamoto & Yamamoto, *Query Priming for Promoting Critical Thinking in Web Search* (CHIIR '18, pp. 12–21) deliberately loaded autocomplete and query suggestion with critical-thinking terms: "(1) With query priming, participants issued more queries and (re-)visited search engine result pages more frequently. (2) Query priming promoted webpage selection targeted at evidence-based decision making. (3) The query priming effect varied relative to participant educational background."

Findings (1) and (2) are measured outside the query box, which earns "beyond the words in the box". This is the constructive mirror of the anchoring worry — a demonstration that suggestion surfaces steer, not a measurement of harm.

*Limits*: single crowdsourced study, terms engineered rather than neutral, effect moderated by education. Adjacent nulls cut against generalising: Gwizdka et al. (Hypertext 2020) could not confirm that "People also ask" suggestions mitigate confirmation bias, and two CHIIR studies (2024, 2026) found no significant behavioural or correctness effect.

### 7. When the system composes an answer, the result set stops being the object — as theory, not yet as measurement

Wilson, *Modelling the impact of generative artificial intelligence on information behaviour research* (Journal of Documentation 82(7), 2026, pp. 376–395): "Existing models of information behaviour assume interaction with passive retrieval systems such as search engines and bibliographic databases. Generative AI systems, which actively generate personalised responses rather than retrieving existing documents, represent a fundamental shift that these models do not address."

Wilson renames a component of his own model: "the term 'information discovery' used in the original model is replaced here by 'information interaction,' which better reflects the active and generative character of AI systems." He names six characteristics requiring theoretical accommodation: "opacity, epistemic authority, sycophancy risk, personalisation, first-person voice construction and commercial optimisation."

*Handling*: cite both Wilson and Shah as attributed positions, never as evidence. Wilson's method is "conceptual analysis grounded in a scoping review of 89 papers" — no original data. Wilson's own paper records complementary rather than replacement use, citing Hersh (2024) that bibliographic databases remain valuable for academics' critical needs.

### 8. When a machine formulates the queries, sessions iterate more but still narrow

Ning, Coelho, Kong, Long, Martins, Magalhães, Callan & Xiong, *Agentic Search in the Wild* (SIGIR '26). 14.44M requests / 3.97M sessions from DeepResearchGym.

"Nearly half (47.77%) are single-query sessions… Among multi-turn sessions, 90% have length ≤10." Across 339k labelled adjacent query pairs: Specialization 21.76%, Generalization 9.02%, Exploration 37.07%, Repetition 32.15%, with Generalization under 11% for every intent type. Conclusion: "Across trajectories, agents show a drill-down bias, favoring local refinement and facet pivots over deliberate broadening or backtracking."

*Three fixes that must travel*: (1) the human baseline quoted alongside is not the authors' measurement — the "77.6% single-query" figure is a 1998 AltaVista log, and its own paper reports a mean of 2.02 queries/session, contradicting the 1.7 quoted beside it. (2) Do not write that this is "the opposite of what exploratory-search accounts prescribe" — those accounts are descriptive, and the paper disclaims the normative read: "These patterns should not be read as direct evidence of agent success or failure." (3) "Narrowing-biased" as a headline is looser than the paper's framing: Exploration is the largest bucket at 37% with ~7% result overlap, i.e. genuine pivoting.

*Limits*: single non-commercial sandbox log, agent population uncharacterised, labels assigned by an LLM and validated only by inter-model agreement with no human gold standard, no success labels.

## What this corpus does not establish

- *Search failure recovery.* No surviving evidence on zero-result rates in shipping products, on what actors do next, or on whether spelling correction, "did you mean", relaxed matching and partial matches help or merely mask. Flamenco's never-render-a-zero-result rule is the only grounded design response here.
- *The searching / filtering / prompting boundary.* The one claim addressing it was refuted 0–3. This is the question most directly relevant to placing Searching among its neighbours in the library, and pass 1 left it unanswered.
- *Generative search and human behaviour.* The agentic finding measures machine clients, not people. Missing entirely: verifiability evaluation, any measurement of whether composed answers narrow exploration or suppress source-checking.
- *Whether formulation support helps or harms on balance.* The evidence shows only that it steers. No surviving source tested autocomplete present vs absent.

## Citation traps

- "Lookup tasks are embedded in exploratory tasks and vice versa" must be cited to Athukorala et al. 2016, not to Marchionini. Marchionini's own embedding is asymmetric (lookup inside learn/investigate); the symmetric wording is Athukorala's. The 2×2 of complexity × goal specificity is also Athukorala's.
- Belkin's anomalous state of knowledge must not be cited off Shah 2026 — it is not in that paper's reference list. Palagi does cite it.
- Furnas's 80–90% is a simulation of single-keyword access, not a measured modern failure rate.
- Kules & Shneiderman's facet bias has a *structural availability* mechanism. Reading it as "the vocabulary problem relocated from the query box to the facet labels" is a defensible synthesis but is not the paper's mechanism, and must be marked as interpretation.
- The Hearst book chapter on faceted navigation is self-citation of the Flamenco study, not independent confirmation.

## Literature

### 1. Term suggestions get used without a measured benefit

*Type:* measured finding — and the only controlled outcome measure in the entire pre-AI corpus.

*Source:* Anick 2003, Prisma on AltaVista, as reported in Hearst, *Search User Interfaces*, Cambridge UP, 2009.

*Grounding:* Bucket test, 15,133 sessions / 8,006 users (Prisma) against 14,595 sessions / 7,857 users (control). Refinement occurred in 56% of Prisma sessions versus 53% of control — significant. Effectiveness measured as result clicks *did not differ* from control. Refinement-type breakdown 25 / 24 / 15 / 12 / 24. Hearst's sentence is "In the Prisma condition, of those sessions containing refinements" — the 25 / 16 / 47 / 38 figures are shares *of sessions containing refinements*, not of all sessions.

*Limits:* One 2003 web-search bucket test, AltaVista, session boundary defined by a 60-minute gap. Uptake rose; the outcome did not move. Second-hand via Hearst; primary methods unread.

### 2. Not every refinement situation calls for conversation

*Type:* theoretical proposal. No study, no system, no participants.

*Source:* Radlinski & Craswell, "A Theoretical Framework for Conversational Search", CHIIR '17, pp. 117–126.

*Grounding:* §3.2, verbatim: the simplest case "clearly does not call for a back-and-forth with a search agent"; and of memoryless refinement, "In such a setting a more complex model may in fact reduce user utility and does not call for conversational approaches to search."

*Limits:* An assertion by the authors, falsifiable and unfalsified — nobody in this source set has tested it. The paper argues sufficiency by walking three worked examples (email search, personal information search, product recommendation).

### 3. The risk in a composed answer sits in the model's stance, not in composition

*Type:* measured finding.

*Source:* Sharma, Liao & Xiao, CHI '24 (arXiv:2402.05880v2).

*Grounding:* Study 1, N = 115, three between-subjects conditions, three controversial topics, closed-corpus RAG over gpt-4-32k-0613, essay-writing task. With a *neutral* composed-answer system, asking narrowed but output did not. Confirmatory Query — the percentage-point gap between attitude-consistent and attitude-inconsistent *queries* — was WebSearch M = 1.46%, ConvSearch M = 15.00%, ConvSearchRef M = 16.15%; F(2,100) = 4.71, p = 0.01; D = 0.76 and 0.60 against WebSearch. Confirmatory Argument in the resulting essays was flat: F(2,100) = 0.002, p = 0.998. The authors: "despite participants' more confirmatory querying with conversational search, the neutral systems still provided relatively balanced information." Stated attitudes did not move (p = 0.60). Downstream judgement of articles shown afterwards did shift — agreement p = 0.002, trust p = 0.03.

With an *opinionated* system agreeing with the actor, both narrowed. Study 2 Confirmatory Query: Consonant 42.92%, Neutral 15.57%, Dissonant 12.33%; F(2,210) = 31.24, p < 0.001, D ≈ 1.0–1.2. Essays: Consonant 51.69%, Neutral 34.79%, Dissonant 15.58%, omnibus p < 0.001 — but *no pairwise comparison reached conventional significance* (Consonant vs Neutral p = 0.09, D = 0.7; Neutral vs Dissonant p = 0.05, D = 0.36). Report the omnibus. A counter-opinionated system did not repair the bias: "we found no evidence supporting H1.b", with the authors' own exception that essays did come out more balanced.

*Limits:* Nothing here is a coverage or breadth measure. Confirmatory Query is what the actor *asks for*; the closest proxy for what they encountered is Confirmatory Argument in the essays. The task *instructed* three queries (observed means 3.40 in Study 1, 3.69 in Study 2), so the percentage is computed over roughly three queries per person and is coarsely quantised. Construct validity across conditions is unresolved: the paper notes conversational queries were "more verbose and expressive" than keyword queries, so an unknown share of the WebSearch-vs-ConvSearch gap may be instrument rather than behaviour; Cohen's Kappa of 0.91 shows two coders agreed with each other, not that the measure means the same thing in both conditions. Closed-world corpus, three US political topics, one essay task, ~20-minute sessions, US Prolific. Two studies from one team.

*Disagreement to carry, in the paper itself:* abstract and introduction say Study 2 N = 213; §5.5 and Table 2 say 223 (Consonant 73, Neutral 75, Dissonant 75); all Study 2 ANCOVAs report df = (2, 210). The Neutral cell is carried over from Study 1's ConvSearch and ConvSearchRef participants with 148 newly recruited — so Neutral is a *non-concurrent* control, and that carry-over sits beside the same paragraph's statement that "Participants in Study 1 were not allowed to participate in Study 2."

### 4. Citation recall and precision fail separably, and were a product decision

*Type:* measured finding (annotation audit; no users involved).

*Source:* Liu, Zhang & Liang (arXiv:2304.09848v2).

*Grounding:* Four commercial generative search engines — Bing Chat, NeevaAI, perplexity.ai, YouChat — responses scraped between late February and late March 2023; 1450 queries each across 12 distributions; single-turn first responses. Averaged across the four, 51.5% of generated statements were fully supported by their citations (recall) and 74.5% of citations supported their associated statement (precision). Per system, recall: perplexity.ai 68.7, NeevaAI 67.6, Bing Chat 58.7, YouChat 11.1 — a spread of about 58 points. Precision: 89.5 / 72.7 / 72.0 / 63.6 — about 25 points. Bing Chat had the highest precision and, YouChat aside, the lowest recall. Definitions verbatim from §2.4: "Citation precision is the proportion of generated citations that support their associated statements". On scope: "verifiability is not factuality — rather than arbitrating if a generated statement is true... verifiability enables users to easily check any generated statement's source." On granularity: "we use sentences for simplicity and reproducibility."

*Limits:* The 51.5% and 74.5% are unweighted means of four system means (51.525 and 74.45) and describe no system that existed; set YouChat's 11.1 aside and the other three sit around 65% recall. Not like-for-like: NeevaAI declined 22.7% of queries and scores are computed only over answered ones. Each query-response pair was annotated once, by one of 34 screened MTurk workers; the agreement figures (>82.0% pairwise, 91.0 F1) come from a 250-pair subsample, are not chance-corrected (no kappa), and F1 is scored against majority consensus. Recall's denominator is itself a single-annotator judgement, since annotators first removed statements they deemed not verification-worthy. Date-stamp is mandatory: nothing in this corpus measures citation quality after March 2023, and two of the four products no longer exist under these names.

*Not a finding:* the r = −0.96 inverse correlation between precision and perceived utility. The paper reports no n, no p-value, and names no correlation type; the only units are the four systems. It is a descriptive observation across four contemporaneous 2023 products. The copying mechanism proposed for it is the authors' hypothesis.

### 5. Dissatisfaction routes out of search; only doubt routes out of chat

*Type:* qualitative observation with a proposed mechanism.

*Source:* Mayerhofer, Capra & Elsweiler, CHIIR '25.

*Grounding:* Eight participants switched web→chat twelve times on dissatisfaction. Nobody switched chat→web on dissatisfaction, because "the chat interface always provided responses that answered the participant's question". Some returned to WebSearch to verify.

*Limits:* 88 sessions, 22 German-speaking participants at one university, snowball sample, average age 29, well-educated, four health tasks, one interface, one chat model with no source attribution by design. The structural reason offered — a chat has no natural failure signal — is the authors' interpretation of an observed asymmetry in a single study, not a measured mechanism. It would be directly affected by whether the chat cites sources, expresses uncertainty, or can return nothing, none of which this configuration did.

### 6. Two labelled input surfaces got two registers

*Type:* qualitative observation.

*Source:* Mayerhofer, Capra & Elsweiler, CHIIR '25.

*Grounding:* Two participants explicitly stated the keywords-in-WebSearch / full-questions-in-ChatAI preference, and seven more in 12 instances followed the pattern without describing it. Query reformulation (C12 vary) was "used to optimise web search results" while "the chat interface primarily received entirely new questions to address." Roughly nine participants used keyword input on the search side and question input on the chat side, and refined queries only on the search side.

*Limits:* The interface was *two panes* — a search box labelled WebSearch on the left, a chat labelled ChatAI on the right, distinct input widgets. The register separation is confounded with an interface that presented two distinctly labelled affordances and invited a choice. Defensible statement: *when two labelled input surfaces are placed side by side, actors used them in distinct registers.* Krippendorff's α = 0.63 before a single researcher annotated the full set; the authors call their qualitative patterns "initial observations rather than definitive conclusions."

### 7. Blending the two surfaces correlated with the worst outcomes

*Type:* measured finding (association, not manipulation).

*Source:* Mayerhofer, Capra & Elsweiler, CHIIR '25, Figure 5 and Table 3.

*Grounding:* Web-only sessions yielded 77% correct (20/26), chat-only 76% (13/17), using both 47% (21/45); χ²(2, N = 88) = 8.39, p = 0.015. Table 3 in task order: T1 web 27 / chat 14 / both 59; T2 32 / 32 / 36; T3 36 / 27 / 36; T4 23 / 5 / 73. A quarter to a third of sessions never touched the chat.

*Limits:* Association, and the authors partly explain it by task difficulty — harder tasks drew both more blending and more errors. This cuts against a blending thesis and belongs beside any claim that actors combine the two productively.

*Related, same study:* on confidence, T1 correctness fell 64% → 36% while confidence rose — but the paper flags this as "consistent with prior work that has found that searching increases post-task confidence regardless of task success [8]" (Bink et al.), so it is a replication, not this study's finding. On verification, Pearson r = 0.03 across 88 sessions; the defensible wording is the authors' own, "we found no evidence that more extensive verification increased the likelihood of reaching the correct answer" — not "verification did not help", and the comparison is confounded by task difficulty. An ANOVA on confidence gives F(2,85) = 3.74, p < 0.05; Tukey found chat-only (M = 3.47) versus both (M = 2.2) significant at p < 0.05, while chat-only versus websearch-only (M = 2.46) did not reach significance at p = 0.12 — one of two contrasts.

*Disagreement to carry, in the paper itself:* "None of the participants asked the chat for sources" is verbatim, but the same paper describes one participant asking the chat for studies supporting its claim, in the single chat-only SU25 Consensus case, and calls it "successful in the single instance it was used."

### 8. An optional verification affordance went largely unused in a low-stakes task

*Type:* descriptive observation, plus a measured null.

*Source:* Sharma, Liao & Xiao, CHI '24.

*Grounding:* "participants clicked less than one reference per search session (M = 0.43, SD = 1.13)." The null is the stronger result: ConvSearchRef never differed from ConvSearch on any hypothesis in either study, and Study 2 aggregated over interface for that reason — "Showing references in the conversational search had no effect."

*Limits:* The 0.43 figure sits in Discussion §7.3 with no stated N and no study attribution, following "both of our studies showed that including references had a very limited impact." Cite it without assigning an N. The authors' hedge belongs with the claim: "participants did not necessarily perform a high-stake task in our study."

*Do not compare 0.43 to 4.48 without saying why they differ.* The §7.1 asymmetries are descriptive with no test reported: WebSearch link clicks 4.48 (SD 4.27) consonant versus 3.28 (SD 3.11) dissonant; reading time 116.6s (SD 177.62) versus 78.66s (SD 111.90); thumbs-up 0.29 versus 0.21. In web search, clicking a link is the only route to any content at all; in a composed-answer interface the answer is already complete and the reference is optional verification on top. The 4.48 is consonant-clicks only (consonant plus dissonant ≈ 7.76). The comparison is confounded by task necessity.

### 9. Interaction shifts attitudes more than exposure does

*Type:* measured finding.

*Source:* Williams-Ceci et al., "Biased AI writing assistants shift users' attitudes on societal issues", *Science Advances* 12(11), 13 March 2026, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adw5578.

*Grounding:* Two preregistered experiments, N = 2,582 total, Prolific, Cornell IRB #0010155. Exp 1 (N = 1,485): Control 499 / AI Treatment 487 / Static Text 499, topic standardized testing. Exp 2 (N = 1,097): Control 312 / AI Treatment 219 / Warning 200 / Debrief 159 / Write-in-favor 207, four issues, pre-task attitudes measured "several weeks before participation." Attitude shift toward the assistant's position: d = 0.24 (Exp 1), d = 0.19 (Exp 2), within-subject d = 0.27. AI Treatment exceeded Static Text (d = 0.13, p = .040) while Static Text versus Control did not reach significance after Tukey adjustment (d = 0.11, p = .091). Warnings and debriefs did not reduce the shift (both p = 1.000 against untreated AI Treatment). The omnibus reads "F(2, 1474) = 10.244, p < .001, 𝑛𝑝2 = 0.01."

*Limits:* "autocomplete" here means inline continuation of the author's own prose, in a Gmail/Docs framing. There is no search-box or query condition anywhere in the paper — the published title dropped the word. Small effects, replicated.

*Awareness, stated correctly:* 73% of treated participants *whose attitude shifted toward the AI* agreed the suggestions were balanced and only 4% disagreed, versus 63% and 19% among treated participants whose attitudes did not shift. Awareness did not differ between the influenced and the uninfluenced: χ²(2, N = 219) = 1.29, p = .524. Self-report on whether suggestions influenced their thinking: 53% disagreed / 31% agreed (Exp 1), 51% / 34% (Exp 2) — roughly half, not "generally".

### 10. An opinionated writing assistant moves what people write and, a little, what they think

*Type:* measured finding.

*Source:* Jakesch et al., "Co-Writing with Opinionated Language Models Affects Users' Views", CHI 2023 (arXiv:2302.00560, DOI 10.1145/3544548.3581196).

*Grounding:* N = 1,506 post-exclusion (507 control / 508 techno-optimist / 491 techno-pessimist), Prolific, US, one topic (social media), one GPT-3 sentence-completion writing assistant, one session. Treated participants argued the model's side about 2× as often as control (2.04×, 95% CI [1.83, 2.30]; 2.0×, [1.79, 2.24]). Attitude survey afterwards shifted with it: 35% → 45% saying social media is good, d = 0.22; the mirror condition d = 0.19.

*Limits:* The 0.38 and 0.20 / d = 0.34 figures come from the analysis of opinion expressed *in the writing*, not the attitude survey — and the paper says outright that "accepting suggestions out of convenience has contributed to the differences in the written opinion." So convenience contributed; a smaller effect (0.20 versus 0.38) persisted among the slowest writers. The paper never tests search and never names query autocomplete; its own limitation warns that "a predictive keyboard that only suggests single words" may behave differently. Its single "search engine" sentence is about monetisation.

### 11. Query suggestions in the lab: used about a third of the time, faster on known-item tasks

*Type:* uptake measure plus one measured speed finding.

*Source:* White et al., SIGIR '07, 36 participants, as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* Query suggestions were used 35.7% of the time and were faster than baseline on known-item tasks. Query-trail destination suggestions were used 35.2% of the time *on exploratory tasks*.

*Limits:* Lab, n = 36, 2007. The 35.2% is task-type-specific and should not be stated bare. Second-hand via Hearst.

### 12. Production uptake of suggestion features is low

*Type:* uptake measure. Usage is not usefulness.

*Sources:* Anick & Kantamneni 2008 (Yahoo Search Assist); Jansen et al. 2007b (Dogpile); Lin et al. 2008 (PubMed) — all as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* Yahoo Search Assist, about 6%. Dogpile, 8.4% of all queries. A separate Dogpile figure, 22.2%, is the share of query-modification *actions* within reformulation sessions — a third denominator, neither users nor queries. Related-article links were clicked in 18.5% of PubMed sessions (one week, June 2007, ~2M sessions).

*Limits:* All uptake, no benefit. Hearst attaches her own scope limit to the PubMed figure: "it is unclear what its utility is for other kinds of search." Note that Hearst calls the 8.4% "additional evidence that query term refinement suggestions are a useful reformulation feature" — which does not follow from a usage rate.

### 13. Roughly half of search sessions in mid-2000s web logs involved query reformulation

*Type:* descriptive observation from logs.

*Sources:* Jansen et al. 2005 and Jansen et al. 2007b, as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* Jansen 2005 — 3M records, 24-hour AltaVista snapshot, 2002: 52% of users modified queries, 32% issued three or more. Jansen 2007b — 2.5M interactions, Dogpile, 2005: 46%+ modified, 37% of queries part of reformulations, 29.4% of sessions with three or more. Direction breakdown (Jansen 2007b): reformulation 22.7, generalization 7.2, specialization 16.3, content change 11.8, plus 9.9 and 9.8 for the "with reformulation" variants.

*Limits:* Session boundaries are heuristic and differ between studies — Jansen's inactivity split (justified by 72% of sessions running under five minutes, which is Jansen 2005's own parenthetical, not a caveat Hearst attaches to her cross-study "roughly 50%"); Anick's 60-minute gap. The two studies are not commensurable. The whole corpus predates mobile-dominant search, instant results, and answer-engine result pages.

### 14. Actors get anchored on their original phrasing

*Type:* qualitative observation.

*Sources:* Hertzum & Frøkjær 1996; Russell 2006 (a presentation) — as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* Russell's eight near-identical Harry Potter queries, reproduced as the instance.

*Limits:* Qualitative; the "thrashing" session is one observed example, from a presentation rather than a paper.

### 15. Misspelling is common; acting on a correction is not reliably observed

*Type:* descriptive observation, with the sources in open disagreement.

*Sources:* Cucerzan & Brill, EMNLP '04; Hargittai 2006; Russell 2006 — all as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* 10–15% of queries contain spelling or typographical errors (Cucerzan & Brill). In one 2006 in-person study of 100 participants, 63% made a spelling or typographical mistake; of the 37 who erred while using Google, *none* clicked the spelling-correction link (Hargittai). Russell 2006 gives 33% uptake for two-word reformulations and 5% for three-word.

*Limits:* The 10–15% is a cause of failure, not a failure rate — misspelled queries often still return results; web-log era, pre-2004. Hargittai is 2006-vintage Google; drop any "statistically representative pool" descriptor, which is Hearst's and names no population it is representative *of*.

*Disagreement to carry:* Hearst states outright, "There are no published large-scale statistics on user uptake of spelling correction." Report Russell's 33% / 5% and Hargittai's zero of 37 side by side; do not average them.

### 16. Classic relevance feedback works offline and is very rare in deployed interfaces

*Type:* synthesis about deployment, not a measured failure.

*Source:* Hearst 2009, synthesising Croft et al. 2001 and Ruthven & Lalmas 2003.

*Grounding:* The cleanest negative in the pre-AI corpus.

*Limits:* It is a synthesis about *deployment*, and "has not been proven successful" is absence-of-evidence phrasing rather than a measured failure.

### 17. Making actors pick from many terms before seeing results costs time and cognitive load

*Type:* measured finding, mixed.

*Source:* Bruza et al., SIGIR '00, 54 participants, as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* An interface showing upwards of 40 terms and withholding the results listing until selection produced *higher average precision* than the web search engine, but slower response and higher cognitive load on a distractor task.

*Limits:* Mixed, not negative. Hearst explicitly attributes such negatives to presentation rather than mechanism: "Some studies have produced negative results, but they seem to stem from problems with the presentation interface." Her supporting design rule — show results immediately, single-term hyperlink refinement fares better — survives as her stated principle, which makes it a recommendation, not a measurement.

### 18. Faceted browsing was strongly preferred despite being much slower

*Type:* measured finding on *self-reported preference*.

*Source:* Yee et al., CHI '03, as reported in Hearst 2009.

*Grounding:* 32 art history students, Flamenco versus a Google-Images-type baseline over a fine arts museum image collection of about 35,000 items, four tasks each, order balanced. Verbatim: "For finding images of roses (a simple, single-facet task), about 50% preferred the baseline. However, for every other type of searching, the Flamenco interface was preferred: 88% said that Flamenco was more useful for the types of searching they usually do and 91% said they preferred Flamenco to the baseline overall." Faceted won while being "an order of magnitude slower".

*Limits:* Three that belong beside the number, not in a footnote — these are self-reported preference, not task performance; Hearst is a co-author of the Flamenco work she is reporting, so this is not an independent evaluation; and Hearst herself records that "the order in which interfaces were viewed had a strong effect on these ratings".

### 19. Judging an autocomplete suffix without its prefix misses a large share of problematic pairs

*Type:* measured finding about *assessment practice*, in synthetic scenarios.

*Source:* Olteanu, Diaz & Kazai, "When Are Search Completion Suggestions Problematic?", CSCW 2020, DOI 10.1145/3415242.

*Grounding:* Across the constructed samples, up to 26.1% of pairs judged problematic (35.6% target / 47.7% neutral / 15.4% problematic / 29% baseline) would have been missed if the suffix were judged without its prefix. Rare prefixes ("search voids") were up to 3× more likely to be linked to problematic suffixes in these synthetic scenarios. The three-dimensional taxonomy (context / content / target), six content-category definitions and seven target categories survive intact and are the part a design page can use.

*Limits:* The suggestion scenarios are synthetic, not observed. §4.2 is titled "Generating Synthetic Search Completion Suggestion Scenarios". The chain: purposive samples (likely-problematic / target / neutral / baseline, each built by matching hand-chosen key phrases) from production Bing queries of February 2019 → queries restricted to 4–20 terms, issued ≥5 times (15 for baseline) → prefixes fixed at exactly 3 terms and kept only when they had over 45 unique suffixes → suggestion lists synthesised by the authors, with half drawn from the *least common* suffixes for each prefix → judged by third-party crowd workers seeing 6 suggestions at a time → "problematic if at least one judge marked it as such." The authors themselves bracket that one-judge rule at an 8–20% gap against majority voting. Every prevalence figure, including the 11.7% "baseline", must be restated as *in these synthetic scenarios* or dropped. It measures perceived problematicness by third-party judges, not harm to searchers and not any outcome.

*Judges disagree with each other:* only 49% of judges consistently said problematic suggestions should be avoided, versus 95% who said engines should offer suggestions at all — and, counterintuitively, the pro-moderation judges were slightly *less* likely to flag (χ² = 51.8, p < 0.01).

### 20. Real-time query expansion improved initial query quality in 2007

*Type:* measured finding, abstract only.

*Source:* White & Marchionini, "Examining the effectiveness of real-time query expansion", *Information Processing & Management* 43(3):685–704, 2007, DOI 10.1016/j.ipm.2006.06.005.

*Grounding:* Three interfaces — a baseline with no expansion support, one offering expansion *during query entry*, one offering it after submission. The authors report that offering real-time expansion "leads to better quality initial queries, more engagement in the search, and an increase in the uptake of query expansion", with their own caution that "care must be taken when implementing RTQE interactively."

*Limits:* Abstract retrieved from a single mirror; the body is unread and N is unverified — do not cite a participant count. RTQE suggests *expansion terms* as you type, not ranked historical queries, so it is close kin to query autocomplete and not the same suggestion object. This establishes that a present-versus-absent comparison exists; it does not license "autocomplete improves query quality."

### 21. Autocomplete made workers slower on a one-or-two-word answer task

*Type:* measured finding, randomised present-versus-absent.

*Source:* Liu & Bagrow, *Human Computation* 6(1):42–55, 2019. Article DOI 10.15346/hc.v6i1.3; Crossref indexes 10.15346/hc.v6i1.89 for the same paper.

*Grounding:* 176 AMT workers (90 control / 86 AUI), 1,001 tasks, a conceptualization task — "FOO is a type of:" with a one-line answer. "the AUI makes workers significantly slower": median 7.64s control versus 12.14s AUI, total. Quality unchanged; typo rate not significantly different (1.73% versus 1.56%); responses were *more* lexically and semantically diverse under the AUI.

*Limits:* Control workers produced 692 words across 496 tasks — roughly 1.4 words per response. A task whose answer is one or two words gives autocomplete essentially no keystrokes to save, so this cannot be run against the keystroke-savings case for autocomplete generally. It shows that on very short entries the cost of reading and choosing from a list exceeds anything typing saves. The suggestion corpus was the Microsoft Concept Graph, not a frequency-ranked query log.

### 22. Query autocomplete use concentrates at the session entry point

*Type:* descriptive observation, abstract only.

*Source:* Smith, Gwizdka & Feild, *Information Processing & Management* 53(5):1139–1155, 2017, DOI 10.1016/j.ipm.2017.05.001.

*Grounding:* The authors state in the abstract that QAC is most likely on the first query of a session and less likely on subsequent queries, with a path dependency — no QAC on query 1 makes later use far less likely. They report value shown in shorter sessions and higher retrieval performance.

*Limits:* N = 29, lab, assigned multifaceted tasks. Closed access; the article body is unread by anyone in this chain, so there is no percentage, no decay curve, and no population-level share-of-queries claim. "Higher retrieval performance" is an association among participants who chose to use QAC, not a randomised comparison.

### 23. Autocomplete search suggestions have been measured to shift stated voting preference

*Type:* measured finding, single research line, unreplicated.

*Source:* Epstein, Aries, Grebbien, Salcedo & Zankich, "The search suggestion effect (SSE): A quantification of how autocomplete search suggestions could be used to impact opinions and votes", *Computers in Human Behavior*, 22 June 2024, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2024.108342. Five experiments.

*Grounding:* Headline shift 50/50 → 90/10. It does include a control group that saw no search suggestions at all.

*Limits:* The published body was not fetched (Elsevier 403); citation and abstract confirmed via OpenAlex. The design details are from the 2018 AIBRT white paper of the same series, *not* the published text: a simulated Google-style search engine, four suggestions, an unfamiliar foreign election (candidates for prime minister of Australia, chosen to guarantee participants were undecided), single session, self-reported voting preference, run by the first author's own institute. No independent replication and no published methodological critique was retrieved — and searching was constrained, so absence of retrieved criticism is not evidence of acceptance.

*Disagreement to carry:* 50/50 → 90/10 is an order of magnitude above the d ≈ 0.2 the Cornell writing work gets. Also note the causal path differs: Epstein measures shift downstream of *clicking* a suggestion and then reading the results it returns; Williams-Ceci and Jakesch isolate the actor writing the biased text themselves.

### 24. A library-catalogue log comparison points favourably at real-time expansion

*Type:* descriptive observation, confounded.

*Source:* Niu & Hemminger, "Effectiveness of real-time query expansion in a library catalog", ASIST 2011, DOI 10.1002/meet.2011.14504801165.

*Grounding:* Before/after log comparison, April 2009 versus April 2010, ~800,000 useful records: 20.26% take-up, longer queries, fewer iterations per search, fewer results viewed, and a large drop in search failure rate.

*Limits:* A one-year temporal gap is not a control condition. Weak, confounded support alongside White & Marchionini, not a second trial.

### 25. Term suggestion versus query suggestion, with no baseline

*Type:* measured finding, but no no-suggestion condition.

*Source:* Kelly, Gyllstrom & Bailey, SIGIR 2009, DOI 10.1145/1571941.1572006.

*Grounding:* 55 subjects, 20 topics; each subject did half with term suggestion and half with query suggestion, crossed with system- versus user-generated sources.

*Limits:* No no-suggestion baseline, so it cannot say whether suggestion helps at all. It is post-query relevance-feedback suggestion, not typing-time autocomplete.

### 26. Surrogate quality, query-biased summaries, highlighting, and first-page attention

*Type:* mixed — one 1998 study, one clickthrough analysis, one author's summary judgement, and a log-plus-eyetracking observation. All pre-2009 web search or TREC.

*Source:* Hearst 2009, §5.1–§5.5, reporting Clarke et al. 2007 (SIGIR '07), Tombros & Sanderson 1998, Landauer 1993 / Lesk 1997 / Marchionini 1995, and Granka et al. 2004.

*Grounding:* §5.1, Hearst's own: "The quality of the document surrogate has a strong effect on the ability of the searcher to judge the relevance of the document." · "Even the most relevant document is unlikely to be selected if the title is uninformative or misleading." The surrogate heuristics ("Where possible, all the query terms should appear in the surrogate…", "Length and complexity of URLs should be reduced…") are Clarke et al. 2007, in Hearst's rendering: "Clarke et al., 2007 concluded that these and other results supported the following heuristics" — derived from web search engine clickthrough analysis, with word-level effects in the same passage (*official*, *tourism* positive; *wikipedia*, *free* negative). §5.2: "research suggests that query-biased summaries are superior to showing the first few sentences in retrieval results" — supported by Tombros & Sanderson 1998, 20 participants, TREC ad hoc data. §5.3: "Highlighting of query terms has been found time and again to be a useful feature for information access interfaces." §5.5: "Studies and query logs show that searchers rarely look beyond the first page of search results. If the searcher does not find what they want in the first page, they usually either give up or reformulate their query."

*Limits:* §5.2 is one 1998 study plus later corroboration, not a settled result. §5.3 is Hearst's summary judgement, supported by pre-1998 text-retrieval work. §5.5's scope is 2009-era *web search* query logs plus Granka et al. 2004 eye-tracking (26 participants). Clarke's heuristics come from a web search engine and are visibly domain-bound. Nothing here was measured in in-app or product search.

### 27. Faceted search, defined

*Type:* recommendation and definition; the parametric contrast is a designer's argument.

*Source:* Hearst 2009, §8.6 and §8.6.1.

*Grounding:* "The main idea is to build a set of category hierarchies each of which corresponds to a different facet (dimension or feature type) that is relevant to the collection to be navigated". Parametric search by contrast "requires the user to select a number of attributes from drop-down menus all at once, thus often leading to empty results sets". §8.6.1: "A key component to successful faceted search interfaces is the seamless integration of keyword search."

*Limits:* §8.6 is Hearst describing her own Flamenco project, and "avoidance of empty results sets" is listed there as a Flamenco design goal — so the parametric contrast is a designer's argument, not a comparative measurement. Use this for a definition (free and verifiable) rather than Tunkelang, which is paywalled.

### 28. Conversational search framing

*Type:* theoretical proposal and framing assertion; no evidence of its own.

*Sources:* Radlinski & Craswell, CHIIR '17, pp. 117–126; Mo, Meng, Aliannejadi & Nie, SIGIR tutorial, DOI 10.1145/3726302.3731686, Padua, pp. 4094–4097; Mo et al. survey, arXiv:2410.15576v2, 5 Aug 2025.

*Grounding:* R&C give Definition 1, the five RRIMS evaluative properties (which include User Revealment and System Revealment), five agent actions (a∅, a¹ₚ, a²⁺ₚ, a¹ᵢ, a²⁺ᵢ) and five user response types (rᵣ, rₚ, r_np, r_c, r_t). On facets, verbatim: "Note that all of these interaction modes – as well as critiques and free text entry discussed below – may also be considered 'faceted search'" — R&C placing faceted search inside their action space *by construction*, a claim about their taxonomy, not about how actors experience the facet/query distinction. On critiquing: item-level relevance feedback is "another form of critiquing… dating to the Rocchio relevance feedback algorithm", and "A critique differs in that it explains how a result could be modified to improve its utility to the user." The tutorial's line about context-dependent queries is a reasonable definitional statement of what distinguishes conversational from ad-hoc query input.

*Limits:* R&C is a theory paper: no study, no system, no participants. The tutorial line is a framing assertion in a four-page abstract describing a ten-minute session, with citations to [62] and [53] rather than evidence of its own. The survey and tutorial share organisers, so one is not independent of the other. The clarification-pane material describes *web search clarification panes* as introduced by Zamani et al., where candidate facets are generated or selected terms representing sub-intents and a click concatenates the selected term onto the query string — a specific Bing-style mechanism, not faceted navigation over structured metadata fields.

### 29. A pointer, not a source

*Type:* published directional claim that cannot be assessed.

*Source:* Kaiser et al., DOI 10.1145/3706599.3720123 — a seven-page late-breaking contribution in the *Extended Abstracts* proceedings, pp. 1–7, closed access with no OA location anywhere.

*Grounding:* n = 1,526. The sentence available is "suggesting it is more often seen as a one-stop solution rather than a connective hub" — the authors' interpretation stated inside the abstract, with no operationalisation of "primary web sources", no test, and no effect size.

*Limits:* Methods unobtainable. It corroborates nothing; it is a pointer to a study someone should obtain.

## Still unknown

*The rate at which searches return nothing.* In any system, at any scale. Absent from this corpus and not derivable from it. Baymard's ~50% measures page affordances, not search failure.

*Whether any recovery move improves task success.* Did-you-mean, partial matching, relaxed matching, fallback to browse, related categories — the pre-AI corpus contains one controlled outcome test (Anick's Prisma), and it was null on result clicks.

*Whether query autocomplete helps or harms on balance.* White & Marchionini point one way on initial query quality in 2007-era term expansion; Liu & Bagrow point the other on speed for one-word answers; Smith et al. suggest the benefit concentrates at session entry; Niu & Hemminger's catalogue logs point favourably but are confounded by a year of other change. Nothing modern, nothing on ranked-historical-query autocomplete, nothing on web search at scale.

*How much of the search-side literature has actually been searched.* This is an honest state of the absence claims above, not a finding. Cai & de Rijke's *A Survey of Query Auto Completion in Information Retrieval* (Foundations and Trends in IR, 2016, DOI 10.1561/1500000055) devotes a chapter to presentation and interaction aspects of QAC and was not consulted; nor was Hofmann et al.'s eye-tracking study of user interactions with query auto completion (CIKM 2014). Ward et al. — a source already in hand — cites White & Marchionini three times, including the sentence that autocomplete "improved the quality of initial queries for both known item and exploratory tasks". Until at least that survey chapter is read, the defensible statement is *no systematic search has been done*, not *no study exists*.

*How much of the 2002–2008 log evidence survives.* Mobile-dominant search, instant results and answer-engine result pages all postdate it.

*Whether the writing-autocomplete steering mechanism survives compression to a query.* Nobody has measured it. The mechanism Williams-Ceci isolate — the actor authors the biased text and behaviour drags attitude after it — has an analogue in query autocomplete, since the actor commits the query as their own. But a three-to-six-word query is not five sentences of argument, and no study bridges that.

*How often real production autocomplete surfaces problematic suggestions to real searchers, and with what effect.* Olteanu et al. cannot answer this by construction. Nothing found does. The gap is not a missing taxonomy; it is that nobody has connected a taxonomy to encounter rates or to any searcher outcome.

*What actors encounter, as opposed to what they ask for and what they then write.* No coverage or diversity-of-exposure measure appears anywhere in the AI corpus. Sharma names this as the open question and proposes eye-tracking.

*Whether citations are checked when the stakes are real.* The 0.43 figure comes from a task with no consequence for being wrong, and the authors say so.

*What reference designs would change the null.* That references as built had no effect is measured. That some other treatment would is untested — every design proposal in Sharma §7.2 and §7.3 is a recommendation.

*Citation recall and precision in any system after March 2023.* No measurement exists in this corpus in either direction. Do not infer improvement or decay.

*Whether register behaviour holds in a genuinely merged single-input interface.* No source tests it. Mayerhofer et al. tested two labelled panes. This is the question a page on the search/chat boundary most needs answered.

*How actors experience the facet/query distinction.* No empirical source tests it. Both theory sources place facets inside a conversational action space by taxonomic construction; Mayerhofer et al. contains zero occurrences of "facet" or "filter". The query-as-filter versus query-as-instruction contrast has no empirical grounding in this corpus at all.

*Whether the switching asymmetry holds outside four German-language health tasks* with a citation-free chat model that never expressed uncertainty and never returned nothing.

*Whether the good-abandonment verification failure reproduces* outside a single constructed task with a single defective featured snippet and self-reported low motivation.

*Whether NN/g's no-notice finding generalises* past the one participant and one page it rests on.

*Whether the Epstein SSE line replicates outside its own lab.* No independent replication and no published methodological critique was retrieved, under constrained searching.

*Whether the pre-2009 web-search and TREC results hold in in-app or product search.* Clarke's surrogate heuristics, the query-biased summary result and the first-page finding were all measured on web search engines or TREC document collections.

*Kaiser's entire method*, and Baymard's benchmark n, sample date and scoring rule. Both unobtainable.

*Primary methods, anywhere in the pre-AI corpus.* Every figure in the Hearst-derived material reaches us second-hand through a 2009 literature review. Her paraphrases are internally consistent and her citation strings are intact, but nobody in this chain has read a primary paper's methods section.

*Replication.* Sharma and Liu are single, unreplicated studies from single teams. So is Mayerhofer.

## Citation traps

### Attribution errors — wrong author, wrong source, wrong structure

*Hearst is not the author of the studies she reports.* Jansen, Anick, White, Bruza, Lin, Cucerzan & Brill, Hargittai, Yee, Clarke, Tombros & Sanderson are separate authors reported in her 2009 chapter. Write "as reported in Hearst 2009" and do not attribute their figures to her.

*Radlinski & Craswell have no action called "user inquires".* The string does not appear in the paper — zero hits for "inquir". The paper has two separate structures a writer could conflate: the five RRIMS *evaluative properties* (which include User Revealment and System Revealment) and a formal *action space* of five agent actions and five user response types, written in symbols. Citing "system asks / system reveals / user reveals / user inquires" as their action space cites a structure that does not exist.

*The nine modes belong to Russell-Rose, Lamantia and Makri.* Tate is not an author of that paper. Nobody in this chain has read *Designing the Search Experience*'s Modes chapter (pp. 71–95). That the book presents the same nine is an inference with the direction of borrowing assumed. Cite the paper; do not bridge to the book.

*Search Patterns is 2010, not 2009.* And the six behaviour patterns (Quit, Narrow, Expand, Pearl Growing, Pogosticking, Thrashing) are attested only on the archived 2011 companion site, not in the book text available.

*"38 biologists questioned, 19 shown mock-ups" implies a subset.* Hearst describes "a followup survey... in which 19 participants from biology professions were shown four different interface mock-ups" — a separate group, not stated as drawn from the 38. The accompanying citation "Pacific Symposium on Biocomputing 568:79, 2008" is Hearst's site mangling pages 568–579; it is not volume:page.

*Liu & Bagrow's own DOI is 10.15346/hc.v6i1.3.* Crossref indexes 10.15346/hc.v6i1.89 for the same paper.

*Author names and citation strings here are inherited from the verification reports.* Several were independently confirmed against Crossref, OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar — Williams-Ceci, Smith et al., Radlinski & Craswell, Kaiser, the Hearst and Morville book records, the Designing the Search Experience chapter list. Others were not, and reach this note through a single mirror retrieval of an abstract (White & Marchionini, Epstein) or through Hearst's own rendering (every pre-AI study she reports). Check any string before it goes on a page.

### Same-name collisions

- *Liu, Zhang & Liang* (2023 citation audit of generative search engines) is not *Liu & Bagrow* (2019 autocomplete trial).
- *White et al., SIGIR '07* (query suggestions, 36 participants, via Hearst) is not *White & Marchionini, IP&M 2007* (real-time query expansion). Same year, same first author, different studies.
- *Jansen et al. 2005* (AltaVista, 2002 snapshot) is not *Jansen et al. 2007b* (Dogpile, 2005). Different denominators, different session definitions, not commensurable.
- *Russell 2006* is a presentation by a different person from *Russell-Rose*, the practitioner author.
- *Mo et al.* names both a survey (arXiv:2410.15576v2) and a SIGIR tutorial. They share organisers, so one does not corroborate the other.
- Two separate NN/g studies appear here: the good-abandonment study and the pooled search meta-analysis corpus (Moran & Goray).

### Recommendations reported as data

*Baymard's five recovery strategies contain no numbers.* The article's use of "proven" must not become "evidenced". Partial matching, relaxed matching and fallback-to-browse have no measured effect anywhere in this corpus.

*Tunkelang's "User studies demonstrate that faceted search provides more effective information-seeking support to users than best-first search"* is verbatim on the publisher page, but it is the author's own summary in the blurb for his own lecture, with no study named and the text behind a paywall. It can only be reported as how the author frames his own 2009 lecture.

*Hearst's "show results immediately, single-term hyperlink refinement fares better"* is her stated design principle, not the Bruza finding. Bruza's actual result was mixed: higher average precision than the web search engine, but slower and higher cognitive load.

*Hearst §8.6's parametric-search contrast* is a Flamenco design goal in a section where she describes her own project, not a comparative measurement.

*Apple and Material Design 3 are recommendations throughout.* Neither page reports a measurement. Both are live pages that change; Apple's most recent changelog entry is 8 June 2026 and specifically concerns terminology, so every quote needs a retrieval date.

*Sharma §7.2 and §7.3 design proposals are untested*, including the appealing one about repurposing references for breadth rather than only for checking.

*Radlinski & Craswell's entire formalism is a theoretical proposal.* No study, no system, no participants.

*The Olteanu taxonomy is a framework; the Olteanu prevalence figures are about synthetic scenarios.* Both come from the same paper and only one is usable as a rate — and it is not a rate about real searchers.

### Uptake reported as effect

This is the easiest error to make with the pre-AI material. *Lin's 18.5%, Anick & Kantamneni's 6%, Dogpile's 8.4% and 22.2%, White's 35.7% and 35.2% are all measures of how often a feature was used.* None is an outcome against a control. In the whole pre-AI corpus exactly one recovery move has a measured outcome against a control — Anick's Prisma bucket test — and it was null on result clicks. Any heading that groups the uptake figures as "evidenced" or "measured" will read as evidence that these moves work.

Hearst herself makes a version of this error: she calls the 8.4% "additional evidence that query term refinement suggestions are a useful reformulation feature."

### Denominator errors

- *Anick's 25% / 16% / 47% / 38%* are shares *of sessions containing refinements*, not of all sessions. Hearst's sentence begins "In the Prisma condition, of those sessions containing refinements".
- *Dogpile's 22.2%* is the share of query-modification *actions* within reformulation sessions — a different denominator from the 8.4% of all queries.
- *Liu's 51.5% and 74.5%* are unweighted means of four system means and describe no system that existed. Quote per-system numbers or none.
- *Sharma's 4.48 versus 0.43* is not like-for-like, and the 4.48 is consonant-clicks only (consonant plus dissonant ≈ 7.76).
- *Baymard's "344 top ecommerce sites"* comes from site chrome, is contradicted by "340+" elsewhere on the same page, and is not given as the ~50% figure's denominator.
- *NN/g's n = 37* is arithmetic inference (7 + 30) and never appears in the article.
- *Williams-Ceci's 73%* is a subgroup figure — treated participants whose attitude shifted toward the AI — not a general rate. And χ²(2, N = 219) = 1.29, p = .524 is the test that awareness did *not* differ between the influenced and the uninfluenced, not the self-report statistic.

### Figures carried out of scope

- *Williams-Ceci's omnibus is F(2, 1474), not F(3, 1474).*
- *"Specialization outweighs generalization roughly two to one"* holds for the pure categories (16.3 versus 7.2 = 2.26:1) and collapses once the "with reformulation" rows are included: 26.2 versus 17.0 = 1.54:1, with the variants near-identical at 9.9 versus 9.8.
- *The 88–90% spelling-correction accuracy at ~50% coverage* describes 2004–2006 algorithms. It is history, not a statement about spelling correction today.
- *"A meta-analysis of 471 different instances of search, users only clicked through to the second page in 2% of cases"* is neither a meta-analysis nor separately cited, and the 2% does not appear on the methodology page it links to. What that page shows is NN/g's own pooled lab corpus.
- *Jumpshot's 35% desktop / 62% mobile zero-click* has no link, no date and no method in the article carrying it, and describes web-wide Google search, so it cannot transfer to a site-search or no-results claim.
- *Mayerhofer Table 3 must be given in task order* — T1 27/14/59, T2 32/32/36, T3 36/27/36, T4 23/5/73 — and with the web-search-only column intact. Reordering the rows silently makes a reader mispair every value, and dropping the web-only column removes the one bearing most directly on whether actors blend.
- *r = −0.96* has no n, no p-value and no named correlation type; the only units are four systems. It cannot support a general claim that accurate citation costs perceived helpfulness.

### Cross-domain transfer without warrant

- *Jakesch and Williams-Ceci are about writing assistants, not search.* Neither paper contains a search-box or query condition. Williams-Ceci's published title dropped the word "autocomplete" for this reason. Jakesch's own limitation warns that "a predictive keyboard that only suggests single words" may behave differently.
- *Liu's citation audit and Sharma's lab study do not join into one argument.* The join spans a February–March 2023 annotation audit of four commercial products with no users involved, set against a 2024 lab study of a closed-corpus RAG prototype whose own citation quality was never measured. Different systems, different years, different corpora. Naming the design risk is fine; presenting it as a finding is not.
- *Epstein's causal path is not Williams-Ceci's.* Epstein measures shift downstream of clicking a suggestion and reading the results it returns; Williams-Ceci isolate the actor writing the biased text themselves.
- *Clarke's surrogate heuristics, the query-biased summary result and the first-page finding were measured on web search engines and TREC collections.* None was measured in in-app or product search.
- *The entire practitioner corpus is a pre-AI baseline.* Carrying surrogate quality, first-page attention, faceted navigation or mode chains into an AI-mediated search interface is an extrapolation none of the sources make.
- *Selecting a facet and typing a follow-up are not the same operation.* The equivalence is an inference generalised from one mechanism — term concatenation in Bing-style clarification panes — to all faceted refinement. It does not hold even within clarification panes: concatenation produces a keyword query, whereas a conversational follow-up triggers context-dependent query rewriting, which the tutorial identifies as the distinguishing hard problem.
- *"Decomposing complex or instruction-based queries"* is verbatim in the Mo tutorial, but it appears in a bulleted list of system-side retrieval techniques describing generation-augmented retrieval. It is a label match on the word "instruction", carrying no design finding about how actors phrase queries.
- *Mayerhofer et al. did not test a merged interface.* Their method describes two separately labelled panes with distinct input widgets. The study cannot support any claim about what a single search box does.
- *The survey's "keyword-and-scan" line is speculation, not corroboration.* It is uncited authorial speculation in the survey's future-directions section on evaluation, phrased as "users might notice" and used to argue for better evaluation protocols. It also comes from overlapping authors, so it is not independent of the tutorial.

### Internal contradictions in single sources — report the disagreement, do not resolve it

- *Hearst's chapter conclusion contradicts the study she reports.* "Term suggestion tools are used roughly 35% of the time that they are offered to users" is a chapter-conclusion generalisation, not a measurement — the denominator is unmeasured by her own explicit admission ten paragraphs earlier, that Jansen "do not report on what proportion of queries were offered refinements". It also conflicts with the production figures in the same chapter (6% and 8.4%), tracking only the lab figure (35.7%) and Anick's refinement-session share. The neighbouring sentence, "Both spelling suggestions and term expansions are effective reformulation tools", directly contradicts Anick's null result, which Hearst reported herself. Do not quote either.
- *Hearst on spelling-correction uptake:* "There are no published large-scale statistics on user uptake of spelling correction", set against Russell's 33% / 5% and Hargittai's zero of 37. Report all three.
- *Sharma's Study 2 N:* 213 in the abstract and introduction, 223 in §5.5 and Table 2, df = (2, 210) in every ANCOVA. And §5.5 describes carrying the Neutral cell over from Study 1 in the same paragraph as "Participants in Study 1 were not allowed to participate in Study 2."
- *Mayerhofer on sources:* "None of the participants asked the chat for sources", set against the same paper's description of one participant asking the chat for supporting studies in the chat-only SU25 Consensus case, "successful in the single instance it was used."
- *Mayerhofer on blending:* Table 3 supports a blending story; Figure 5 shows blending correlated with the worst outcomes (47% correct versus 77% and 76%, χ²(2, N = 88) = 8.39, p = 0.015). Both come from the same paper.
