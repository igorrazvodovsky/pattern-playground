# Practice evidence: searching in shipping products — 2026-08-27

Companion to `2026-08-27.md` (literature). Practitioner research, product behaviour and design-guidance texts, kept separate so the two provenance classes stay distinguishable.

> *Provenance*. Baymard and NN/g pages were fetched and every quotation was matched character-for-character against the source, with the numbers inside them traced to their own cited sources where those exist — several did not survive that trace. Apple HIG and Material Design 3 were rendered on 2026-08-27 and are live pages that change. The books were verified as far as free text allows: Hearst is fully readable, Morville & Callender through the sampler and copyright page, Russell-Rose & Tate through Chapter 2 and the Crossref chapter records, Tunkelang only through publisher abstracts. Production log figures reported second-hand through Hearst Ch 5–6 sit in the literature note, not here.

## Takeaways for the pattern page

- Do not state a failure rate. No query-level zero-result rate exists in this corpus, in any system, at any scale — and the absence is itself worth naming on the page.
- Exactly one recovery move in the whole pre-AI corpus has a measured outcome against a control (Anick's Prisma bucket test, in the literature note), and it was null on result clicks. Everything else — did-you-mean, partial matching, relaxed matching, fallback to browse, related categories — is recommendation.
- The one citable design rule for failure is Flamenco's, and it is preventative rather than recuperative: never render a link that would return zero results; preview counts on every result-selecting link.
- Both platform guidance texts converge on two things the page can state plainly: search wants a single identified location, and its current scope must be visible. Both treat suggestion timing as a choice, not a prescription.
- Good abandonment is the pre-generative name for what generative search has made the default, and NN/g's finding — 5 of 18 answers wrong, traced to one defective snippet read only in bold — is the practice-side counterpart of the citation-verifiability literature.

## Practice

Same ordering criterion. Note that the practitioner corpus is 2009–2013 apart from the two living guideline pages, and none of it addresses conversational or generative search.

### 1. About half of benchmarked ecommerce sites have no-results pages without effective recovery paths

*Type:* practitioner benchmarking — a measure of *recovery affordances on the page*, not of how often searches fail.

*Source:* Baymard Institute, ecommerce Search UX benchmark. Article published 2019-02-04, updated 2025-02-18.

*Grounding:* Roughly 50% of sites implement no-results pages without effective recovery paths.

*Limits:* The article's three phrasings of this are one underlying measurement restated, not three corroborating findings. The article states no n — "344 top ecommerce sites" comes from site chrome, which elsewhere on the same page says "340+", and is not given as this figure's denominator. "Latest benchmark" is undated, so the figure's vintage is unknown across a 2019–2025 update window. The benchmark n, sample date and scoring rule are paywalled.

### 2. Composed answers can be accepted without checking, and were sometimes wrong

*Type:* practitioner eye-tracking study with exact denominators.

*Source:* Nielsen Norman Group, good-abandonment study.

*Grounding:* One fact-finding task (median household income 2017), deliberately constructed with misleading information scent. 7 participants gave up, 30 searched, 18 found an answer via good abandonment, and 5 of those 18 were wrong.

*Limits:* n = 37 is arithmetic inference (7 + 30) and is never stated in the article. The 5 are not five independent failures — the article decomposes them into two who read one defective Wikipedia-derived featured snippet and two who "didn't read the featured snippet closely", all tracing to a single Google extraction error on one query. Carry the authors' own low-motivation hedge. One constructed task, one defective snippet.

*Not a finding:* the authors' explanation that extraction removes the cues actors use to judge credibility. It is argued from hypotheticals — "Maybe the visual design... Maybe the content..." — and is the design-relevant hypothesis, not a measurement.

### 3. De-emphasised no-results text can go unread

*Type:* qualitative observation — an existence proof, not a rate.

*Source:* Nielsen Norman Group (Whitenton).

*Grounding:* Eye-tracking of one participant on one page; Whitenton writes "the user has misspelled a search term... where *his* eyes focused". NN/g calls it a "shockingly common failure".

*Limits:* One participant, one page. "Shockingly common" is an unquantified practitioner claim. This is adequate to justify making the no-results statement prominent and useless as a frequency.

### 4. Second-page clicks were rare in NN/g's own US lab sessions

*Type:* descriptive observation over a pooled lab corpus.

*Source:* Nielsen Norman Group; methodology page Moran & Goray, 2019-11-17.

*Grounding:* The methodology page describes 471 queries from four usability studies, 83 participants, Raleigh / Kansas City / San Francisco, 2016–2018, tasks that were not about search ("we did not ask users to use (or not use) search"), overwhelmingly Google.

*Limits:* This is *not* a meta-analysis and the "2%" figure does not appear on the methodology page at all. Usable only as: in NN/g's own US lab sessions, second-page clicks were rare.

### 5. Site and intranet search fail more visibly than web search

*Type:* practitioner observation, no rate attached.

*Sources:* Whitenton (NN/g), 2014; Baymard.

*Grounding:* Whitenton's asymmetry claim — web-wide search rarely returns zero results, site and intranet search often does. Baymard: "on-site searches sometimes return no matching results."

*Limits:* Neither quantifies anything. *No query-level zero-result rate exists anywhere in this corpus*, in any system, at any scale.

### 6. Actors rarely act on written search tips

*Type:* qualitative observation from usability testing.

*Source:* Baymard.

*Limits:* No numbers.

### 7. Recovery strategies for no-results pages

*Type:* recommendation. Not measured anywhere in this corpus.

*Sources:* Baymard's five strategies; NN/g's three guidelines.

*Limits:* Baymard's article contains no numbers for any of the five, and its use of the word "proven" must not be carried into an evidence claim. Partial matching, relaxed matching and fallback-to-browse have no measured effect anywhere in this corpus.

### 8. Apple's platform guidance for search

*Type:* recommendation.

*Source:* Apple Human Interface Guidelines, "Searching". Rendered 2026-08-27; the page's most recent changelog entry is 8 June 2026, "Updated terminology and refined best practices" — so these are the most drift-prone claims here and any quote needs a retrieval date.

*Grounding:* Five best practices, verbatim leads. "If search is important, give it a primary position in your app or view" — Notes puts a search field in the bottom toolbar; Photos and Apple TV give search a dedicated tab. "Aim to make your app's content searchable through a single location" — "People appreciate having one clearly identified location they can use to find anything they're looking for in your app", with the exception that "For apps with clearly distinct sections, it may still be useful to offer a local search" (the iOS Music example, where search acts as a filter on the current view). "Clearly display the current scope of a search" — "Use a descriptive placeholder text, a scope bar, or a title to help reinforce what someone is currently searching". "Provide suggestions to make searching easier" — recent searches before typing, predictive suggestions during typing. "Take privacy into consideration before displaying search history" — "If you do show search history, provide a way for people to clear it if they want." Platform considerations: "No additional considerations for iOS, iPadOS, macOS, tvOS, visionOS, or watchOS." Everything after the best-practices block is Spotlight indexing guidance.

*Limits:* Apple does prescribe suggestions during typing — "offer predictive search suggestions while they're typing" — but says nothing about whether *results* update per keystroke or only on submit.

### 9. Material Design 3's guidance for search

*Type:* recommendation.

*Source:* Material Design 3, Search guidelines and overview pages, rendered 2026-08-27.

*Grounding:* Entry points — "The search bar is a persistent and prominent search field at the top of the screen" for content "in a specific view"; "For global search, use a persistent search app bar, integrated into an app bar"; "Use a search icon button when search is a secondary action". Focused-search layouts — "Docked opens a list below the search bar, with a scrim covering main content" · "Full-screen expands to fill the screen", with full-screen the default for compact windows (<600dp) and docked for medium (600–839dp) and expanded (840–1199dp): "Search suggestions or results should swap from full-screen in compact windows to docked in larger". Scroll behaviour — a search bar can "Scroll away with content, then reappear when a person begins scrolling up" or "Remain fixed at the top of the screen". Timing is given as three options, not a prescription: "Show historical suggestions before typing" · "Show suggestions or results as someone is typing" · "Wait to show suggestions or results until a search is queried". Container — "In the contained style, the search bar container remains the same shape in both the unfocused and focused states. Avoid changing the container behavior." · "Use a maximum of two trailing icons." Contrast — "Avoid using a surface container high color on a surface container background. This can cause the search bar to blend in, making it difficult for people to find."

*Naming:* "search view" is absent from the guidelines page but present on the overview sibling as retired naming — "February 2025 Naming: Search bar and search view are now collectively named search", and under M2→M3 differences, "Two official variants of search components: search bar and search view". Defensible statement: *search view* was M3-baseline terminology, superseded in the February 2025 M3 Expressive update; the current model is search bar → focused search.

### 10. Design guidelines are hard to follow, and Hearst says why

*Type:* recommendation, plus the author's critique of recommendations.

*Source:* Hearst 2009, §1.1, §1.3, §1.4, §1.6, §1.10.

*Grounding:* §1.1, Hearst's own: "Search is a means towards some other end, rather than a goal in itself" and "the fewer distractions while reading, the more usable the interface". §1.4, her critique, contiguous and the most usable thing in the chapter: "These guidelines provide good advice for search UI design. However, design guidelines can be difficult to follow, for a number of reasons. First, they are under-specified; they do not usually say how to achieve the guideline's goals. Second, meeting one guideline often conflicts with meeting another." §1.6: "Users prefer comprehensible, predictable, and controllable environments." §1.10: "small details can make the difference between a successful and a failed design."

The eight desiderata in §1.4 — *informative feedback · user control · reduce short-term memory load · shortcuts for skilled users · reduce errors · consistency · easy reversal · design for closure* — are Shneiderman et al. 1997, reported by Hearst. The §1.3 list — *Learnability / Efficiency / Memorability / Errors / Satisfaction* — is Shneiderman & Plaisant 2004, restated by Nielsen 2003b, per Hearst.

*Limits:* The §1.4 lead-in is *not contiguous* in the source; a citation tooltip splits it. Quote it as "an influential paper by [Shneiderman et al., 1997] specifies eight design desiderata for search user interfaces generally (re-ordered below)" with the elision marked. The same hazard applies to any Hearst string crossing a citation anchor.

### 11. Nine modes of search and discovery, and the chains they form

*Type:* framework derived from stakeholder-articulated requirements. Not behaviour data.

*Source:* Russell-Rose, Lamantia & Makri, "Defining and Applying a Language for Discovery".

*Grounding:* Nine modes, verbatim definitions — *Locate* "To find a specific (possibly known) item" · *Verify* "To confirm that an item meets some specific, objective criterion" · *Monitor* "Maintain awareness of the status of an item for purposes of management or control" · *Compare* "To identify similarities & differences within a set of items" · *Comprehend* "To generate independent insight by interpreting patterns within a data set" · *Evaluate* "To use judgement to determine the value of an item with respect to a specific goal" · *Explore* "To investigate an item or data set for the purpose of knowledge discovery" · *Analyze* "To examine an item or data set to identify patterns & relationships" · *Synthesize* "To create a novel or composite artefact from diverse inputs".

The co-occurrence claim, verbatim: "their real value lies not so much in their occurrence as individual instances but in the patterns of co-occurrence they reveal… modes combine to form distinct chains and patterns."

*Eight* chains, split by context. Site search: *Insight-driven* (Explore-Analyze-Comprehend), *Opportunistic* (Explore-Locate-Evaluate), *Qualified* (Locate-Verify). Then "By contrast, enterprise search was characterized by…": *Comparative* (Analyze-Compare-Evaluate), *Exploratory* (Explore-Analyze-Evaluate), *Strategic Insight* (Analyze-Comprehend-Evaluate), *Strategic Oversight* (Monitor-Analyze-Evaluate), *Comparison-driven Synthesis* (Analyze-Compare-Synthesize).

*Limits:* The empirical base is 381 micro-scenarios, 277 site search and 104 enterprise, "collected as part of a series of requirements workshops involving stakeholders and customer-facing staff from various client organisations", "generated by participants in breakout sessions and subsequently moderated by the workshop facilitator", then "prioritised by the group". These are moderated, deduplicated, prioritised stakeholder-articulated requirements. End users did not produce them and nobody observed a search session. Any count drawn from them — Locate 120 site versus 2 enterprise; Analyze 84 enterprise versus 58 site — is a count of what client-side participants said they needed, across eight or so consulting engagements. The Marchionini three-tier grouping is labelled by the paper itself as retrospective: "we have retrospectively explored the degree to which they align with existing frameworks."

### 12. A pattern language for search

*Type:* recommendation / pattern catalogue.

*Source:* Morville & Callender, *Search Patterns*, O'Reilly, 2010 — not 2009. Copyright page: "Copyright © 2010 Peter Morville and Jeff Callender", "January 2010: First Edition", ISBN 978-0-596-80227-1.

*Grounding:* Ten design patterns with page numbers, from the book's own table of contents: Autocomplete 82, Best First 86, Federated Search 91, Faceted Navigation 95, Advanced Search 102, Personalization 105, Pagination 110, Structured Results 117, Actionable Results 120, Unified Discovery 125. Nine design principles, Chapter 3: Incremental Construction 68, Progressive Disclosure 69, Immediate Response 71, Alternate Views 72, Predictability 74, Recognition Over Recall 75, Minimal Disruption 77, Direct Manipulation 78, Context of Use 80. Chapter 1 verbatim: "search is a wicked problem of terrific consequence" · "Search is the worst usability problem on the Web. It's held that title for many years." · "We will look to the center by describing a pattern language for search that explains user psychology and behavior, embraces emerging technologies and rich interaction models, and illustrates repeatable solutions to common problems."

*Limits:* The six behaviour patterns — Quit, Narrow, Expand, Pearl Growing, Pogosticking, Thrashing — appear only in the archived 2011 companion-site navigation. None of the six strings occurs in the free sampler, and Chapter 3's "Patterns of Behavior" section (p. 52) is not in it. Cite them as the companion site's pattern library, not as book text.

### 13. Designing the Search Experience — structure only

*Type:* bibliographic, not a claim.

*Source:* Russell-Rose & Tate, *Designing the Search Experience*, Morgan Kaufmann/Elsevier. Site says "Published 2012 by Morgan Kaufmann", copyright page 2013, ISBN 978-0-12-396981-1. Chapter 2 is a free PDF; the rest was unreachable.

*Grounding:* Part structure, confirmed against all 22 Crossref chapter records. Part 1 *A Framework for Search and Discovery* (1–2) — The User 3–21, Information Seeking 23–45, Context 47–69, Modes of Search and Discovery 71–95. Part 2 *Design Solutions* (97) — Formulating the Query 99–128, Displaying and Manipulating Results 129–166, Faceted Search 167–218, Mobile Search 219–251, Social Search 253–275. Part 3 *Designing the Future* (277) — Cross-Channel Information Interaction 279–293.

*Limits:* Report this as a part structure and nothing more. No named "search framework layers" model is verifiable from any text reachable here.

### 14. Tunkelang on faceted search — paywalled

*Type:* bibliographic, with one usable line.

*Source:* Tunkelang, *Faceted Search*, Morgan & Claypool, 2009. DOI 10.2200/S00190ED1V01Y200904ICR005 now redirects to Springer. Softcover 978-3-031-01134-4, published 1 June 2009; eBook 978-3-031-02262-3, republished 31 May 2022. Every chapter page reads "This is a preview of subscription content".

*Grounding:* The only citable line about the search/browse boundary is the Chapter 3 abstract, which gives the argument's shape and not a definition: "In the first chapter, we saw that faceted classification addresses some of the limitations of a taxonomy for knowledge representation. In the second chapter, we saw that both search and directory navigation have limitations as interfaces for information retrieval."

*Limits:* His Chapter 1 definition of facets, and wherever he draws the search/browse line, are unread. Use Hearst §8.6 for a definition instead.

### 15. Students used autocomplete mainly to fix spelling

*Type:* qualitative observation from usability testing. Placed in Practice rather than Literature despite the journal venue, because the method is speak-aloud usability testing and the authors disclaim quantitative status.

*Source:* Ward, Hahn & Feist, *Information Technology and Libraries* 31(4):6–19, 2012. Full PDF freely available.

*Grounding:* Two rounds of usability testing, 5 students then 8, speak-aloud, no present-versus-absent condition. Students used autocomplete mainly to fix spelling on known-item searches — all 8 second-round students took a suggestion on at least 2 of 3 known-item tasks — sometimes without noticing they had corrected their own spelling. Several read the suggestion list as confirmation that a topic was "real" and previously searched. Those confident in a topic skipped the list because typing it was faster.

*Limits:* The authors state plainly: "this method does not allow us to state definitive trends for all autocomplete implementations. It is not a statistically significant method by quantitative standards."

### 16. A composed answer designed to keep the result set

*Type:* design position, not an outcome measure.

*Source:* NeevaAI, as described in Liu, Zhang & Liang.

*Grounding:* "its response is designed for display within a conventional search results page."

*Limits:* It is the one case in this corpus of a composed answer built around a surviving result set. Nothing measures whether that helped.
