## Context

An actor composes a standing condition — clauses over fields, operators and values, combined and
negated, added and deleted — and the system applies it to cases the actor is not present for.
Filters and saved searches, spam and mail rules, smart playlists, IFTTT-style automations, agent
tuning rules, autonomy bounds.

The candidate node sits below `workflow` (the multi-step procedure as artifact) and above
`bounded-choice` (one clause's value pick). Questions:

1. Is composing the condition one move, or does end-user programming literature split trigger
   authoring from action authoring so decisively that a single node lies?
2. Is the rule unit genuinely separable from the multi-step procedure?
3. Boolean composition is known to be error-prone — is the real pattern about *avoiding* explicit
   boolean composition rather than supporting it?
4. Is previewing a rule by its edge cases (newly matches, barely matches, barely fails) attested, or
   is the attested move something else?

  - Grounding: abstract + author-institution summary; not full text.

- *Trigger-Action Programming in the Wild: An Analysis of 200,000 IFTTT Recipes* (CHI 2016) — Ur, Pak
  Ho, Brawner, Lee, Mennicken, Picard, Schulze & Littman. 224,590 publicly shared programs, over
  100,000 distinct authoring users; "a very broad array of connections that appear to fill gaps in
  functionality, yet users often duplicate others' programs". [par.nsf.gov/biblio/10026427]
  - Speaks to: Q2
  - Transfer: strong — scale evidence that the single rule is the object people actually author and
    share; duplication is evidence for the generated-draft entry point.
  - Grounding: abstract, NSF public-access record.

- *Supporting Mental Model Accuracy in Trigger-Action Programming* (UbiComp 2015) — Huang & Cakmak.
  Identifies two classes of occurrence in TAP rules — instantaneous *events* and protracted *states*
  — and finds users routinely confuse them; argues the "If ⟨trigger⟩ Then ⟨action⟩" form does not
  support the distinction, and proposes interface-level fixes (grouping and naming states separately
  from events, different temporal conjunctions and verbal structures).
  - Speaks to: Q1
  - Transfer: strong — locates the difficulty inside the condition, and makes it a *wording* problem,
    which is a design lever.
  - Grounding: abstract-only via secondary sources. Flagged: full text not read.

- *How Users Interpret Bugs in Trigger-Action Programming* (CHI 2019) — Brackenbury, Deora, Ritchey,
  Vallee, He, Wang, Littman & Ur. "We first systematize the temporal paradigms through which TAP
  systems could express rules. We then identify ten classes of TAP programming bugs related to
  control flow, timing, and inaccurate user expectations." 153-participant online study; half the
  shown rules carried a bug. "For most of the bug classes, we found that the presence of a bug made
  it harder for participants to correctly predict the behavior of the rule."
  - Speaks to: Q1, Q4
  - Transfer: strong — the finding is that a written rule does not tell its reader what it does, which
    is the force the preview exists to answer.
  - Grounding: abstract, NSF public-access record.

- *Empowering End Users in Debugging Trigger-Action Rules* (CHI 2019) — Corno, De Russis & Monge
  Roffarello. EUDebug lets the user compose a rule in an IFTTT-like interface and offers step-by-step
  simulation; a hybrid Semantic Coloured Petri Net models, checks and simulates the rules *and their
  interactions*, highlighting "possible problems that the set of all defined rules may generate".
  Exploratory study, 15 end users.
  - Speaks to: Q4, and unexpectedly Q1
  - Transfer: strong for the preview move; the rule-set framing was not in the strawman at all.
  - Grounding: abstract and system description via publisher and secondary pages; the open PDF mirror

- *Helping Users Debug Trigger-Action Programs* (IMWUT 6(4), 2022) — Zhang, Zhou, Littman, Ur & Lu.
  30 participants, 84 hours, in an instrumented 3-D smart-home simulator; 16 obstacles coded across
  four debugging stages. Defines the rule anatomy as "IF a trigger happens, WHILE conditions are
  true, THEN apply an action", with a trigger "a statement over events" and a condition "a Boolean
  proposition over the status of device capabilities". Contributes "A Patch Behavior Visualization
  interface … that shows users what would have happened in the history if a patch were applied."
  - Speaks to: Q1, Q2, Q4
  - Transfer: strong — the single most useful paper in the run.
  - Grounding: **full text read** (author-hosted PDF).

- *A Graphical Filter/Flow Representation of Boolean Queries: A Prototype Implementation and
  Evaluation* (JASIS 44(6), 1993) — Young & Shneiderman. Twenty subjects with no Boolean-logic
  experience, five comprehension and five composition tasks in each of two interfaces. Composition:
  4.2 correct with Filter/Flow against 1.9 with SQL (F(1,18) = 65.8, p < 0.05). "The total number of
  errors made in Filter/Flow was 12, of which 9 were Boolean errors. In SQL, 28 errors were made, of
  which 25 were Boolean errors." "A total of fifty parentheses errors were made in SQL, while only
  two errors were made in specifying the order of the attribute menus in Filter/Flow."
  - Speaks to: Q3
  - Transfer: strong on the mechanism, weak on the domain (bibliographic search, 1993). The
    parenthesis/ordering contrast transfers cleanly to any nested-clause builder.
  - Grounding: **full text read** (UMD HCIL tech report 92-07, the pre-print of the JASIS paper).

- *Dynamic Queries for Information Exploration: An Implementation and Evaluation* (CHI 1992) —
  Ahlberg, Williamson & Shneiderman. Queries formulated with graphical widgets such as sliders;
  "using sliders for each condition of the query, the user may change the query dynamically and
  receives immediate feedback by the visual representation of the resulting data set". Eighteen
  subjects were significantly faster than with two form-fill-in interfaces.
  - Speaks to: Q4
  - Transfer: partial — establishes continuous effect feedback during composition, but over a visible
    result set rather than over standing future behaviour.
  - Grounding: **weakest in the run.** The author-site PDF is a scan and resisted extraction; the
    quoted span comes from secondary descriptions of the abstract, not from the paper.

- *Think Outside the Search Box: A Comparative Study of Visual and Form-Based Query Builders*
  (arXiv 2205.04212, 2022) — Russell-Rose et al. Lab study comparing a visual-canvas query builder
  with the line-by-line form-based builders that literature-database vendors ship. Results
  "demonstrate the potential of visual interfaces to mitigate some of the shortcomings associated
  with form-based interfaces and encourage more exploratory search behaviour", and "the value of
  having a temporary 'scratch' space in query formulation".
  - Speaks to: Q3
  - Transfer: strong — professional search is the mature end of the same builder surface, and the
    scratch-space finding is a concrete design implication the strawman did not have.
  - Grounding: abstract (arxiv).

## Convergent findings

### The single rule is the unit, and it is separable from the multi-step procedure

Ur et al. put a number on it: 78% of the smart-home behaviours 318 people asked for were one trigger
paired with one action, and only 22% needed several of either. Two years later the same group found
224,590 shared one-rule programs authored by more than 100,000 people. Zhang et al.'s study design
takes the same unit for granted — five tasks, each a small set of if-then rules, none of them a
pipeline. The multi-step procedure is the minority case in the literature that studies this surface,
not the general case the single rule is a degenerate instance of.

*Answers Q2: separable.* The rule is its own object with its own study literature; the procedure is a
different and rarer one.

Papers: Ur et al. 2014; Ur et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2022.

### The difficulty lives on the condition side, and it is a semantic problem, not a syntactic one

Zhang et al.'s anatomy splits the rule three ways — trigger (a statement over *events*), condition (a
Boolean proposition over *states*), action — and the failures cluster entirely in the first two.
Perceiving IF statements as qualifiers on states rather than on events "was revealed in 21 sessions
and all of these 21 sessions ended up with debugging failures". Huang & Cakmak had named the same
confusion seven years earlier and argued it is created by the interface's wording: the
"If ⟨trigger⟩ Then ⟨action⟩" form does not distinguish an instant from an interval, so the fix is
grouping, naming and temporal conjunctions rather than more expressive syntax. Brackenbury et al.
systematise the temporal paradigms a TAP system *could* use, and find the choice governs whether
readers predict the rule's behaviour correctly.

Nothing in the run reports a comparable body of difficulty on the action side. The literature does
split trigger from action — but the split it insists on runs *within* the condition (event against
state), not between condition and action.

*Answers Q1: one move, and it is a condition-composition move.*

Papers: Zhang et al. 2022; Huang & Cakmak 2015; Brackenbury et al. 2019.

### Explicit Boolean syntax is the failure mode; the fix is making combination visible

Young & Shneiderman's numbers are unusually blunt. Of 28 errors made in SQL, 25 were Boolean errors,
against 9 of 12 in the graphical equivalent; composition accuracy more than doubled (4.2 against
1.9 of five). The dominant single error was grouping, not operator choice: fifty parenthesis errors
in SQL against two ordering errors in Filter/Flow. Thirty years on, Russell-Rose et al. find the
line-by-line form-based builders that professional search vendors actually ship carry the same
shortcomings, and that a visual canvas encourages exploratory behaviour a form does not — with a
temporary scratch space as a specific, transferable affordance.

The convergent claim is narrower than "avoid Booleans": people compose Boolean conditions
successfully when the *grouping* is shown rather than punctuated. The clause chip bars and nested
clause trees the corpus's neighbours already use are on the right side of this result.

*Answers Q3 as a partial challenge* — see below.

Papers: Young & Shneiderman 1993; Russell-Rose et al. 2022.

### Effect preview is attested — but grounded in the record, not in synthetic edge cases

Three systems converge on showing the actor the rule's effects rather than its text, and they reach
for three different grounds. Zhang et al.'s Patch Behavior Visualization "shows users what would have
happened in the history if a patch were applied" — the changed rule replayed against the actual
record. EUDebug offers step-by-step simulation of a rule being composed, checked against the rules
already standing. Ahlberg et al.'s dynamic queries give immediate feedback on the result set as each
condition is adjusted — the same move where the rule's object is a visible collection.

All three are grounded in real or observed cases. None samples the boundary the way Noessel's
constrained natural language builder proposes (what newly matches, what barely matches, what barely
fails).

*Answers Q4: attested, with a correction* — see below.

Papers: Zhang et al. 2022; Corno et al. 2019; Ahlberg et al. 1992.

### A rule's meaning is set by the rules already standing

This was not in the strawman. EUDebug's object of analysis is not the rule but "the set of all defined
rules" and the problems their interaction may generate. Zhang et al. coded a matching obstacle,
*Inter-rule Dependency*, in 7 control sessions: participants assumed two rules with opposite actions
would cancel out, assumed listed rules fire in list order, and assumed a WHILE clause attached to one
rule constrained all of them. Each is a false model of how the new rule joins the standing set.

Composing a rule is therefore never composing in isolation, and a preview that shows only the new
rule's own matches is showing the actor less than they need.

Papers: Corno et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022.

## What this challenges

*The edge-case preview is Noessel's proposal, not an established finding.* The strawman took "show
what newly matches, what barely matches, what barely fails" as the move to be documented. The
literature converges instead on replaying the changed rule against the record of what actually
happened, and on simulating it against the rules already standing. Noessel's boundary sampling and
the literature's historical replay answer the same force by different means, and the replay form is
the one with study evidence behind it. The pattern should lead with effects-over-cases as the move
and treat both boundary sampling and historical replay as its forms — not present the boundary list
as the move itself.

*The rule is not the unit of meaning, even though it is the unit of authoring.* The strawman framed
the node around one composed condition. Two independent sources say the composed rule's behaviour is
only determined against the rules already in force, and that actors hold specific false models of how
a new rule joins that set. This is a force the node has to carry, and it is the strongest link back
to `workflow`, which already names the prescription–account gap for procedures.

*"Support Boolean composition" needs qualifying.* The residue attribution treated clause composition
with and/or/not as the invariant. Young & Shneiderman's parenthesis result says the operators are not
the problem and the grouping is: fifty errors against two, in the same task. A builder that exposes
precedence as punctuation reproduces the SQL condition; one that shows grouping structurally does
not. The node's force should be stated as *combination against legibility of grouping*, not as
Booleans being hard.

*`workflow`'s "Foreseeing effect" force is confirmed and mis-scoped.* Its wording — "the hardest part
of authoring a rule is not writing it but estimating what it will do to live cases before it runs" —
is exactly right and is evidenced, but every study grounding it studies single rules, not procedures.
The force belongs to the rule node, with `workflow` citing it.
