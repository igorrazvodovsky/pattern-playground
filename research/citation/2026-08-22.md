# Research: citation of sources in generated output — 2026-08-22

Query: what citations in generated answers do to trust and verification, how often they fail, and where the move ends and its neighbours begin. Generated via `/research-gate`, run while maturing the `citation` seed.

> *Retrieval provenance*. arxiv primary: 19 variant queries, ~140 unique candidates after category filtering; most were NLP method or benchmark papers and were dropped in reranking. Semantic Scholar returned 429 on the first probe and was not retried, so the *Lineage* section is omitted. Backfilled: see [2026-08-29-lineage.md](2026-08-29-lineage.md). Three venue-locked canon items (lateral reading, the authority heuristic, Wikipedia's citation-reason taxonomy) were grounded through publisher pages and WebSearch, not the API — see the *Addendum*. Two papers (Kim et al.; Narayanan Venkit et al.) were read in full from the arxiv HTML; the rest are abstract-only and marked as such. Practice evidence (product teardowns, the Pew click study) is in the sibling note `2026-08-22-practice.md`, not folded in here.

## Context

*Topic*: citation of sources in generated output.

*Interaction*: an actor receives generated claims that carry pointers to the sources behind them — inline markers, chips, footnotes, a source panel — and can verify a claim by following the pointer rather than re-researching it.

*Setting*: conversational and retrieval-augmented surfaces (generative search, assistants, document Q&A) where the actor ends up owning output they did not write. The overreliance cluster (cognitive forcing functions, expressed uncertainty) is the neighbour.

*Questions driving retrieval*:

1. Do citations change whether actors verify, or mainly change how much they trust — and does a citation raise trust even when it is wrong or never followed?
2. What makes a citation verifiable in practice — granularity, quoted span versus whole document, cost of following — and how often do generated citations fail to support the claim they sit on?
3. Does citing constrain what the system can say, and what does that do to the answer?
4. Where is the boundary between citation and neighbouring moves — showing reasoning, expressing uncertainty, marking who wrote what?

## Retrieved papers

1. *Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines* (Liu, Zhang, Liang — 2023). Human audit of Bing Chat, NeevaAI, perplexity.ai, YouChat. [arXiv:2304.09848](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09848)
   - Speaks to: Q2. Defines verifiability as citation recall (every statement supported) plus citation precision (every citation supports its statement). Finds "a mere 51.5% of generated sentences are fully supported by citations and only 74.5% of citations support their associated sentence", and names the resulting "facade of trustworthiness".
   - Transfer: *strong*. The precision/recall split is the vocabulary the pattern needs for its failure modes. Abstract-only.

2. *Search Engines in an AI Era: The False Promise of Factual and Verifiable Source-Cited Responses* (Narayanan Venkit, Laban, Zhou, Mao, et al. — 2024). 21-participant study of answer engines versus traditional search, 16 limitations, 16 design recommendations, automated evaluation of You.com, Perplexity, BingChat. [arXiv:2410.22349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22349)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q2, Q3. Full-text read. Participants hovered an average of 2 sources with an answer engine against 12 with a search engine, and clicked 1 against 4. One participant: "I'll just see that there is a source. That's it. I don't verify it." Engagement rose when the answer contradicted the participant's stance (2.95 hovered vs 1.08). Citation accuracy: You.com 68.3%, BingChat 65.8%, Perplexity 49.0%; 23–32% of statements unsupported by any listed source. Recommendations that bear on the pattern: "implement localized source citation", "implement interactive citation", "ensure listed & cited sources match", "cite all relevant sources", "no answer when info not found".
   - Transfer: *strong*. Same surface, same actor situation.

3. *Fostering Appropriate Reliance on Large Language Models: The Role of Explanations, Sources, and Inconsistencies* (Kim, Vaughan, Liao, Lombrozo — CHI 2025). Think-aloud study plus a pre-registered experiment, N=308, objective questions. [arXiv:2502.08554](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08554)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q4. Full-text read. Sources were three real, relevant, clickable links appended to the answer. Sources cut agreement with incorrect answers from 78.2% to 68.2% and raised agreement with correct ones from 67.2% to 73.4%; explanations raised agreement with both. "189 out of 308 participants never clicked on sources"; clicking happened in 25.8% of instances where sources were present, and clickers were more accurate (60.1% vs 49.2%). "The presence of explanation may reduce users' natural tendency to examine sources, especially when they find the explanation to be of high quality." The authors suggest "placing sources above explanations" and that "if the provided sources are junk or just broken links, then presumably they will not help".
   - Transfer: *strong*. The cleanest causal evidence that sources and explanations pull reliance in different directions.

4. *Human Trust in AI Search: A Large-Scale Experiment* (Li, Aral — 2025). Pre-registered randomised experiment on a U.S.-representative sample, plus ~80,000 real search results across seven countries. [arXiv:2504.06435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06435)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q4. "Reference links and citations significantly increase trust in GenAI, even when those links and citations are incorrect or hallucinated. Uncertainty highlighting … makes us less willing to trust and share generative information whether that confidence is high or low."
   - Transfer: *strong*. The population-scale confirmation of the authority-without-verification force, and a direct contrast with expressed uncertainty. Abstract-only.

5. *Citations and Trust in LLM Generated Responses* (Ding, Facciani, Poudel, Joyce, et al. — AAAI 2025). Live question-answering experiment varying citation count (0, 1, 5) and relevance (relevant vs random). [arXiv:2501.01303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01303)
   - Speaks to: Q1. "A significant increase in trust when citations were present, a result that held true even when the citations were random; we also found a significant decrease in trust when participants checked the citations." Framed as an anti-monitoring model: trust substitutes for checking.
   - Transfer: *strong*. Random citations raising trust is the sharpest form of the finding; the drop on checking shows what following actually does. Abstract-only.

6. *Attribution Gradients: Incrementally Unfolding Citations for Critical Examination of Attributed AI Answers* (Kambhamettu, Hwang, Laban, Head — 2025). Design technique plus lab study. [arXiv:2510.00361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00361)
   - Speaks to: Q2. "Reading the cited sources is costly, and citation links themselves offer little guidance about what evidence they contain." The gradient brings "evidence amounts, supporting/contradictory excerpts, links to source, contextual explanation into one place" and lets the actor "unravel second-degree citations in place"; it "increased the depth of what readers took away from the sources versus a standard citation and document QA design."
   - Transfer: *strong*. The one design-intervention paper in the set; it uses information-foraging vocabulary (scent, prey) explicitly. Abstract-only.

7. *Verified Misguidance: Measuring Structural Citation Failures in Search-Augmented LLMs* (Seo, Jeong, Kim, Jang — 2026). 11,200 real queries, ten models, 761,495 citation pairs scored on intent alignment, source suitability, and answer–source fidelity. [arXiv:2605.28565](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28565)
   - Speaks to: Q2. Names the pattern where "models cite real, accessible sources yet fail along one or more dimensions"; 30.6% of citations distort their sources; faithful models pick unsuitable sources and vice versa. Opens with the premise that users "rarely verify the cited pages themselves."
   - Transfer: *strong*. Establishes that the characteristic failure is a real source that does not say what the claim says, not a dead link. Abstract-only.

8. *Comparing Traditional and LLM-based Search for Consumer Choice: A Randomized Experiment* (Spatharioti, Rothschild, Goldstein, Hofman — 2023). [arXiv:2307.03744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03744)
   - Speaks to: Q4. Overreliance "on incorrect information when the LLM erred"; a second experiment shows "confidence-based highlighting substantially increases the rate at which users spot incorrect information".
   - Transfer: *partial*. Not about citations, but it is the nearest controlled comparison of a neighbouring move (confidence marks) on the same surface, and it gives the overreliance baseline the citation evidence sits against. Abstract-only.

Also read (abstract-only), kept as supporting rather than core:

- *Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools* (Magesh, Surani, Dahl, Suzgun, et al. — 2024). Lexis+ AI and Westlaw AI-Assisted Research "hallucinate between 17% and 33% of the time" despite RAG; the paper's "misgrounded" category is the same failure Seo et al. measure at scale. [arXiv:2405.20362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362)
- *Cited but Not Verified: Parsing and Evaluating Source Attribution in LLM Deep Research Agents* (Onweller et al. — 2026). Frontier models keep "link validity above 94% and relevance above 80%, yet achieve only 39–77% factual accuracy" — links work, claims don't. [arXiv:2605.06635](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06635)
- *Generation-Time vs. Post-hoc Citation* (Saxena et al. — 2025). "A consistent trade-off between coverage and citation correctness"; generation-time citing "prioritize[s] precision at the cost of coverage and speed". [arXiv:2509.21557](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21557)
- *Verifiable Generation with Subsentence-Level Fine-Grained Citations* (Cao, Wang — 2024) and *Learning Fine-Grained Grounded Citations* (Huang et al. — 2024): the granularity line — sentence-level citations lack "specificity about which parts of a sentence are backed by the cited sources". [arXiv:2406.06125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06125), [arXiv:2408.04568](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04568)
- *Verifiable by Design: Aligning Language Models to Quote from Pre-Training Data* (Zhang et al. — 2024): verbatim quoting as a route to verifiability. [arXiv:2404.03862](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03862)
- *Measuring Attribution in Natural Language Generation Models* (Rashkin et al. — 2021): the AIS ("attributable to identified sources") definition that later attribution work builds on. [arXiv:2112.12870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12870)
- *Not All Uncertainty Is Equal* (Villavicencio, Pan, Wang — 2026): token-level uncertainty raised agreement, response-level uncertainty lowered self-confidence — granularity changes what a mark does. [arXiv:2605.28571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28571)
- *The Decision to Verify* (Yazan, Situmeang, Verberne — 2026): "the decision to verify is driven primarily by existing user perceptions … rather than answer properties"; reliance persisted even with web search one click away. [arXiv:2605.28498](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28498)
- *Generative Echo Chamber?* (Sharma, Liao, Xiao — CHI 2024): conversational search narrows inquiry; pairs with the 2-vs-12 hovering result. [arXiv:2402.05880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05880)
- *Citation Needed: A Taxonomy and Algorithmic Assessment of Wikipedia's Verifiability* (Redi, Fetahu, Morgan, Taraborelli — 2019): an editor-derived taxonomy of *why* a statement needs an inline citation. [arXiv:1902.11116](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.11116)

## Convergent findings

### 1. The mark of a citation raises trust on its own, independent of whether the citation is right or followed

*Synthesis*. Three independent experiments agree: Li & Aral (citations raise trust "even when … incorrect or hallucinated"), Ding et al. (random citations raise trust as much as relevant ones), and the Narayanan Venkit participant who sees "that there is a source. That's it." Seo et al. build their whole benchmark on the premise that cited pages are rarely visited. Liu et al. call the result a "facade of trustworthiness". This is the seed's second resulting clause, and the evidence says it is the *dominant* effect for most actors, not a side effect.

*Papers*: Li & Aral 2025; Ding et al. 2025; Narayanan Venkit et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Seo et al. 2026.

*Disagreement*: none on direction. Kim et al. find sources *reduce* overreliance on wrong answers — but their sources were real and relevant; nobody has tested whether real-but-misgrounded citations also reduce it.

### 2. Following is rare, and it is where the value is

*Synthesis*. Kim et al.: 61% of participants never clicked a source; 25.8% of source-present instances drew a click; clickers were 11 points more accurate. Narayanan Venkit: 2 sources hovered, 1 clicked, against 12 and 4 with a search engine. Ding: trust *drops* when citations are checked — checking is the moment the authority effect gives way to evidence. Yazan et al.: whether someone verifies is set more by their prior disposition than by anything in the answer. The design lever is therefore the cost and scent of following, not the presence of the mark.

*Papers*: Kim et al. 2025; Narayanan Venkit et al. 2024; Ding et al. 2025; Yazan et al. 2026; Sharma et al. 2024.

### 3. The characteristic failure is a real source that does not say what the claim says

*Synthesis*. Dead links are rare (Onweller: >94% resolve). What fails is support: Liu's 51.5% sentence recall and 74.5% citation precision; Narayanan Venkit's 49–68% citation accuracy; Seo's 30.6% distortion; Magesh's 17–33% hallucination in tools marketed as hallucination-free. Seo et al. name it "verified misguidance" and add a structural tension — models that quote faithfully pick unsuitable sources, and vice versa. A pointer to a whole document therefore under-specifies what the actor should check.

*Papers*: Liu et al. 2023; Narayanan Venkit et al. 2024; Seo et al. 2026; Magesh et al. 2024; Onweller et al. 2026.

### 4. Verifiability lives in the span and the excerpt, not the document

*Synthesis*. The granularity line (Cao & Wang; Huang et al.) argues that sentence-level citation "lack[s] specificity about which parts … are backed"; Zhang et al. make verbatim quotation the verifiable unit; Kambhamettu et al. bring "supporting/contradictory excerpts" in place and show deeper reading as a result; Narayanan Venkit's recommendations ask for "localized" and "interactive" citation. The converging design move: a citation should expose the passage that supports the claim where the claim is, and let the document be the second hop.

*Papers*: Kambhamettu et al. 2025; Cao & Wang 2024; Huang et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Narayanan Venkit et al. 2024.

### 5. Citing changes what gets generated — coverage against correctness

*Synthesis*. Saxena et al. find a "consistent trade-off between coverage and citation correctness": citing during generation buys precision and loses coverage. Narayanan Venkit's "cherry-picking" limitation and the "no answer when info not found" recommendation are the interface-side view of the same thing. The seed's third clause (generation leans toward the retrievable) holds, with a sharper shape: strict citing makes the system say less, and what it says is shaped by what the retriever found.

*Papers*: Saxena et al. 2025; Narayanan Venkit et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024.

### 6. Sources, explanations, and uncertainty marks pull reliance in different directions

*Synthesis*. Kim et al.: explanations raise reliance on right and wrong answers alike; sources lower reliance on wrong ones; an explanation suppresses source-checking. Li & Aral: citations raise trust, uncertainty highlighting lowers it whether confidence is high or low. Spatharioti: confidence highlighting helps people spot errors. Villavicencio: the granularity of an uncertainty mark flips its effect. These are not interchangeable "transparency features"; each is a distinct move with its own effect on reliance, which is the argument for keeping citation, transparent reasoning, and expressed uncertainty as separate patterns joined by `complements` edges.

*Papers*: Kim et al. 2025; Li & Aral 2025; Spatharioti et al. 2023; Villavicencio et al. 2026.

## Addendum: canon reads (venue-locked)

Grounded via publisher pages and WebSearch rather than the API; abstract- and summary-level only, no full-text reads.

- *Lateral Reading and the Nature of Expertise* (Wineburg, McGrew — Teachers College Record 121(11), 2019). Professional fact-checkers "read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening up new browser tabs in order to judge the credibility of the original site", reaching "more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time" than historians or undergraduates who stayed on the page. [journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016146811912101102)
  - Implication: verification is done by *leaving*. A citation is the affordance for leaving; a design that keeps the actor inside the answer (hover cards, excerpts) trades lateral reading for convenience. Kambhamettu's in-place excerpts and Rao's "opening in a new tab collapses the rhetoric" (`references/rhetoric-of-hyperlink.md`) are the two sides of that trade.
- *The MAIN Model: A Heuristic Approach to Understanding Technology Effects on Credibility* (Sundar — in Metzger & Flanagin, *Digital Media, Youth, and Credibility*, 2008). Interface cues trigger credibility heuristics — authority, bandwagon, prominence — without the content being evaluated. [issuelab.org PDF](https://www.issuelab.org/resources/875/875.pdf)
  - Implication: a citation marker is an authority cue in Sundar's sense; clusters 1 and 2 above are the MAIN model's prediction borne out on generated text. Gives the pattern a pre-LLM explanation for why the mark works without the follow.
- *Citation Needed* (Redi et al. — WWW 2019, arXiv above). Wikipedia editors' taxonomy of reasons a statement needs an inline citation (quotations, statistics, scientific and historical claims, contentious or surprising statements, and so on).
  - Implication: not every claim needs a pointer. A citation policy — which statements earn a mark — is a design decision the generated-answer products have not made explicit; Narayanan Venkit's "missing citations for key claims" is the symptom.

## What this challenges

- *"Verification collapses from re-research to spot-checking."* Only for the minority who follow. Kim's 61% never-clickers and Pew's 1% in-summary click rate (practice note) say the typical outcome is trust without verification. The resulting clause should be conditional: the collapse is available, not delivered.
- *The authority-without-verification force is listed as one consequence among three.* The evidence makes it the primary effect of the mark for most actors; the pattern's forces section should carry it as the central tension, and the solution should be stated so that it answers it (span-level pointers, excerpt in place, sources placed before explanation).
- *The seed's forms (inline markers, chips, source browser, retrieval-context preview) omit the form the literature singles out* — the supporting span or quotation surfaced at the claim. Shape of AI's catalogue lists it ("inline highlights", "direct quotations"), and Kambhamettu's study is the only controlled evidence that a form changes reading depth.
- *The seed's lead says "verify by following rather than re-researching."* Lateral reading says following is itself a form of re-research — a short one. The pattern's claim is better put as lowering the cost of checking a specific claim, not removing research.
- *The existing CFF research note treats verifiability as a precondition that tasks have or lack (Fok & Weld).* Citation is the move that manufactures verifiability for a task that otherwise lacks it — which makes it a precursor to cognitive forcing functions, not just a neighbour.
