## Context

From `query.yml`: an actor drags an element to a destination whose meaning
performs a semantic write (card → lane writes the grouping attribute; chip →
overview surfaces it); computed destinations refuse audibly; every drag has a
non-pointer twin. Deciding: pattern node, mechanism annotation, or both.

Questions:
1. One named pattern or split (reorder / transfer / upload) in the corpora?
2. Is the non-pointer twin normative (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.7)?
3. Does direct-manipulation theory name "spatial proxy for a semantic write"?
4. Operation or action — where does dnd attention-demand sit?
5. Canvas/data-viz "drag" (pan, marquee, brush) — merge or keep parallel?

## Retrieved papers

- *A comparison of input devices in elemental pointing and dragging tasks*
  (MacKenzie, Sellen & Buxton, CHI 1991) — dragging carries ~3× the error rate
  of pointing (mouse 10.8% vs 3.5%; trackball 17.3% vs 3.9%) and lower Fitts'
  bandwidth; origin of the state-0/1/2 input model where dragging is "State 2",
  constrained by "the extent of muscle and limb interaction required to
  maintain State 2 motion". Full-text read, https://www.yorku.ca/mack/CHI91.html
  - Speaks to: Q4. Transfer: strong — motor cost of the gesture itself.
- *FluidViews: Adaptive Drag-and-Drop Token Filters for Heterogeneous
  Multi-View Visual Analytics* (arXiv:2606.18260, 2026) — "Drag-as-Filter
  allows analysts to pick up a mark and drop it onto another view to apply
  context-sensitive filters in place — no menus, panels, or modal dialogs
  required"; filters as "first-class, manipulable objects".
  - Speaks to: Q3. Transfer: strong — near-twin of the attribute-chip demo:
    dragging a representation into a view performs a semantic write on it.
- *Drag, Infer, Reproject: Grounding LLMs through Spatial Interaction for
  Image Clustering* (CriterionSI, arXiv:2606.28517, 2026) — "semantic
  interaction methods encode users' clustering criterion … from direct
  manipulation"; incremental drags are read as expressions of an emerging
  semantic criterion.
  - Speaks to: Q3. Transfer: partial — sits in the *semantic interaction*
    lineage (Endert et al.), the modern name for drags-as-semantic-input;
    here the write is inferred rather than destination-defined.
- *Inventions on Drag and Drop in GUI* (arXiv:1404.7121, 2014) — patent
  survey; its framing is direct evidence for destination-carried meaning:
  "dropping on a folder may copy or move … a remote FTP location may upload …
  a printer icon may print … the trash can may delete".
  - Speaks to: Q3. Transfer: partial — catalogues the polysemy that makes
    refusal/signifier design the hard part.
- *Animated Drag and Drop Interaction for Dynamic Multidimensional Graphs*
  (arXiv:1902.01564, 2019) — dnd as an analysis interaction over graph views,
  augmented with controllable animation for comparison tasks.
  - Speaks to: Q1 (coarseness). Transfer: weak-moderate.
- *HoloSpot* (arXiv:2410.11110, 2024) — MR "dollhouse" dnd translated into
  real robot actions; the drop is a proxy for a physical operation.
  - Speaks to: Q3. Transfer: weak — but the extreme case of the proxy claim.
- *Building keyboard accessible Drag and Drop* (Somani et al., ASSETS 2014,
  demo) — "it may take a lot of effort for web developers … to build complex
  keyboard accessible DnD components"; abstract-only.
  - Speaks to: Q2. Transfer: partial — the cost side of the twin requirement.
- *Keyboard and mouse errors due to motor disabilities* (Trewin & Pain,
  IJHCS 1999) — empirical lineage behind treating drag as a dexterity
  barrier; metadata grounded via S2, abstract paywalled — flagged.
  - Speaks to: Q2. Transfer: partial — the motivation SC 2.5.7 later codified.

## Canon reads

### Pattern corpora (Q1)

- *Tidwell, Designing Interfaces* (1st–3rd ed.; Wayback + O'Reilly preview) —
  no dnd pattern in any edition. Dragging appears inside patterns (Movable
  Panels: "consider putting a 'handle' on each piece that the user can grab").
  3rd ed. files "Drag-and-Drop" in ch. 8 *Doing Things* as an action-invocation
  method in a run beside "keyboard actions" and "drop-down menus".
- *van Welie* (Wayback index, ~130 patterns) — no dnd pattern; even List
  Builder never uses the word "drag". Notably *names the pan move* (Map
  Navigator, Panning Navigator) while leaving dnd unnamed.
- *NN/g, "Drag-and-Drop: How to Design for Ease of Use"* — one technique with
  enumerated uses ("grouping, reordering, moving, or resizing objects"). The
  signifier problem stated: a dnd signifier "has to signal two functions —
  (1) that the item is 'grabbable' and (2) what dragging it somewhere will
  accomplish." Recommends precise non-drag companions (arrow keys, menus).
- *Apple HIG, "Drag and drop"* — one behavior deliberately unified across
  containers, apps, devices ("people select content in one location, called
  the source, and drop it in another, called the destination"); within- vs
  cross-container handled as move-vs-copy semantics of the same behavior.
  "Offer alternative ways to accomplish drag-and-drop actions."
- *Material* — no design-guidance dnd pattern; drag lives in the gesture
  taxonomy (drag = "fine gesture, slower, more controlled, typically has an
  on-screen target" vs swipe). The Angular CDK *code* layer splits
  reorder-within-list vs transfer-between-lists.
- *Atlassian pragmatic-drag-and-drop design guidelines* — one capability
  sliced by *phase* (before drag / start / while dragging / on drop /
  accessible controls) and affordance tier (always-visible handle vs
  hover-revealed vs implied draggables), not by use case.
- *Scott & Neil, Designing Web Interfaces* (2009; Wayback) — the strongest
  split: chapter "Drag and Drop" with five named patterns — Drag and Drop
  Module, List, Object, Action, Collection — each with considerations
  including "Non-drag and drop alternative" and "Hinting at drag and drop".
  Selection-by-pointer is carved into a separate chapter (Direct Selection).
- *UI-Patterns.com* — single "Drag and drop" pattern: "The user needs to
  perform operations on one or more objects by moving them from one place to
  another"; sub-uses mentioned, not split.

Tally: one coarse thing — Apple, NN/g, UI-Patterns, Atlassian. Split by use
case — Scott & Neil (and Angular CDK at code level). Absent as a pattern —
Tidwell, van Welie, Material. Upload appears in no corpus as a separate
pattern.

### Accessibility norms (Q2)

- *WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.7 Dragging Movements* (Level AA, new in 2.2), normative:
  "All functionality that uses a dragging movement for operation can be
  achieved by a single pointer without dragging, unless dragging is essential
  or the functionality is determined by the user agent and not modified by
  the author." Understanding doc: the required alternative is *single-pointer*
  — "Achieving keyboard equivalence for a dragging operation does not
  automatically meet this success criterion". Acceptable forms: tap-then-tap,
  adjacent move buttons, a pop-up move menu.
- *WCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard* (Level A) independently requires a keyboard path to
  the drag's *outcome* — dnd is endpoint-, not path-dependent, so it does not
  fall under 2.1.1's path-dependence exception.
- *ARIA/APG* — `aria-grabbed`/`aria-dropeffect` deprecated since ARIA 1.1
  (2017), "expected to be replaced by a new feature in a future version" that
  has never materialized; the APG patterns index has no dnd, sortable, or
  reorder pattern. A standards vacuum for the gesture itself.
- *Design systems* — Atlassian: "Not all people can successfully complete
  pointer based drag operations. Provide other ways … buttons, menus, and
  forms"; recommends *against* arrow-key dragging for most experiences.
  GitHub Primer: multiple parallel paths (keyboard dragging, click-and-drag,
  move dialog) + live-region choreography; GitHub's engineering write-up
  calls dnd a "high-risk pattern", notes arrow-key dragging collides with
  screen-reader navigation, and found users across abilities preferring the
  dialog. Salesforce (Hausler) is the minority pole: make the drag itself
  keyboard-operable (grab/move/drop + announcements) across four cases.

### Direct-manipulation theory (Q3, Q4)

- *Shneiderman 1983* (full text, UMD) — direct manipulation defined by
  continuous representation, "physical actions … instead of complex syntax",
  and "rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose impact … is
  immediately visible" (plus a usually-dropped layered-learning bullet).
  Names what dnd replaces (command syntax), not the gesture-meaning mapping.
- *Hutchins, Hollan & Norman 1985* (full text) — the mapping has a name:
  *articulatory directness*. "Articulatory distance reflects the relationship
  between the physical form of an expression in the interaction language and
  its meaning." The dnd case is addressed head-on: pointing devices "support
  the articulatory directness of input for tasks that can be represented
  spatially. The mouse is useful … because we map so many kinds of
  relationships (even ones that are not intrinsically spatial) on to spatial
  metaphors." Also Draper's *inter-referential I/O* — "an output expression
  can serve as a component of an input expression" — which is exactly what a
  drop target is.
- *Gentner & Nielsen 1996, "The Anti-Mac Interface"* (full text, nngroup) —
  the failure mode of destination-carried meaning: metaphor "can also cripple
  the interface with irrelevant limitations", and "the dark side of a direct
  manipulation interface is that you have to directly manipulate everything."
  (The trash-ejects-disk example is *not* in Anti-Mac; it is grounded only in
  the practitioner-tier Interface Hall of Shame — cite accordingly.)
- *Buxton, "Chunking and Phrasing"* (full text, billbuxton.com) — muscular
  tension phrases compound tasks into one chunk: "the 'glue' that ties the
  three sub-tasks together is the tension of holding the mouse button down …
  errors of syntax and mode errors are virtually impossible to make", and the
  held tension "gives constant feedback that we are in a temporary state, or
  mode." "Any concept … that can be described in a single word or phrase
  should be able to be articulated by a single gesture."
- *Kaptelinin (Encyclopedia of HCI ch., for Leontiev)* — "Operations are
  routine processes … oriented toward the conditions … People are typically
  not aware of their operations"; an operation "can be a result of
  step-by-step automatization of an originally conscious action", and on
  breakdown is "transformed into conscious actions again."

### Canvas vocabulary (Q5)

- *d3* splits the word across three modules with disjoint stated purposes:
  d3-drag ("manipulating spatial elements: … 'drag' the object to a new
  location, and release to 'drop'"), d3-brush ("interactive specification of
  a one- or two-dimensional selected region using a pointing gesture"),
  d3-zoom ("click-and-drag to pan (translate)"). Same physical gesture, three
  semantics, three vocabularies.
- *tldraw* — "brushing" (marquee), "translating" (move) are select-tool
  states; panning is a separate *hand tool*.
- *Figma* — marquee vs move disambiguated by *origin point*: drag starting on
  empty canvas selects, on an object moves.
- *Material m1* — pan defined apart from long-press drag-to-move.
- *van Welie* — names Panning Navigator as its own pattern while dnd is absent.

## Convergent findings

### 1. One coarse node is the corpus norm; Scott & Neil's five supply the variant names

No corpus splits dnd the way the strawman feared (reorder/transfer/upload as
separate patterns). Libraries either leave it unnamed (Tidwell, van Welie,
Material), name it once (UI-Patterns, NN/g, Apple, Atlassian), or — uniquely —
split by use case (Scott & Neil: Module / List / Object / Action /
Collection). Upload is nobody's variant. Tidwell 3rd ed. filing dnd beside
keyboard actions as an action-invocation method is direct precedent for
placing it at the same level as Keyboard shortcuts.
Papers: Tidwell, van Welie, NN/g, Apple HIG, Material, Atlassian,
Scott & Neil, UI-Patterns.
*Draft project takeaway*: one coarse `drag-and-drop` node stands on corpus
precedent; borrow Scott & Neil's carving as within-page variant names, and
drop "upload" from the variant list until practice demands it.

### 2. The twin is doubly normative — and "non-pointer twin" understates it

WCAG 2.1.1 (A) requires a keyboard path to every drag outcome; WCAG 2.2 SC
2.5.7 (AA) *separately* requires a single-pointer non-dragging path, and a
keyboard alternative alone does not satisfy it. With ARIA's dnd states
deprecated since 2017 and no APG pattern, the ecosystem converges on
alternative controls (menus, buttons, dialogs) over AT-operable drags —
Atlassian recommends against arrow-key dragging; GitHub found users across
abilities preferring the move dialog. A "Move to…" menu satisfies both norms
at once.
Papers: WCAG 2.2 + Understanding, ARIA 1.3 draft, APG, Atlassian, Primer,
GitHub blog, Salesforce, ASSETS '14, Trewin & Pain 1999.
*Draft project takeaway*: state the twin as two obligations, not one — every
non-essential drag needs a keyboard twin (A) and a drag-free pointer twin
(AA); the demos' menu twin discharges both, the chip toggle likewise.

### 3. Theory already names the framing: articulatory directness, leaning on designed metaphor

"Spatial proxy for a semantic operation" is Hutchins/Hollan/Norman's
articulatory directness, with their sharpening: when the meaning is not
intrinsically spatial, directness is achieved by *mapping the relationship
onto a spatial metaphor* — a designed artifact that can overload (Gentner &
Nielsen's metaphor critique; the folkloric trash-eject). Draper's
inter-referential I/O names the drop-target move exactly: output serving as
input. The modern research lineage is *semantic interaction* (CriterionSI,
FluidViews) — drags read as expressions of analytic intent.
Papers: Hutchins/Hollan/Norman, Shneiderman, Gentner & Nielsen, FluidViews,
CriterionSI, Inventions on DnD.
*Draft project takeaway*: weave "articulatory directness" and "the destination
is output serving as input" into the page's vocabulary via references/, and
keep the refusal behaviour argued from metaphor-overload (one destination,
one meaning), not just politeness.

### 4. Phrased like an operation, priced like an action

Buxton's tension-phrasing binds press–move–release into one chunk whose mode
errors are "virtually impossible", and activity theory supplies the
automatization mechanism — the operation classification holds for the skilled
actor. But the chunk has measurably elevated motor cost (dragging ~3× the
error rate and lower Fitts' bandwidth than pointing), and NN/g's two-part
signifier problem means target legibility stays focal during the drag in a
way keyboard shortcuts never are. Operation level, with the cost caveat
owned in the page.
Papers: Buxton, Kaptelinin, MacKenzie/Sellen/Buxton, NN/g.
*Draft project takeaway*: classify `activityLevel: operation` on Buxton's
phrasing + AT automatization, and let the page carry the counterweight: the
gesture is one chunk but an expensive one, which is why the twin is also the
ergonomic fast path, not just the accessible one.

### 5. Canvas "drag" is a different move wearing the same word

The tooling itself keeps them apart: d3's three-module split, tldraw's
brush/translate/hand-tool states, Figma's origin-point disambiguation,
Material's pan-vs-drag distinction, van Welie naming pan but not dnd. Nothing
in any corpus merges drag-to-pan or marquee with drag-and-drop.
Papers: d3 docs, tldraw docs, Figma help, Material m1, van Welie.
*Draft project takeaway*: keep pan/marquee/brush parallel and link from the
dnd page's tangential edges (pan-and-zoom, selection) — the word is shared,
the move is not; d3's module boundary is the citable evidence.
