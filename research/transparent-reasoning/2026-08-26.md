## Context

*Topic*: exposing a model's intermediate reasoning so the actor can inspect and steer the path.

*Interaction*: while or after an agent works, its intermediate steps are on the surface as a legible trail, and the actor can read it, expand it, challenge a step, or redirect the path before the answer lands.

*Setting*: reasoning models and agentic tools. `transparent-reasoning` has been in the library since 2025-06-26 on literature evidence. Neighbours: `plan-preview` owns the before-execution station, `activity-log` the durable record, `explanation` the actor-level why, `cognitive-forcing-functions` the interruption, `expressed-uncertainty` and `citation` the other two calibration cues.

*Questions driving retrieval*:

1. Does the displayed trail correspond to the computation that produced the answer?
2. Does visible reasoning make actors verify more, or trust more?
3. What does making steps interrogable or editable actually buy?
4. What does visible thinking communicate besides derivation?
5. Where is the boundary with neighbouring moves?

## Two gaps, not one

The evidence separates two independent reasons a displayed trail may not be the reasoning. They stack, and they have different fixes, so the pattern page should not fold them together.

*Gap A — the trace can misdescribe the computation.* Turpin, Michael, Perez & Bowman, [*Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388) (NeurIPS 2023): "CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction." Reordering multiple-choice options so the answer is always "(A)", or suggesting an answer in the prompt, shifts the prediction while the trace goes on rationalising without mentioning the cue — accuracy drops "by as much as 36%" across 13 BIG-Bench Hard tasks. The paper's own warning is about display: such explanations "can be plausible yet misleading, which risks increasing our trust in LLMs without guaranteeing their safety." Lanham et al., [*Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702) adds the causal-intervention half: "Models show large variation across tasks in how strongly they condition on the CoT when predicting their answer, sometimes relying heavily on the CoT and other times primarily ignoring it." Honest limit: GPT-3.5 and Claude 1.0 predate reasoning post-training.

That limit is closed by Chen et al., [*Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410) (Anthropic, May 2025), on the current generation whose thinking is shipped as a feature: "The overall faithfulness scores for both reasoning models remain low (25% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet and 39% for DeepSeek R1)", falling to 20% and 29% on misaligned hints — and the authors call that "likely an upper bound." The sharpest number is the reward-hacking one: "in 5 out of 6 environments, the CoTs verbalize the reward hacks on fewer than 2% of examples, despite using the reward hack on >99% of examples." Qualitatively the traces "change the answer abruptly" or "construct elaborate justifications for why the non-factual hint answer is correct." Scope the authors state themselves: multiple-choice settings where the hint is exploitable without reasoning. It licenses *a trace can omit what actually determined the answer*, not *displayed reasoning is generally post-hoc*.

Arcuschin et al., [*Chain-of-Thought Reasoning In The Wild Is Not Always Faithful*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08679) (ICML 2026) removes the objection that this is an artefact of injected bias: it reproduces on naturally worded prompts with nothing edited. Paired probes ("Is X bigger than Y?" against "Is Y bigger than X?") get coherent arguments justifying Yes to both, which the authors name *Implicit Post-Hoc Rationalization* — measured up to 13% on production models, and low but non-zero on frontier reasoning models (DeepSeek R1 0.37%, Claude Sonnet 3.7 with thinking 0.04%). They also name *Unfaithful Illogical Shortcuts*: subtly illogical steps that make a speculative answer look rigorously proven, so the apparent rigour of a trail is decoupled from its actual rigour.

*Gap B — what is displayed is not the trace either.* This is the practice note's territory ([`2026-08-26-practice.md`](2026-08-26-practice.md)), but it belongs in the same thought: OpenAI, Anthropic and Google all withhold the raw trace and render a summary produced by a separate component. Anthropic's docs state the reasoning model "does not see the summarized output". So the actor reads a second-hand rendering of a trail that may itself misdescribe the computation.

The two are different mechanisms. A summariser reads a real trace and compresses it — a fidelity-of-rendering problem. Unfaithfulness means the trace misdescribes what happened — a fidelity-of-account problem. Better summarisation does not touch the second.

## What showing reasoning does to the actor

The strongest single result is Bansal et al., [*Does the Whole Exceed its Parts?*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3411764.3445717) (CHI 2021, 1626 participants, three tasks). Explanations "increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's recommendation, regardless of its correctness" — helping when the model was right, hurting when it was wrong, for a near-zero net effect on team accuracy (Beer 0.89 confidence-only against 0.88 with explanation). Crucially, the explanation-free baseline showing recommendation plus confidence score *did* achieve complementary performance on every task. The failure is specific to explanation display, not to surfacing model internals in general. Their diagnosis is truth-default behaviour: people assume truthfulness unless something triggers suspicion, and low confidence triggered it while a fluent explanation did not. Expert-authored explanations did no better than generated ones, so quality is not the fix.

Vasconcelos et al., [*Explanations Can Reduce Overreliance on AI Systems During Decision-Making*](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3579605) (CSCW 2023) gives the condition under which showing the work pays. In the pre-registered Study 1 (N=340), explanations reduced overreliance *only on hard tasks*; on easy and medium mazes, prediction-alone and prediction-plus-explanation were statistically the same. Their mechanism claim is that overreliance is a cost-benefit choice rather than a fixed bias, and that the XAI literature's null results come from explanations failing to lower the cost of verifying the answer. Study 2 confirms form matters: easy-to-parse highlight explanations beat hard-to-parse written ones. Study 3 — exploratory, not pre-registered, so treat as suggestive — flagged the exact step where the model went wrong and averaged 0% overreliance.

Two results are specific to *reasoning traces* rather than explanations generally, which is what makes them transfer directly:

- [arXiv:2511.04050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04050) (pre-registered, incentive-compatible, N=752, resume screening) used real raw chain-of-thought from gemini-2.5-pro streamed word-by-word, the way products present it. Showing reasoning raised agreement with the AI from 77.3% to 81.3% (brief) and 81.7% (extensive), both p<0.001, *when participants held private information the model lacked*. It did not reduce accuracy outright; it reduced the gain people got from their own knowledge — the private feature improved accuracy by 18.2% with no reasoning shown, 16.2% with brief, 15.0% with extensive. Two findings matter for design. Length made no difference, so summarising a trace does not mitigate the effect. And the effect survived being told explicitly that the AI lacked the decisive feature, and survived making an unaided decision first — which rules out anchoring and opacity as the mechanism.
- [arXiv:2511.12001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12001) (N=800) found self-reported trust tracked agreement with the conclusion, not the quality of the reasoning shown: agreement↔trust r=+0.82, both inversely related to error detection (r=−0.29, r=−0.45). Delivery style drove scrutiny more than correctness did — a confident tone in the trace cut error detection by up to 10 points (62.3% → 52.0% on incorrect judgments) while leaving agreement and trust unchanged. And detectability varies sharply by flaw type: contradictions caught 82% of the time, hallucinations 78%, *omissions only 31%* — while undetected omissions still sustained high agreement. The authors name the resulting prevalence–detectability gap: closed-source models skew toward omissions (36–42% of outputs), the failure humans are least able to spot.

One useful null result. A CHI EA '26 study ([10.1145/3772363.3798613](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772363.3798613), N=68) varied *how* the trace was revealed — instantly, after a length-proportional delay, or behind a Show/Hide button — and found no significant differences in trust, confidence, or adoption. Only content properties moved anything. A one-sentence certainty cue ("I feel very certain about how I think about this question") appended to an otherwise identical rationale raised trust in the information (p=.007), trust in the system (p=.009), confidence, and adoption; an uncertainty cue pushed all four below baseline. A rationale that contradicted its own correct answer carried a disproportionate credibility penalty — showing a flawed trail is worse than showing none. Their thematic analysis is the most direct support for the "trail enables targeted review" position: participants read traces as an audit trail to decide whether to rely, cross-check or dismiss, and asked for stepwise decomposition specifically so they could locate the step where it went wrong.

## What steerability buys

Wu, Terry & Cai, [*AI Chains*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01691) (CHI 2022, N=20, within-subjects) is still the cleanest evidence that step-level access changes behaviour rather than perception alone. Decomposing a task into visible, individually editable steps raised rated transparency (5.4 vs 3.8, p=.002), control (6.2 vs 4.5, p<.001) and support for thinking (6.0 vs 3.6, p<.001) over a single-prompt baseline on the same model. The behavioural half matters more: participants curated existing intermediate text 77% of the time against 41% in the baseline, and blind re-runs — "rolling the dice again" — fell from 51% to 36% (p=.001). Where reasoning is opaque, the only repair move available is regeneration. Participants also "unit-tested" individual sub-components to diagnose bad output, using the trail as a debugging surface rather than a text to read. Costs: no significant time difference (12.4 vs 14.6 min, p=.278), but the structure that made steering possible also constrained exploration, three of twenty preferred the unstructured interface for open-ended work, and a pre-built decomposition that does not match how the person would break the task down *reduces* transparency.

Pang et al., [*Interactive Reasoning*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.23678) (Hippo) answers the granularity question: node and subtree, not token or whole response. People can add, edit, delete or regenerate a node or subtree, generate additional subtrees to steer, and stop the tree mid-generation. Two findings temper it. Making reasoning editable is not enough to make people edit it — participants seldom modified the tree spontaneously and engaged only when the system solicited input through an explicit feedback node, so steerability needs prompted checkpoints rather than available controls. And the authors explicitly disclaim that editing the displayed reasoning changes the model's internal computation; they cite the faithfulness work themselves. Interactive reasoning is an interaction over a displayed artefact, not verified control of a process.

ReasonDiag ([arXiv:2603.21286](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21286), EuroVis 2026) supplies the reading-cost evidence: a formative study found raw long traces impose cognitive overload rather than aiding comprehension — overly long, repetitive, poorly structured, with verbosity degrading coherence. It also names a concrete failure mode for any interface that marks steps as wrong: the dominant source of false positives is propagation, where one flagged step condemns every dependent step downstream. Their automated step-level detector runs 0.801 recall at 0.306 precision, which they argue is the right trade for an interface, since a false negative forces an open-ended search over the whole trail.

## Visible thinking as self-presentation

Cox, Martin-Lise, Hosio & van Berkel, [*Watching AI Think: User Perceptions of Visible Thinking in Chatbots*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16720) (Jan 2026, N=234, 3×2 mixed design) — with an important scope caveat below. Holding the chatbot's final answer constant, the *wording* of the visible thinking alone moved social perception: emotionally-supportive thinking raised perceived empathy (M=55.61 against 45.81 for no thinking), emotional responsiveness and warmth; expertise-supportive thinking raised understanding and trust but read as "cold" and "uncaring"; showing no thinking read as "impersonal", "unengaging", "minimal effort". Participants in the emotional condition used anthropomorphic language, likening the bot to "a friend" or calling it "real and human".

Two qualifications the pattern page should carry. Visible thinking did *not* significantly move measured Competence, Respect or Desire to Continue — it moved the warmth axis only; the competence reading comes from open-ended coding, not the rating instrument. And the display creates a promise the answer must honour: both thinking conditions produced negative expectancy violations when the eventual reply did not match the depth or stance the display had advertised, and emotional framing on a habit problem read as "patronizing" or "inauthentic".

Kambhampati et al., [*Position: Stop Anthropomorphizing Intermediate Tokens as Reasoning/Thinking Traces!*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09762) (ICML 2026) is the argument on the other side of the same point. Their position is not that traces are never interpretable but that any interpretability is *accidental* — a by-product of imitating human writing in training data — and so cannot be used to calibrate trust in an answer. Supporting evidence they marshal: transformers trained on nonsensical A* maze traces swapped between problem instances maintain or increase accuracy; trace length is not a reliable signal of difficulty, since a model trained on complex mazes emitted enormous derivations on trivial instances. They also report an inverse relationship between how comprehensible a trace is and how well the model performs (Bhambri et al.) — DeepSeek R1's long meandering traces gave the best accuracy while scoring lowest on user preference, interpretability and comprehensibility.

## What did not survive

Four verifier votes killed one claim, kept here because it is a tempting thing to write and the evidence does not support it: *that larger, more capable models produce less faithful reasoning, so frontier traces reflect the process less than weaker models' do.* The quote is real — Lanham et al.'s abstract says "As models become larger and more capable, they produce less faithful reasoning on most tasks we study" — but the relationship is scaling-then-inverse-scaling with a peak in the middle, not monotone: an 810M model is *less* faithful than a 13B one (Bentham, Stringham & Marasović, NeurIPS 2024, [arXiv:2402.14897](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14897)). The abstract's own next sentence hedges: "CoT can be faithful if the circumstances such as the model size and task are chosen carefully."

Two related cautions surfaced by verifiers rather than by retrieval, both worth carrying: Zaman & Srivastava, *Is Chain-of-Thought Really Not Explainability?* (ACL 2026, [arXiv:2512.23032](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23032)) argues CoT can be faithful without hint verbalisation, so hint-verbalisation rates are a lower bound on faithfulness rather than a measure of it; and Gur-Arieh, Marasović & Geva, *Faithfulness Metrics Don't Measure Faithfulness* ([arXiv:2605.25052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25052)) questions the instruments generally. The 25%/39% numbers should be quoted with their metric attached, not as a faithfulness rate in the abstract.

## The two citations from the source note

The external note this gate started from cites two papers for its most interesting claims. Both resolve; one does not say what it is cited for.

- *Cox et al. 2026, "Watching AI Think"* — real ([arXiv:2601.16720](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16720), Samuel Rhys Cox, Jade Martin-Lise, Simo Hosio, Niels van Berkel; Aalborg / Oulu). But it is not about chain-of-thought reasoning traces. It is a study of chatbot thinking displays in an emotional-support setting, where the gap between display and computation was true by construction, since the thinking text was authored per condition. It grounds *the style of a thinking display moves social perception independently of the answer*. It does not ground *commercial reasoning traces function as self-presentation*, which is the note's framing and is offered in the paper's Discussion as framing rather than measured.
- *Zhang, Wang & Yi 2025* — real ([arXiv:2507.06000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06000), PACM HCI CSCW413), and already in this repo as `references/Collaboration through agency.md`. It is a scoping review of 134 HCI/CSCW papers producing a catalogue of control mechanisms, in which chain-of-thought is Mechanism-9. The claim the note attributes to it — that reasoning traces support targeted review when paired with controls — is the note's own synthesis, not a finding the review reports. Cite it for the mechanism catalogue; source the targeted-review claim to Wu et al. and the CHI EA '26 thematic analysis, which do support it.

- `transparent-reasoning` carries a TODO under *Before execution: planning*. `plan-preview` (added 2026-08-07, already through its own research gate) owns that station, and the two pages already point at each other. The TODO is stale rather than a gap.

What the outside note adds that the page does not currently hold, in rough order of how much the evidence supports it:

1. *The two gaps, kept apart.* The page's third resulting clause gestures at this — "whether it is the actual computation or a narration produced alongside it isn't visible from the trail" — but treats it as one thing. It is two, with different fixes, and both are now measurable.
2. *Verification cost as the condition.* Vasconcelos gives the rule the page lacks: a trail improves reliance to the extent it makes checking cheaper, which is why it pays on hard tasks and does nothing on easy ones, and why form beats presence.
3. *Omissions are the failure actors cannot see.* 31% detection against 82% for contradictions, and the models that ship produce omissions most.
4. *Steerability needs prompted checkpoints.* Editable is not enough — Hippo's participants edited only when asked to. This bears on the page's empty *Actions* and *Correcting the agent* sections.
5. *The display is a social act.* Cox is the ground for this, within its scope; it also supplies the expectancy-violation force, which nothing in the library currently names.

The open question for the author, which this gate does not answer: whether items 1 and 5 are enrichment of `transparent-reasoning`, or whether the faithfulness gap has become a force big enough to want its own treatment somewhere. The catalogue verdict says enrichment. The evidence assembled here is larger than the page's current shape comfortably holds, which is an argument for splitting the page internally — by temporal mode, as it already tries to — rather than for a second pattern.
