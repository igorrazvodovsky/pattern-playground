## Context

The actor changes something where it is shown — the display is also the control — instead of translating the change into a separate form, dialog, or page and coming back to check whether it landed. The pattern claims three affordance modes (always live, on activation, on approach), a granularity dimension (value / fragment / whole artefact), and a boundary where the change belongs on a separate form instead.

Questions:

1. What does the direct-manipulation literature say about the display doubling as the control — the cost of translating between an input arrangement and a result, and what closing that gap buys?
2. Is there evidence on accidental edits and stray input on always-editable surfaces, especially read-heavy ones — and what arrangements the literature proposes for surfaces that are mostly read but occasionally written?
3. Does editing across a collection differ enough from editing a single value in place to be a separate move?
4. What is known about affordance visibility trade-offs — controls revealed on hover/approach vs always visible — for surfaces that must also be read?

## Convergent findings

### The display-vs-separate-surface choice is the model-world vs conversation metaphor, and it is a measurable continuum, not two poles

Hutchins, Hollan & Norman name the two arrangements the pattern contrasts: under the conversation metaphor "the interface is an implied intermediary between the user and the world about which things are said"; under the model-world metaphor "the interface is itself a world where the user can act… The world of interest is explicitly represented and there is no intermediary between user and world." The pattern's lead — translating what you see into someone else's arrangement and coming back to check — is their gulf of execution and evaluation told from the actor's side. Beaudouin-Lafon then makes the choice measurable: the degree of indirection is "a 2D measure of the spatial and temporal offsets generated by an instrument". Dialog boxes "have a high degree of spatial and temporal indirection"; selection handles "provide a very direct interaction: low degree of indirection, high degree of compatibility". Crucially the space between the poles is populated: "Inspectors and property boxes are an alternative to dialog boxes that have a lower degree of temporal indirection. Since they can stay open, they can be activated with pointing… rather than selection in a menu." And "a large spatial offset is not necessarily undesirable."

Papers: Hutchins/Hollan/Norman 1985; Beaudouin-Lafon 2000.

### Piecemeal commit is what buys the sense of causality

Beaudouin-Lafon on why in-place edits feel different from a form's assembled set: "the arguments specified in a dialog box are taken into account only when the OK or Apply button is activated. In general, short temporal offsets are desirable because they exploit the human perception-action loop and give a sense of causality." This grounds the pattern's resulting clause (changes commit one at a time, no pending state) as the *mechanism* of the benefit, not just a side effect: the pending state a form maintains is temporal indirection.

Papers: Beaudouin-Lafon 2000.

### Editing AI output directly beats re-instructing it, with a precision-for-effort trade

Three papers converge from different directions. DirectGPT: participants "were 50% faster and relied on 50% fewer and 72% shorter prompts to edit text, code, and vector images compared to baseline ChatGPT", via "continuous representation of generated objects of interest… manipulable outputs… and undo mechanisms". The doctors study: "indirect editing reduced effort but introduced errors, while direct editing ensured precision but with higher workload." Point and Instruct: text alone makes it "difficult to select a particular dog and move it to a precise location", while "direct manipulation is well suited to visual tasks like selecting objects and specifying locations". Where they part: DirectGPT treats direct manipulation as the faster default; the doctors study found it costlier but safer; Point and Instruct argues neither channel wins — selection and location belong to manipulation, description to language. Conversation-with-a-model is the conversation metaphor literalised, and the forty-year-old argument repeats with the same result.

Papers: DirectGPT 2024; Editing with AI 2025; Point and Instruct 2024. (Textoshop supports the material-not-commands framing.)

### Writable projections need a maintained mapping, and the display's expressiveness sets the boundary

Sketch-n-Sketch ("direct manipulation of the program's graphical output corresponds to writing code… edits not possible with the mouse can still be enacted through ordinary text edits to the program"), the CSG CAD work ("the system ensures consistency between both spaces"), and NaturalEdit ("interactive representations tightly linked to source code… intent-driven bidirectional synchronization") converge: when the displayed surface is computed from an underlying representation, editing in place requires the system to maintain an explicit bidirectional mapping, and there is always a class of edits the display cannot express, which falls back to the source. This is the pattern's `related: data-view` edge with a sharper claim: which edits resolve is not just "data view's question" — it is bounded by the projection's expressiveness, and the fallback surface is part of the design.

Papers: Sketch-n-Sketch 2019; Bidirectional CSG CAD 2024; NaturalEdit 2025.

### Accidental edits on always-live surfaces are a mode problem, and feedback is the lever

Sellen, Kurtenbach & Buxton studied exactly the two-state structure the pattern's "always live" mode dissolves: mode errors as issuing navigation commands in insert mode or inserting text in command mode. "Both kinesthetic and visual feedback [were] effective in reducing mode errors" — with sustained kinesthetic feedback (holding the mode) the stronger arrangement. Transfer to the read-heavy To-do: an always-live surface has no guarding mode at all, so stray input lands by construction; the literature's remedy is not restriction but a deliberately reintroduced mode boundary with strong sensory feedback about which side of it the actor is on. The Notion lock toggle practitioners adopt is a rediscovered mode switch; the research suggests its weakness is feedback (nothing continuously shows the surface is armed), not the existence of the mode.

Papers: Sellen/Kurtenbach/Buxton 1992.

### The three appearance modes are Gaver's affordance taxonomy

Gaver separates affordances from the perceptual information about them, yielding "perceptible, false, hidden affordances and correct rejection". The pattern's *always live* with nothing marking editability is a hidden affordance ("actions that are possible but may not be visible but may be inferred"); *on approach* is an affordance whose information is gated on a probe gesture — hidden until hover; a surface that looks editable but is not (a locked record rendered like an input) is a false affordance. "When affordances are perceptible, they offer a direct link between perception and action" — which is what the reading/changing force trades away. Deuce is a worked example on a text surface: widgets drawn atop code on approach, with structural selection preferred over the always-available text selection.

Papers: Gaver 1991; Deuce 2017.

### Editing across a collection is where the move canonically runs out

Hutchins, Hollan & Norman, in "Problems with direct manipulation": "a repetitive operation is probably best done via a script, that is, through a symbolic description of the tasks that are to be accomplished. Direct manipulation interfaces have difficulty handling variables, or distinguishing the depiction of an individual element from a representation of a set or class of elements." And: "When we give up the conversation metaphor, we also give up dealing in descriptions, and in some contexts, there is great power in descriptions." Answer to Q3: yes — editing a collection is a change to a *described set*, not to a thing being looked at, and belongs to the symbolic/description family of moves, not to a stretched version of this one.

Papers: Hutchins/Hollan/Norman 1985.

## What this challenges

- *"The two poles of where a change happens" is too coarse.* Beaudouin-Lafon's indirection space is two-dimensional and populated in the middle: inspectors and property boxes stay open, act on the current selection, and apply immediately — lower temporal indirection than a form, higher spatial indirection than in-place. The pattern currently has no home for the inspector/property panel, which is neither this move nor [form](../../apps/patterns/src/content/patterns/form.mdx)'s separate errand. Either the `complements: form` note softens from "poles" to endpoints of a continuum, or the inspector is a gap in the catalogue.
- *Directness is not ease of use.* Hutchins/Hollan/Norman explicitly: "It is important not to equate directness with ease of use… There are surely instances when one might wisely trade off directness for generality, or for more facile ways of saying abstract things." The pattern's "When the change belongs elsewhere" section already carries this, but the solution statement reads as if in-place is the default and form the exception; the canon treats it as a genuine trade.
- *To-do #2 has a canonical answer.* Collection-wide editing is a different move (description over a set), per Hutchins/Hollan/Norman — not a variant of this pattern.
- *To-do #1 has a direction.* The read-heavy case is a mode-design problem: reintroduce an explicit read/edit boundary with continuous sensory feedback (Sellen et al.), rather than restriction or a saved view.
