# Research: actor-governable memory in AI systems — 2026-08-25

Query: whether the actor's handle on what a system holds forward is one design move or several, what the defaults actually govern, and where the move ends and its neighbours begin. Generated via `/deep-research`, run while deciding whether the `selective-memory` seed matures, splits, or goes.

> *Retrieval provenance*. Five search angles, 24 sources fetched, 118 claims extracted, 25 carried into adversarial verification (3 verifiers each, 2-of-3 to kill), 21 confirmed and 4 refuted. A budget cap dropped 5 claim sets before verification — those covered ephemerality, deletion practice, and all product sources, so they are recorded below under *Fetched but unverified* and must not be cited with the same weight. Every source in the first section was read in full text; none is abstract-only. Practice evidence is in the sibling note `2026-08-25-practice.md`.

## Context

*Topic*: actor-governable memory — inspecting, correcting, scoping, and erasing what a system holds forward between encounters.

*Interaction*: a system carries facts and context from one encounter to the next. The actor can see what is held, correct or delete an item, set how far a given exchange reaches into the store, or step outside memory for a session.

*Setting*: conversational assistants with persistent memory, plus the personalisation, user-modelling and profile-management surfaces they inherit from.

*Scope note*: "memory" means system-side persistence of user data. Not human working memory, not recognition-over-recall, not model architecture.

*Questions driving retrieval*: does anyone curate; what do actors believe is held; does correction and erasure work; is scoping-in-advance the same move as correcting-afterwards; where is the boundary with neighbouring moves.

## Retrieved papers

1. *The Algorithmic Self-Portrait: Deconstructing Memory in ChatGPT* (Dash et al. — ACM Web Conference 2026). 2,050 memory entries donated by 80 heavy ChatGPT users via GDPR Article 15 exports. [arXiv:2602.01450](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01450)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q5. The only on-target quantity in the corpus. "Only 4% (n = 84) include an explicit request to perform a memory operation… Consequently, 96% of all memory entries are unilaterally initiated by ChatGPT, without a detected direct command from participants." Their conclusion: "user influence over memory formation is mediated primarily through interaction content rather than direct control over update events."
   - Two narrowings that change the meaning if dropped: 4% is a share of *entries*, not of people — 25 of the 65 memory-having participants (38.5%) requested at least one operation. And initiation was detected by regex over trigger terms, so 4% is a lower bound.
   - Transfer: *strong*. This is the pattern's central situation, measured.

2. *Are Privacy Dashboards Good for End Users?* (Farke, Balash, Golla, Dürmuth, Aviv — USENIX Security 2021). n=153 US Prolific, Google My Activity, pre/post design. [arXiv:2105.14066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14066)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q2, Q3. Engagement: "only a third (n=55; 35.9%) of the participants indicate that they have visited their My Activity page prior to our study" — five years after launch, self-reported, on a technically-engaged sample. Concern after exposure: 61 (40%) moved down the scale, 69 (45%) unchanged, 23 (15%) up (W=2519.5, p<0.001, r=0.32). Spontaneous "creepy" reactions were rare (n=4, 3%) against 33% surprise, but that is an open-code rate, not a prevalence measure.
   - The paper's own verdict is double-edged: dashboards "are a net positive for online services… but it is not clear that these dashboards help end-users, broadly, to increase their privacy." The mechanism it names for non-use is privacy resignation and management burden, not decoration.
   - On per-item curation: "My Activity allows users a plethora of choices, but it may be too difficult for users to make informed decisions about individual activities as the impact of keeping or deleting individual items is opaque to the user." This is the authors' design analysis from interface inspection; the instrument contained no item on understanding deletion's impact.
   - Transfer: *strong* on the forces, *by analogy* on the numbers — My Activity is a record of what the system did, not what it kept.

3. *How Does Connecting Online Activities to Advertising Inferences Impact Privacy Perceptions?* (Farke, Balash, Golla, Dürmuth, Aviv — PoPETs 2024(2) 372–390). n=170, 133 with a populated ad-interest page. [arXiv:2312.13813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13813)
   - Speaks to: Q2, Q3. Participants labelled their own real Google activities with the interests they thought Google would infer: "only 21% of assigned interests occurred in Ad Settings." Restricted to the 133 with a populated page it is 700/2,615 = 26.8%, which the paper glosses as "about one quarter". Prediction fails; recognition does not — 65 rated the real list moderately or extremely accurate, 50 somewhat or slightly, only 3 not at all.
   - The *generic paradox*: the drop in concern came from participants reading Google's labels as generic and low-risk against their own more specific guesses. The authors predict "by focusing on the generic quality of individual interests and not on the volume and accuracy of those interests, people will be less likely to manage or remove their interests" — despite a median of 180 inferred interests each. This is a prediction, not a measured curation rate.
   - The sharpest single finding for the pattern's failure modes: of the 54 participants coded *some-interests-incorrect*, exactly one was coded *wants-to-delete* — and per-item deletion was available at the time, so a missing affordance does not explain it. P74: "Because a lot is so wrong it doesn't matter."
   - Transfer: *strong*. Inaccuracy discharging the motive to correct is a force the seed does not name.

4. *(Un)informed Consent: Studying GDPR Consent Notices in the Field* (Utz, Degeling, Fahl, Schaub, Holz — ACM CCS 2019). 82,890 visitors; 36,395 in the choice experiment. [arXiv:1909.02638](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.02638)
   - Speaks to: Q1. "The pre-selected versions led around 30% of mobile users and 10% of desktop users to accept all third parties. In contrast, only a small fraction (<0.1%) allowed all third parties when given the opt-in choice." Pre-selection was the only difference between conditions. Even with controls on the first page, only 10.0% unchecked one or more vendors and 6.9% one or more categories.
   - Time-sensitive: pre-ticked boxes became unlawful in the EU after CJEU *Planet49* (C-673/17, Oct 2019). Cite for what defaults do, never as a description of current consent interfaces. The several-hundred-fold swing is site-specific; Cookiebot's 3-million-log comparison gives roughly 18x (98.84% vs 5.59%).
   - Transfer: *by analogy*. The canonical measurement of defaults governing a record.

5. *Dark Patterns after the GDPR* (Nouwens, Liccardi, Veale, Karger, Kagal — CHI 2020). n=40, 1,280 instrumented pop-up encounters during real browsing. [arXiv:2001.02479](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02479)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q5. Instrumented rather than self-reported. "Almost all interactions (93.1%) were limited to the first page of the pop-up… a 'more options' link… was clicked only 88 times (6.9%). When participants were exposed to a scrollable list of data collection purposes or vendors on the first or subsequent pages (560 occasions), they ignored it 68.6% (384) of the time… anything not immediately visible to the user, anything requiring interaction to access, might as well not exist." Per-item curation: "only 17 answers out of 1280 (1.3%)."
   - Placement effects are large and measured: removing "reject all" from the first page raised the accept rate by 22.0 points (95% CI 0.149–0.290) and 23.1 points, both p<0.001; putting granular controls on the first page decreased consent by 8–20 points; notification style was inert for the accept rate (p=0.702).
   - Constraint on the framing: R² is 0.117/0.135, so placement explains 12–14% of variance. State it as "placement shifts the recorded answer by 22–23 points independently of stated preference", not as "preference does not matter". The authors call n=40 a "best case scenario" sample.
   - Transfer: *by analogy*, but the design consequence is direct — a memory panel reached from a settings menu is, on this evidence, unreachable.

6. *Facebook Algorithms and Personal Data* (Pew Research Center — Jan 2019). n=963 US Facebook users, GfK KnowledgePanel, MOE 3.4pp. [pewresearch.org](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2019/01/16/facebook-algorithms-and-personal-data/)
   - Speaks to: Q1, Q2. "74% say they did not know this list of categories existed on Facebook before being directed to the page"; 12% aware. Yet on inspection 59% said it reflected their interests very (13%) or somewhat (46%) accurately, 27% not very (22%) or not at all (5%).
   - The 27% is a share of *people rating a list as a whole*, not a per-item error rate.
   - Transfer: *by analogy*, on a probability sample — the awareness gap at population scale.

7. *Monteiro et al.* (2026, *preprint*, not peer-reviewed). 18 ChatGPT users shown 215 inferences drawn from their own histories. [arXiv:2605.10013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10013)
   - Speaks to: Q2, Q3, Q4. Mean comfort 4.0/5; "participants reacted more strongly with curiosity and interest rather than distress and concern". Discomfort concentrated in a small region rated both intrusive and surprising (n=23, mean comfort 1.74, against 4.82 for the 82 rated low on both); 14 of 18 rated at least one inference comfort ≤2.
   - The two-failure-mode split that bears on the split question: *misrepresentation* (22/215 = 10.2%, the system got the actor wrong) is "addressable at retention via correction or retraction"; *exposure* (15/215 = 7%, the system accurate about something the actor did not want surfaced) is "addressable by scoping inference-making at generation". They argue "a single undifferentiated opt-out, the prevailing pattern in consumer LLM interfaces, does not map well onto the range of concerns our data describes".
   - Two limits on reading that as two patterns: their "scoping at generation" means per-item accept/reject before an inference enters context, not a mode like incognito; and they explicitly decline to adjudicate the advance-versus-standing question, calling the balance "a design question rather than a finding".
   - P18 named the missing handle: "if there was a way that I could just reply back to the inference, like, chat, you're wrong. Like, I only asked you one time."
   - Transfer: *strong* on the failure taxonomy. Exploratory by the authors' own description.

8. *Zhang et al.* (2025, *preprint*, not peer-reviewed; the fetched text still carries unfilled ACM template placeholders). n=18 interviews, recruited via Tsinghua with heavy Chinese-product usage. [arXiv:2508.07664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.07664)
   - Speaks to: Q4, Q5. The single most decisive source on the split question, and it points toward one territory. Both halves sit inside one subsection, *Lack of Meaningful Control and Transparency*, which walks creation, management, usage and update in order. Advance scoping appears as P8's imagined "browser-like incognito window", P4's wish to "distinguish, to use this part of the memory and not other parts, distinguish personas", and P11's "switch identities". Correction appears in the same passage as "information filtering, viewing and editing" (P3), direct deletion (P5, P9), timed deletion (P6), and P11's "correction mechanism". P8 appears on both sides personally.
   - Substitution under failure: P17's assistant "seemed to always remember my first requirement and kept modifying the code according to the first requirement's standard", forcing them to "start a completely new environment" — an actor reaching for advance scoping because correction was unavailable. That account leads directly into P8's incognito wish.
   - §5.2 places the two on one axis, describing incognito mode as "an extrema of this balancing"; §5.5 fuses them in one architectural recommendation (memory "workspaces" or "personas", with a new conversation defaulting to context-free).
   - Transfer: *strong* in kind, *weak* in weight — n=18, qualitative, one cultural context.

## Convergent findings

### 1. The system writes the record; the actor does not

96% of ChatGPT memory entries are system-initiated (Dash et al.). This is the fact the pattern turns on, and it is what separates this territory from `settings` and `ai-tuning`, where the actor authors the content. The correction problem exists here precisely because the actor did not write what is held.

The adjacent surfaces agree on shape: 35.9% had ever opened My Activity five years after launch; 74% did not know Facebook's interest list existed; 1.3% of consent answers involved a per-item selection; pre-selection alone moved accept-all from under 0.1% to 10–30%.

*Papers*: Dash et al. 2026; Farke et al. 2021; Pew 2019; Nouwens et al. 2020; Utz et al. 2019.

### 2. A control one interaction away is absent

Nouwens et al. measured this directly: 93.1% of interactions never left the first page, "more options" was clicked 6.9% of the time, and scrollable lists were ignored 68.6% of the time. Their sentence is the design consequence — "anything not immediately visible to the user, anything requiring interaction to access, might as well not exist."

This cuts against the seed's assumption that `settings` is "where memory controls conventionally live". On this evidence, conventional placement is the reason nobody uses them.

*Papers*: Nouwens et al. 2020; Utz et al. 2019.

### 3. Showing the record lowers concern — and the researchers read that as a failure

Three independent studies, same direction: 40% moved down the concern scale on My Activity (p<0.001, r=0.32); a significant moderate decrease on real Google ad interests (p<0.001), with perceived benefit unchanged; mean comfort 4.0/5 across 215 surfaced ChatGPT inferences. So the worry that making memory visible backfires is not supported.

The sting is the mechanism. Farke et al. name the *generic paradox*: items look harmless one at a time, which discharges the motive to curate the volume. Inaccuracy does the same work — of 54 participants who found interests incorrect, exactly one wanted to delete anything.

*Papers*: Farke et al. 2021; Farke et al. 2024; Monteiro et al. 2026.

### 4. Actors cannot reconstruct what is held, but can adjudicate it once shown

Prediction accuracy was 21% (26.8% restricted to populated pages). Recognition held up: most participants judged the real list at least somewhat accurate, and Pew's probability sample splits 59/27 the same way. The affirmative case for a visible memory list is recognition, not recall — which also means the list has to be *shown*, not merely *available*.

*Papers*: Farke et al. 2024; Pew 2019.

### 5. One territory, two moves, with different triggers

Zhang et al. put advance scoping and after-the-fact correction in one subsection, one axis, and one architectural recommendation, with the same actors on both sides and P17 substituting one for the other under failure. Monteiro et al. show the failure modes are not interchangeable — misrepresentation is fixed at retention, exposure is fixed at generation — but decline to adjudicate whether that means two patterns.

The corpus supports *distinct forces named inside one pattern*, not two patterns. The strongest independent confirmation is in the unverified set below (Khan et al.: actors scope in advance *because* they do not trust deletion).

*Papers*: Zhang et al. 2025; Monteiro et al. 2026.

### 6. The bear case, and why it does not land

Farke et al. conclude that transparency tools "garner trust with their users and improve their perceptions without necessarily changing users' behaviors" — 37% would not change any setting, 26% unsure. If the pattern were decoration, that would be the evidence for dropping it.

It is not decoration. The mechanism the authors name is privacy resignation ("believing that data collection will occur regardless of their choices") and management burden ("the burden of properly managing their privacy is too high despite the availability of the transparency tool"). The control works; nobody pays its cost. That routes the pattern toward cheap curation, defensible defaults, and system-proposed edits — not toward a richer manual panel, and not toward no panel.

Do not restate the numbers as "75% would not use memory controls"; the authors binned "unsure" with "no" for the regressions.

*Papers*: Farke et al. 2021; Farke et al. 2024; Monteiro et al. 2026.

## Fetched but unverified

The budget cap dropped these before adversarial verification. Every quote below was extracted from fetched full text, but none survived three-verifier checking, so they carry less weight than the section above. Several are more on-target than the verified set.

- *Khan et al., cloud deletion practices* (SOUPS 2017, n=26). The cleanest one-pattern evidence in the whole corpus: "Some participants reported that they explicitly decide on what goes in to the cloud before they upload… This approach is common among people who have high privacy concerns and low trust towards providers… users want control over what is synced over to the cloud so that they will not have to delete." Same actor, same force, choice between the two moves turning on whether after-the-fact deletion is trusted. Also: deletion failure is caused by "lack of information about deletion, incomplete mental models… and poorly designed user interfaces", not indifference. [research-information.bris.ac.uk](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/i-feel-stupid-i-cant-delete-a-study-of-users-cloud-deletion-pract/)
- *Habib et al., Away From Prying Eyes* (SOUPS 2018). Ephemerality is an act-level excursion, not a standing mode: private-only sessions averaged ~23 minutes against ~43 for normal ones, and in mixed sessions only 34% of page visits were private. Misconception rates: 39% believed private browsing allows anonymous browsing; 22% believed it stops the browser sending any cookies; among those who shopped in private mode, 24%/20% believed it protected their credit card details. Privacy awareness did not correlate with correct understanding — only prior use did. [usenix.org](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/soups2018/soups2018-habib-prying.pdf)
- *Wu et al., private-browsing disclosures* (WWW 2018, n=460, 13 disclosures). In-product explanation largely fails: only Chrome's desktop disclosure beat a deliberately vague control, and by a single scenario. 56.3% believed searches made while logged into a Google account would not be saved. Two disclosures made comprehension *worse* than near-silence — on whether downloaded files remain listed, 32.4% of control participants held the misconception against 62.2% for Brave and 77.1% for Firefox, the two that explicitly mention downloads. Accuracy tracked observability: people were right about locally visible effects and wrong about opaque server-side ones. [blaseur.com](https://www.blaseur.com/papers/www18privatebrowsing.pdf)
- *GDPR erasure audit* (PoPETs 2022, 90 services). 27% (24/90) did not fully delete or anonymise after a confirmed erasure request; 18% (12/67) kept sending newsletters afterwards; two services that were demonstrably still storing an email address answered a formal access request saying no data was stored. Only 3 of 90 explained what actually happens to which data. Confirmation is inconsistent: 48% of in-account requests produced a confirmation email; 79% of delayed deletions never sent a notice when deletion occurred. [petsymposium.org](https://petsymposium.org/popets/2022/popets-2022-0080.php)
- *Schnitzler et al., message deletion* (CSCW 2022, n=125, between-subjects). 66.4% correctly anticipated what deletion did, varying by app: WhatsApp 80%, Facebook Messenger 71%, Skype 49%. Presenting scope as an explicit in-the-moment choice ("Delete for me" vs "Delete for everyone") improved prediction by 30 points over silent global deletion. The most frequent named reason to delete was *revision* (k=17), ahead of inappropriateness (8) and regret (7). And per-item curation is a minority behaviour even one tap away: 56.8% almost never delete. [anupamdas.org](https://anupamdas.org/paper/CSCW2022.pdf)
- *Li et al., Privacy Control in Conversational LLM Platforms: A Walkthrough Study* (CHI 2026). Names the unit — "a memory snippet is a specific data unit that captures what the model decides to keep and use from a user-input message, usually in a shortened or rephrased form". Documents deletion-that-does-not-delete: "deleting a chat session has only a local effect: the conversation disappears from the side panel, but any information stored in memory remains." Controls are fragmented across five surfaces (conversational UI, side panel, settings, shared-links portal, customisation), and "most other controls produce only local effects". [arXiv:2602.10684](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10684v1)
- *SemanticCommit* (2025, n=12, a 30-item memory store). Half the participants (6/12) always began by asking the system to locate conflicts *before* any change was applied — a preview-before-commit step inside correction. The chat baseline failed to detect a single conflict across 18 cases involving 10 participants, and in 9 the participant accepted the unchanged output. Visibility does not stop over-reliance: 5 of 12 skipped reviewing anything the system had not flagged. Per-item review cost time (5m41s vs 4m07s, p≈0.004) but not workload (all p≥0.45). Of 8 participants who had used memory features, 4 actively managed entries. [arXiv:2504.09283](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09283)
- *CIMemories* (2025). Scope expressed in language is not a binding control: "as usage increases from 1 to 40 tasks, GPT-5's violations rise from 0.1% to 9.6%, reaching 25.1% when the same prompt is executed 5 times". Frontier models leak up to 69% of profile attributes into inappropriate contexts. Frames persistent memory as contextual integrity — appropriate flow between contexts, not secrecy. Note: the benchmark has no actor-facing condition at all, so it speaks to model behaviour, not to what an actor can see or fix. [arXiv:2511.14937](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14937)

## Refuted in verification

Four claims were killed and are excluded from the findings. Recording them so they are not re-derived:

- That finer-grained per-item controls produce abandonment rather than curation (0–3 against, Utz et al.). The vendor-notice condition's non-interaction rate does not support the causal generalisation.
- That 28%/52% of ChatGPT memory entries carry GDPR-defined personal or psychological content (1–2, Dash et al.).
- The "Attribution Shield" pre-emptive-intervention framing (0–3, Dash et al.) — no efficacy evaluation exists.
- That Monteiro et al. are the corpus's closest attempt to adjudicate the one-versus-two question (0–3) — they explicitly decline to.

## Open questions

- Do the actor populations for advance scoping and after-the-fact correction diverge at scale? Zhang et al. show same-actor overlap at n=18 qualitative. Nothing measures whether the people who reach for temporary chat are the people who edit memory lists.
- Does deletion in a memory interface correspond to removal from the model's behaviour? No verified source treats machine unlearning as an interface question, and the one paper examining per-item deletion reports its consequences are undocumented even for a simple activity log.
- What is the curation rate for an assistant memory panel specifically? Every engagement number here comes from an activity log, a consent notice, or an ad-preference page. Dash et al. measured only the write channel.
- Is *review-and-commit* a third move? Zhang et al. §5.5 proposes a warning icon showing a proposed memory update for the actor to accept; Monteiro et al. propose surfacing each new inference with the activity that produced it before it enters context; SemanticCommit observed 6 of 12 participants constructing the step by hand. Pre-storage gating is neither mode-scoping nor retrospective correction, and no source evaluates whether actors would sustain it or abandon it the way they abandon consent notices. In this library's terms that is `approval-gate` applied to memory writes.

## Caveats

*Domain transfer is the largest weakness.* Exactly one verified quantity is about assistant memory. Every other hard number comes from cookie consent, an activity log, or an ad-interest profile. The pattern's own boundary is between what a system *did* and what it *kept*, so these transfer by analogy and should be labelled that way anywhere they are used.

*Small samples on the central question.* The one-pattern verdict rests on two preprints with n=18 each, both qualitative, both skewed young and AI-familiar. Neither tests population divergence at scale; at n=18 neither can.

*Self-report versus behaviour.* The 35.9% engagement figure, the 74% awareness figure, and every intent-to-change number are self-reported, with social desirability running in the direction that makes the low-engagement finding conservative. Instrumented behavioural data is confined to Nouwens et al. (n=40) and the Utz/Cookiebot logs.

*Time sensitivity.* Utz et al.'s pre-ticked boxes became unlawful in the EU in Oct 2019. Pew's fieldwork is Sept–Oct 2018 on a since-redesigned surface. Google replaced Ad Settings with My Ad Center in Nov 2022, after the PoPETs fieldwork. The ChatGPT memory implementation is single-vendor and changes often.

## Takeaways for the pattern page

- Lead from the situation the evidence establishes: the system writes the record, the actor did not, and the actor's handle on it is partial. Not from an entitlement ("theirs to inspect, correct, scope and erase") that no shipping product currently delivers.
- The defaults-govern clause is the best-evidenced part of the seed and should move from third place to the centre, with its mechanism named — resignation and curation cost, plus placement.
- Add the trigger the seed misses. Its `initiating` names only "a wrong remembered fact". Monteiro's exposure category and Willison's Half Moon Bay case (practice note) are *correct-but-unwanted* continuity, where correction has no purchase because nothing is wrong, and the remedy is scope.
- Add the legibility gap as a force: held state exceeds shown state. ChatGPT's rolling dossier has no inspection surface at all (practice note).
- Keep one node. The moves substitute for each other for the same actor under the same force, and the sharpest evidence for that is Khan et al.'s actors scoping in advance because they do not trust deletion.
