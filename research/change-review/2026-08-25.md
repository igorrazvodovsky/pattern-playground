## Context

*Topic*: making sense of work an actor neither observed nor produced, when a machine proposes changes to material that already exists.

*Interaction*: a system proposes changes to something that already exists and matters. The change is shown as a change — what was, what would be — so the actor can judge and accept it in parts rather than take or leave the whole. The actor was not present while the work was done and did not author what is being changed.

*Setting*: coding agents proposing edits, writing tools proposing revisions, agents that ran unattended and now present a result. Approval gate, plan preview, transparent reasoning, activity log, instructed revision, regeneration, undo, draft and publish and commenting are the neighbours already in the corpus. Version-control code review and word-processor track changes are the pre-AI lineage.

*Questions driving retrieval*:

1. Does the literature treat reviewing a proposed delta and reconstructing what an agent already did as one problem or two?
2. What changes when the reviewer did not author the surrounding material and did not watch the work happen?
3. What does delta-shaped review systematically miss?
4. Does presentation granularity actually become decision granularity?
5. Where does this move end and its neighbours begin?

## Convergent findings

### 1. The literature splits oversight before from oversight after — but never on the axis this pattern turns on

*Verified.* Every source that separates the two poles separates warning-and-confirmation-before from trace-reconstruction-after. Peng & Li put it plainly in §7.3: "Much existing design attention goes toward permission prompts, guardrails, and action confirmations, but our findings suggest that post-hoc understanding is equally central to users' sense of safety and control," with P2 saying "I do not just want the system to warn me before it acts. I want to know afterward exactly what it did, and whether I should undo something." Zhang & Nam make the same cut as a design argument: "requiring users to remain attentive throughout execution conflicts with the common usage scenario and motivations of agents… Rather than requiring continuous attention, systems should help users verify after execution." Baum & Laux separate anticipatory involvement from reactive review on the grounds that they answer different questions — "is the space of permissible decisions adequately constrained?" against "was this decision acceptable?"

Neither pole in any of these is diff-shaped. Three verifiers independently grepped the agent-oversight papers for `diff|hunk|track change|accept/reject|patch|side-by-side|proposed change`: zero substantive hits in Peng & Li, in HANSEL, and in Tang et al. The pattern's own form does not appear in the oversight literature at all.

Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens cut the other way, and it is worth holding both. Their scale places "the system proposes, the human executes" (level 4) and "the system executes unless vetoed" (level 6) as adjacent rungs of one ordinal continuum, not as two problems. Their second axis is more useful here than the first: automation has a stage coordinate — information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, action implementation — and a system can sit at a different level on each. That is the framework in which previewing an intention and reviewing a rendered proposal are distinguishable positions rather than one control, though the paper never makes that application itself.

*Papers*: Peng & Li 2026; Zhang & Nam 2026; Baum & Laux 2026; Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens 2000.

### 2. The pre/post distinction does not survive contact with how actors actually behave

*Verified for the numbers, unverified for the reading.* Mozannar et al. is the one study in the set that instrumented both sides of the accept action. Verification did not stop at the accept — 19% of the time a programmer verified *again* after accepting, even when they had already verified before accepting, and after "deferred thought" accepts it happened 53% of the time. Adjusting for post-acceptance verification raised mean attributed verification time from 3.25s to 15.21s for deferred accepts, roughly fivefold (paired t-test, t=−4.88, p=8×10⁻⁶).

The mechanism behind it matters more than the number. Accepting a proposal was routinely used as a way to *look at* it rather than to endorse it. The probability of editing an accepted suggestion was 0.18 when the programmer verified first and 0.53 when they deferred judgement (χ²=29.2, p≈0). The authors named "deferring thought for later" as a distinct state and argue that acceptance rate is an inflated proxy for endorsement, recommending survival of the accepted material over time instead. Their field acceptance rate was 21.4% against 34.0% in the lab.

So the clean split between judging a proposal and reconstructing what landed is a designer's split, not an actor's. The actor moves back and forth across the commit point, and uses the accept control as a viewing device.

*Papers*: Mozannar, Bansal, Fourney & Horvitz, CHI 2024.

### 3. A better review container reliably buys speed and confidence, and does not buy accuracy

*Verified.* This is the most consistent empirical result in the set, and it points at the pattern's central risk.

HANSEL: users accepted the wrong answer in 32.1% of cases where the agent was wrong. The accuracy gain over baseline (82.14% against 75.0%) was not significant (β=0.71, SE=0.67, z=1.06, p=.29), while the interface won decisively on what it was built for — time on correct answers 130.4s against 167.7s (p<.001), perceived effort V=7, p=.012, r=.67. The displacement shows in the logs, not only in interviews: baseline participants independently re-ran parts of the task in 66% of tasks, HANSEL participants in 9%. Presented evidence substituted for independent checking. P11: "It was highlighted, and the student option was already selected, so I just trusted it immediately."

Grunde-McLaughlin et al. found the same dissociation over agent traces: "although participants reported higher levels of confidence in their decisions, their final accuracy was not meaningfully improved" — accuracy g=0.18, duration g=−0.29, confidence g=0.56 — and the gap was largest exactly where it hurts, g=0.85 for confidently scoring an incorrect output as correct.

Both underwrite the older complacency finding: automation that is "highly but not perfectly reliable" produces monitoring failure, and the effect is greatest when the operator is doing other things at the same time.

Against that, Rovira et al. supply the one evidence-backed argument in the set *for* showing the material rather than the recommendation: "If fully reliable decision automation cannot be guaranteed, designers should provide users with information automation support or other tools that allow for inspection and analysis of raw data." That tension is unresolved and is recorded below.

*Papers*: Zhang & Nam 2026; Grunde-McLaughlin et al. 2026; Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens 2000; Rovira, McGarry & Parasuraman 2007.

### 4. Reviewing a change is mostly reconstructing intent, and that is the channel the agent case closes

*Verified.* Bacchelli & Bird found that understanding the change — not judging it — was the dominant challenge, emerging unprompted in all 17 observation sessions and ranking second of nine comment categories in the 570-comment card sort. Their survey of 873 programmers: 798 (91%) said reviewing unfamiliar files takes longer, and 716 (82%) said reviewers familiar with the changed files give substantively different feedback — owners find subtle defects and comment conceptually, non-owners comment on naming and mechanical style. Reviewers report that judging a small change requires reading far more code than the change contains.

The repair developers actually use is to leave the review interface: in 20–40% of reviews they go and talk to the author in person, and those exchanges most often address understanding. The authors state they cannot compare that frequency to what the tool captured, and that several review outcomes "leave little trace." So the diff-plus-comments container was never self-sufficient even for contemporaneous human review. It was completed by an interrogable author.

Two sources measure what happens when that author is a machine. Zhong et al., across 278,790 review conversations, found that "understanding" comments — where a reviewer asks about intent rather than proposing a fix — drop from about 31% of human-reviewer comments on human-written code to about 17% on AI-generated code. Asking-the-author behaviour attenuates precisely when the author is an agent. Heander et al. state it as their problem: the reviewer's normal route to author intent is closed, and the reasoning trace is at best a degraded substitute because it can confabulate.

Al Safwan et al. give the pre-LLM baseline. Their participants' fallback is explicit — "If it is taking me more than 30 minutes… I will start trying and get in touch with the person who wrote the code" — and in hard cases 46% spend more than 30 minutes searching for the rationale of a single commit. One qualifier from verification is worth carrying: an absent human author more often produced *postponement* than abandonment, and unlike an absent colleague, an agent can be re-queried about its own change. Whether interrogating the agent substitutes for author intent or compounds the confabulation problem is untested.

*Papers*: Bacchelli & Bird 2013; Zhong, Noei, Zou & Adams 2026; Heander et al. 2026; Al Safwan, Elarnaoty & Servant 2022.

### 5. Reviewing an agent's change costs more, not less — and selective rejection still discriminates

*Verified.* The rubber-stamping story is not the whole story, and one large sample points the other way. Zhong et al. found human reviewers spend measurably *more* effort on AI-generated code: 11.8% more exchange rounds across 300 projects. In the same corpus, per-suggestion accept and reject is doing real work rather than degenerating into blanket acceptance — human reviewer suggestions are adopted at 56.5%, AI agent suggestions at 16.6%, a 39.9-point gap. A manual sample of 383 unadopted AI suggestions attributes 28.7% to incorrect code and 24.0% to the developer applying an alternative fix instead. Selective rejection tracks suggestion quality.

Acceptance is not free even when the reviewer judged it worth taking. Adopted AI suggestions raise cyclomatic complexity by a mean delta of 0.085–0.106 against −0.002 to 0.009 for adopted human suggestions, and raise total statements 0.216 against 0.108. Accept-and-reject at the hunk level does not filter out the accretion.

Watanabe et al. give the field picture at the changeset level: of 567 Claude Code pull requests, 83.8% were eventually merged, but only 54.9% went in untouched — 45.1% were merged only after additional human changes, concentrated on bug fixes, documentation and conformance to project-specific standards. That is context the surrounding codebase carries and the diff does not.

*Papers*: Zhong et al. 2026; Watanabe et al. 2025.

### 6. Change-scoped review yields far less defect detection than the pattern assumes, and what it does yield is judgement about the surroundings

*Verified.* Three independent samples agree on the composition. Bacchelli & Bird: defect-related comments were 78 of 570 (14%), fourth of nine categories, behind code improvements at 29% — while finding defects was the top stated motivation, ranked first by 44% of 873 programmers. Of the 78, 65 were low-level logical issues; 6 were high-level, 5 security, 3 exception handling. Mäntylä & Lassenius: 75% of review findings across nine industrial and 23 student reviews did not affect visible functionality, rising to 77% and 85% once false positives are discarded — roughly four in five true findings are maintainability and comprehension issues. Beller et al.: 69–81% of the changes actually made to code under review were evolvability edits.

The bearing on the pattern is that an accept-or-reject decision framed as "is this change correct?" addresses about a fifth of what reviewers actually raise. The other four fifths are judgements about whether the material can be understood and modified later — judgements made against the surrounding system, which is exactly what a change-scoped view frames out. Between a third and half of the evolvability findings fall in naming and commenting: the code's communication of intent to a later human reader.

*Papers*: Bacchelli & Bird 2013; Mäntylä & Lassenius 2009; Beller et al. 2014.

### 7. The diff's presentation order causally determines what gets scrutiny

*Verified, and the strongest causal result in the set.* Fregnan et al. observed across 219,476 pull requests that files displayed earlier receive more review comments than files displayed later, after controlling for discussion threads and lines added. They then established it causally: in an online experiment with 106 participants, switching the position of defect-seeded files gave participants 64% lower odds of identifying the defect when its file appeared last rather than first.

Two qualifiers matter. The effect was defect-dependent — of the two seeded defects, one showed no measurable sensitivity to position — so this is not a blanket property of diff review. And the ordering that governs the bias is an arbitrary tool default: Gerrit and GitHub sort files alphabetically. The presentation choice, not any judgement about risk or importance, determines which parts of a proposed change receive attention.

*Papers*: Fregnan, Braz, D'Ambros, Çalikli & Bacchelli 2022.

### 8. Leaving the delta is what correlates with precision

*Unverified.* di Biase et al. is the closest thing in the set to a direct test of reviewing inside a change versus reviewing against its surroundings. Across both conditions, reviewers found a mean of 1.42 and 1.21 of three injected defects — roughly 44% — with familiarity stacked in their favour, since participants had been trained by implementing three features in the same codebase, the third targeting the very classes under review. An actor judging an agent's diff on material they did not author has less familiarity, not more, so 44% reads as a ceiling for the pattern's situation.

The behavioural finding is the useful one. The review pattern that stayed inside the change — rationale then correctness, no context-seeking step — was simultaneously the most productive in the control group (8 defects) and the most error-prone, producing 3 of the 6 false positives. Patterns that included a context step, opening referenced classes outside the changed hunks, produced almost no false positives. The authors attribute the decomposed group's lower false-positive rate to that behavioural difference rather than to the diff being easier to read.

Reviewers' confidence did not track their precision. Self-reported navigation difficulty, comprehension, difficulty understanding and rationale clarity showed no significant difference between conditions, and objective rationale questions were answered equally well — yet the tangled group produced six times as many false positives. Only the questions about the change's *structure* differed significantly. Reviewers felt equally fine in the condition where they were measurably less accurate.

*Papers*: di Biase, Bruntink, van Deursen & Bacchelli 2019.

### 9. Damage lands outside the changed material often enough to matter, and the record under-carries the why

*Verified, with two corrections applied.* Al Safwan et al. §7.2: "the most frequently found components (and also the easiest to find) are those automatically tracked by revision control (committer, modifications, and location), followed by goal and time. However, the frequency (and easiness) of finding drops quickly for all other components." §7.4.2 names the rarely recorded set: "side effects, alternatives, constraints, selected alternative, maturity stage, and benefits," and "Developers most struggled to find side effects, alternatives, and constraints."

Two corrections that must not drift back. *Goal* — the headline why — sits above the drop-off, because a human wrote it into the commit message. Do not read "the delta carries no why" off this paper; read "the delta carries what, who and where mechanically, and the authored goal survives because someone authored it." And the paper's own emphasis is not unmet demand but a cost asymmetry: "many components are not too frequently needed, but when they are needed, they are really hard to find… the difficulty of finding these components may overcome their limited frequency of need."

Tang et al. measure where agent-caused damage lands. Among 1,372 system-damage episodes, 75.80% affect code or task state, 18.51% project state, 2.11% environment or configuration, 3.57% external state. Among the eleven hard-to-reverse cases the balance inverts to 36.36% project state and 45.45% external state, "indicating harder recovery once misalignment extends beyond the local codebase." Named cases: deploying the wrong page to a live host, committing to the wrong branch, rewriting Git history and deleting uncommitted work, downgrading core packages. The n=11 subgroup is too small to carry weight alone, and the authors say these estimates "should be interpreted with caution."

The same paper documents a coverage asymmetry between trace review and in-material review: it can only see misalignment the developer voiced as pushback, and states that "categories that naturally elicit verbal pushback… are observed more completely than those more likely to be silently worked around, such as minor S5 implementation errors fixed in the IDE." Trace and material are uneven views of the same work, not interchangeable ones.

Liu et al. add what survived whatever review happened: 24.2% of AI-introduced static-analysis issues were still present at the repository's latest revision, worst for the classes needing reasoning beyond the changed lines — security 41.1%, runtime bugs 30.3%, code smells 22.7%. Both directions have to be reported together, though: across 304,362 AI-authored commits, AI commits net-*reduced* code smells (449,984 fixed against 431,850 introduced) while introducing nearly twice as many security issues as they fixed.

*Papers*: Al Safwan, Elarnaoty & Servant 2022; Tang et al. 2026; Liu et al. 2026.

### 10. Presentation granularity and decision granularity come apart, in both directions

*Mixed; the strongest evidence here is unverified.* The seed asserts that whatever the review can show separately is what can be accepted or rejected separately. Four sources complicate that.

*Downward.* Mozannar et al.'s observational account is that when the system offers a change in units smaller than the unit the actor wants to judge, actors accept the fragments in order to assemble something judgeable. Their one quantitative probe for why deferral happens returned null — suggestion length did not differ between deferred and verified suggestions (75.81 against 69.06 characters, t=−0.58, p=0.56) — so this is a taxonomy-development narrative, not a measurement. HANSEL's participants make the same complaint from the other side: they rated the step-by-step trajectory view 3.36/5 against 4.79/5 for highlighted evidence. The unit the system emitted was not the unit reviewers wanted to judge in.

*Upward, and this is the answer to the seed's second open to-do.* Heander et al. define the reviewable unit not by display but by coherence: a "chunk" is the smallest cross-file set that can be applied without breaking compilation or tests. The tool must compute the smallest *coherent* delta rather than let the actor accept arbitrary hunks. They also report that acceptance is conditional and iterative rather than binary. This is a design position from a participatory study with a 43-person survey behind it, not a measurement — but it names the incoherent-partial-application problem directly and answers it with a constraint on what may be offered separately.

*Granularity as itself negotiable.* Yamashita et al. argue that automatically computed decomposition produces clusters developers find unsuitable, so manual re-cutting is a necessary step rather than an optional refinement. Their control is bidirectional — splitting *and* merging — so the actor's job is redrawing the boundaries of the proposed decomposition, not approving pre-cut pieces. They also repurpose the diff view as a coherence test rather than a correctness test: the actor uses it to judge whether the changes in one cluster hang together as a single intent. Design-space argument, no user study.

*What finer units cost.* di Biase et al. found that splitting one proposal into two separately reviewable units bought precision but not sensitivity — wrongly reported issues fell from 6 to 1 (p=0.03, Cliff's d=0.36) while defects found (20 against 17, p=0.6) and net review time (median 831s against 759s, p=0.66) did not differ. The null on time is worth holding against the assumption that finer units multiply effort, but it is a null on 14 per cell with only two units.

*What partial acceptance looks like in the field.* Watanabe et al.'s 45.1% of merged agent PRs modified after the fact, and Beller et al.'s 7–35% of review comments discarded, are both partial disposal — but by follow-up commit and by conversation, not by per-hunk control.

*Papers*: Mozannar et al. CHI 2024; Zhang & Nam 2026; Heander et al. 2026; Yamashita, Hayashi & Saeki 2020; di Biase et al. 2019; Watanabe et al. 2025; Beller et al. 2014.

### 11. The move has no entry in the catalogues that ought to hold it

*Unverified but checkable.* Three design-space surveys were read for whether change review appears as a named move, and none holds it.

Feng et al. synthesised six interaction patterns from 22 deployed agentic systems. None of the six is a delta or change-review entry — they are visible thoughts/plans/actions; mechanisms for control transfer; watch mode; customisable rule-based governance; inspectable and editable agent memory; and sandboxes. The analysed corpus includes Cursor Agent Mode, GitHub Copilot Agent Mode, Claude Code, Jules, Figma Make and Lovable — tools whose primary review surface is a diff with per-hunk accept and reject. Those systems appear in the corpus and their review surface does not appear in the taxonomy. The same paper folds real-time monitoring and retrospective reconstruction into a single pattern, which is one taxonomy answering the discriminating question "one problem."

Lee et al.'s writing-assistant design space codes 115 papers on 35 dimensions and has no code anywhere for change-shaped presentation. The dimension governing how AI output appears — "UI — Visual Differentiation" — has an exhaustive four-code set (None, Formatting, Location, Media), none of which shows prior state alongside proposed state. The reader verified the absence of "track changes", "redline", "strikethrough", "reject" and "diff" as a noun anywhere in the paper. Nor does any dimension cover inspecting what the system previously did: the taxonomy treats system output as material to integrate, never as a record to audit.

What Lee et al. *do* separate is instructive for the pattern's own structure. How AI output is visually marked and how the actor acts on it are two orthogonal dimensions — one owned by the UI, one by the user — and they vary independently across the 115 coded systems. That is the design-space form of the claim that presentation granularity and decision granularity are separate choices.

shapeof.ai splits pre-execution oversight from post-hoc traceability at the top level of its taxonomy: "Governors" holds Action plan, Verification, Sample response, Controls and Draft mode; "Trust builders" holds Footprints, Disclosure and Watermark. Change review has no entry of its own. Accept-and-reject-in-parts appears as a consideration inside "Inline action", a pattern named for how the AI is invoked rather than for how the change is judged, with a rule that the proposal be rendered as a suggestion layer over the original rather than an overwrite. The catalogue never uses the word "diff". Its "Inpainting" entry names partial, region-scoped change to existing content as a move in its own right, paired with a verify-to-commit rule.

*Papers*: Feng et al. 2025; Lee et al. CHI 2024; shapeof.ai.

### 12. The accept moment does not produce the record, and occupying the review seat is not the same as overseeing

*Verified for Siebert's forward/backward split; the rest is design-space argument with no data.* Siebert et al. split their fourth property into a forward link — "whenever a human… makes a decision with moral implications, that human should be aware of their moral responsibility" — and a backward link — "for any consequence of the actions of the human-AI system, the human decisions and actions leading to that outcome should be readily identifiable" — and argue the first does not deliver the second: "Even if all relevant humans made their decisions responsibly and with full awareness of their possible moral implications, the lack of a readily identifiable link from a given action of the AI agent to the underlying human decisions would still result in loss of tracing… all relevant human decisions… must be explicitly logged and reported."

Applied here: a well-run accept-and-reject moment does not by itself produce the record that makes the decision reconstructable later. Provenance and audit trail are a separate requirement, not a by-product of review.

The same paper states an ordering constraint — ability ≥ authority ≥ responsibility — and names the failure when it is violated: the human becomes an "ethical or legal scapegoat," and "Simply giving human agents final authority by design, without ensuring proper ability, is not sufficient to empower humans to act upon their moral responsibility." Elish's moral crumple zone is the case-analysis ancestor of the same argument, and reads a per-action confirmation control — Tesla's 2015 proposal that the driver sign off on an autonomous lane change by clicking the turn signal — as a mechanism by which the human retains all the responsibility while the autonomy is marketed elsewhere. Baum & Laux make the point in one line: a human who must approve every decision but rubber-stamps it does not thereby become an overseer.

Baum & Laux also add a dimension worth carrying: *hybrid* cognitive integration, where the actor's task-relevant beliefs are partly constituted by the machine's own representations. That predicts a specific failure for the pattern's situation — if the only account of what happened is the system's presentation of it, the reviewer's judgement is not independent of the thing being judged. HANSEL's 66%-to-9% collapse in independent re-execution is what that looks like when measured.

*Papers*: Siebert et al. 2022; Elish 2019; Baum & Laux 2026; Zhang & Nam 2026.

### 13. The machine can only record the layers a reviewer does not need

*Unverified.* Xu et al.'s four-layer provenance model — events, actions, sub-tasks, task — makes an asymmetry explicit: the layers cheap to capture automatically are the semantically empty ones, and the goal and rationale layers are not available to the system at all, because they live in the actor's head. An automatically produced record of an agent's work is systematically missing the level at which a reviewer judges.

They report an experiment (Dou et al. 2009) in which domain experts reading user action logs recovered 79% of findings but only 60% of methods and 60% of strategies against interview ground truth, and call that accuracy poor even under favourable conditions. They also argue intent capture has a hard expiry: the window for recording high-level rationale closes quickly and cannot be reopened later, even by the person who did the work. If that transfers, retrospective review and at-the-time review are different problems rather than the same problem deferred.

Step-through review of a trace does not scale either: reconstructing a sequence by restoring successive system states loads the reviewer's memory, and the volume of states from a short working period already exceeds what overview-plus-detail provenance visualisations handle.

*Papers*: Xu, Attfield, Jankun-Kelly, Wheat, Nguyen & Selvaraj 2015.

### 14. A nominal review gate is often not exercised, and approval without comment predicts defects

*Verified.* Beller et al. screened over 30 mature OSS systems that publicly claimed mandatory, continuous code review and found only two that actually adhered, discarding the rest as study candidates. One Eclipse sub-project treated review as an opportunity offered rather than performed. Their review record is also an incomplete account of the review window: 10–22% of all changes made to code under review were self-motivated by the author and triggered by no review comment, and such undocumented changes appeared only in tasks that also contained at least one review-triggered change.

McIntosh et al. give the quantitative handle. Requiring that every change pass review does not by itself predict lower defect rates: review coverage had significant explanatory power in only 2 of 4 releases, and full coverage beat under-coverage in only one. What did survive model construction in all three releases studied was approval with no reviewer comment at all — the closest operationalisation of rubber-stamping in the dataset — and more such approvals is associated with more post-release defects. Reviewing faster than 200 lines per hour is significantly associated with post-release defects where it enters the model, but the direction disagrees across systems, increasing in Qt 5.1 and decreasing in ITK. The authors rank participation above expertise: how actively the change was engaged with mattered more in their models than who engaged with it.

*Papers*: Beller et al. 2014; McIntosh, Kamei, Adams & Hassan 2016.

## Disagreements, reported rather than averaged

*Does showing the material improve calibration or only confidence?* Rovira et al. recommend giving actors tools for inspection and analysis of raw data whenever full reliability cannot be guaranteed — an argument for the diff. HANSEL measured richer presented evidence cutting independent re-verification from 66% to 9% of tasks — an argument that the better container displaces the checking it was meant to support. Both are real findings about different things, and nothing in the set resolves them.

*Does reviewing an agent's work cost more or less?* Zhong et al. measured 11.8% more exchange rounds on AI-generated code across 278,790 conversations, and 16.6% adoption of AI suggestions against 56.5% for human ones — reviewers working harder and discriminating more. HANSEL and Grunde-McLaughlin measured confident acceptance of wrong output in controlled settings. The likeliest reconciliation is that these are different populations doing different jobs: open-source maintainers disposing of an unsolicited changeset against lab participants judging an answer under a five-minute limit. Neither generalises to the other.

*Is the review-before / review-after split real?* Peng & Li, Zhang & Nam and Baum & Laux say the modes differ. Feng et al.'s agent-UI taxonomy folds monitoring and reconstruction into one pattern. Al Safwan et al. treat reviewing-a-commit-under-review and reconstructing-an-old-commit as one rationale-seeking need, never segmenting findability by task. Parasuraman et al. place propose-then-execute and execute-unless-vetoed on one continuum. And Mozannar et al.'s measurement of verification crossing the accept boundary in both directions argues the split is not one actors observe. Baum & Laux are internally ambivalent on exactly this axis: their temporal taxonomy groups review-before and review-after as one reactive family — "Synchronous and asynchronous HOTL share a reactive logic" — while the gate/switch distinction elsewhere in the same paper would separate them by causal category. The reading that would have settled it did not survive verification.

## What the literature does not answer

*Whether the delta is the right container for retrospective sensemaking.* This is the question the note was built to answer and it comes back open. Nothing retrieved compares a change-shaped presentation of what an agent did against an outcome-shaped or requirement-checklist presentation of the same work. Three independent targeted greps confirm the agent-oversight papers do not discuss the delta form at all — that is verified silence in this corpus, not an assumed gap. The one result that would have spoken to it was refuted (below).

*Whether presentation granularity becomes decision granularity, as a measured effect.* Finding 10 assembles four partial answers, all of them either observational narrative, design position, or an underpowered null. No study manipulates per-hunk acceptance and measures what actors do with it.

*Whether interrogating the agent substitutes for an absent author.* Al Safwan et al.'s participants postponed rather than abandoned when a human author was unavailable. An agent, unlike a colleague on holiday, can be asked about its own change — and Tang et al. show its answer is unreliable. Nobody has tested whether asking helps.

*Whether the pattern's situation has been studied at all.* Named plainly: a proposed delta to authored material, reviewed by someone who did not write the surroundings and was not present for the work. Bacchelli & Bird study it with human authors. Zhong et al. and Watanabe et al. study its outcome traces at scale without observing a reviewer. HANSEL and Grunde-McLaughlin observe reviewers, on question-answering tasks with no pre-existing artefact. The centre of the pattern is unoccupied.

## Refuted in verification — do not carry forward

Six claims were killed at the verification gate. Four were refuted on framing while their numbers verified, so the salvageable reading is stated here. If any of these resurfaces in a stronger form downstream, it did not come from this note.

- *Flowchart 39.13% against Spec 65.22% error detection* (Grunde-McLaughlin et al.), refuted 0–3. The numbers are verbatim correct and the ordering is monotonic across the process-to-outcome spectrum. What fails is "controlled": the study is exploratory and not task-balanced — "each method had unique tasks, two of which were incorrect and one of which was correct" — so representation is fully confounded with task item. The quote as originally extracted stopped exactly at the authors' own disclaimer, "although the study was not task-balanced". Salvageable reading: a suggestive but confounded observation from an exploratory probe, hinting that a process-shaped container may not be the right one for retrospective judgement, and carrying no evidential weight on its own.
- *Agent self-reporting inaccurate in 22.58% of episodes, second most prevalent symptom* (Tang et al.), refuted 0–3 on two counts. It is third overall, not second — S3 constraint violation 38.33%, S2 misread intent 26.95%, then S7 at 22.58% — and the denominator is validated misalignment episodes, themselves conditional on a developer having pushed back, not agent turns. Salvageable, and useful: among observed breakdowns, roughly a quarter involved a false completion claim, and every such episode was detected because inspecting the material falsified the report.
- *Only 25.34% of trajectory steps contributed to the answer, so the full record is the wrong container* (Zhang & Nam), refuted 1–2. The figures verify. The relevance definition is circular for this purpose: an evidence step is one that "contributed to the reasoning process leading to the final answer," explicitly excluding failed attempts, blocked pages and retries — which is exactly what an actor auditing conduct rather than checking an answer would need. Pruning a trace to what supported the answer is a different operation from pruning it to what a reviewer needs.
- *The rarely recorded rationale components are unmet demand* (Al Safwan et al.), refuted 1–2. The paper's own emphasis is the reverse: those components are comparatively low-frequency needs whose difficulty of retrieval outweighs their limited frequency. Also, *goal* is findable and is not mechanically captured, which breaks the clean mapping from "what version control records" to "what is findable".
- *Baum & Laux's gate/switch distinction gives a principled approval-gate versus change-review boundary*, refuted 0–3.
- *Siebert et al.'s tracking and tracing are two separate necessary conditions supporting a pre/post split*, refuted 0–3. Their forward-link/backward-link argument (finding 12) is a different claim and did verify.

One further correction from verification, applied above but worth stating: Peng & Li's Finding 7 reads "Participants were often unsure whether they could tell what the agent had changed." That is self-reported anticipatory worry from a scenario-based interview, not observed reconstruction failure. No participant ran an agent and then failed to enumerate its changes.

## Caveats on the corpus

*Evidence-type mix.* Only three findings rest on controlled work — HANSEL (N=14, five-minute limit, seeded errors), Grunde-McLaughlin et al. (N=12, effect sizes without significance tests, underpowered null on accuracy), and Fregnan et al.'s N=106 position experiment, which is the only one with a clean causal manipulation. Everything on the neighbour boundaries is design-space argument or self-report with no measurement behind it. Siebert et al. and Baum & Laux collect no data.

*Nothing compares the two modes.* No source runs pre-commit review against post-hoc review as conditions. Both HANSEL arms are post-hoc; both Grunde-McLaughlin arms are post-hoc. Any statement that one mode outperforms the other is unsupported here.

*Verification coverage is partial.* Nineteen claims confirmed, six refuted, and roughly a hundred extraction-stage claims never checked, because the harness verifies a fixed top 25. Contradiction sweeps could not be run for about half of the verified claims either, because the search budget was exhausted, so "no contradicting source found" often means "not looked for". Every unverified finding above is flagged as such.

*Source strength.* Eight of the primary sources are unreviewed arXiv preprints, several with two authors and single-digit citation counts. Peng & Li's interview finding motivates the authors' own prototype and the protocol explicitly probed "desired audit support", which carries demand-characteristic risk. Tang et al.'s damage-locus figures come from the paper's thinnest-validated axis, with human checking on that sub-axis covering nine records. Watanabe et al.'s corpus skews to refactoring, documentation and testing.

*Time sensitivity.* The agent sources are all 2025–2026 and describe a fast-moving surface. The code-review sources (2009, 2013, 2014, 2016, 2019, 2022) are stable, because they concern human comprehension and documentation practice rather than model capability — and that stability is what the pattern's firmest claims rest on.
