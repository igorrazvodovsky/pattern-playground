# Change review: mature the seed, and correct the force it states backwards

Proposed, not executed. The research gate is complete; no pattern files have been touched.

`change-review` has been a seed since 2026-08-07, carrying one citation, a disclosure that it came from the cross-catalogue triage, and two standing to-dos. A `/deep-research` gate ran on 2026-08-25 — `PARA/Projects/pattern playground/research/change-review/` holds the query and the literature note. The gate was framed around one discriminating question, because the seed and the way the node is often described pull in different directions: the seed's initiating situation is proposal-shaped, about a change that has not landed, while the broader framing — making sense of an agent's output the actor neither observed nor produced — is retrospective, about work already done. If the literature treated those as two problems, the node would split and its retrospective half would merge into neighbours that already exist. This plan records what the gate returned and what executing on it would touch.

## What the gate found

*The literature splits oversight before from oversight after, but never on the axis this node turns on.* Every source that separates the two poles separates permission prompts and action confirmations from post-hoc trace reconstruction. Peng & Li put it directly — design attention has gone to "permission prompts, guardrails, and action confirmations" while "post-hoc understanding is equally central" — and their own retrospective container is an action-grouped narrative log of actions, touched resources, permission history and side effects. Its shape is a record of actions, not a delta. Three independent greps over the agent-oversight papers for diff, hunk, track changes, accept/reject and patch returned nothing. The delta form does not appear in that literature at all, and the containers the retrospective work reaches for are the ones this corpus already calls `activity-log` and `provenance-marking`.

*So the retrospective half does not belong here, and the reason is the container rather than the timing.* Reconstructing what an agent did is a different object — a record of actions by an entity over time — not a differently-timed view of the same object. The same boundary separates the pre-landing delta from the catch-up move, whose situation is absence rather than proposal. The gate confirms it and supplies the grounds.

*The pre/post distinction is a designer's distinction, not one actors observe.* Mozannar et al. instrumented both sides of the accept action across 21 programmers and 1,024 suggestions. Verification did not stop at the accept: it happened again after acceptance 19% of the time even when the programmer had verified beforehand, and 53% of the time after accepts made without verifying. Accepting was routinely a way to look at a proposal rather than to endorse it — the probability of editing an accepted suggestion was 0.18 when verified first and 0.53 when judgement was deferred. The actor moves back and forth across the commit point, and uses the accept control as a viewing device.

*The node states its granularity force backwards.* The current clause reads that whatever the review can show separately is what can be accepted or rejected separately. The evidence says presentation granularity and decision granularity come apart in both directions. Downward: when units are smaller than what the actor wants to judge, the actor accepts fragments in order to assemble something judgeable, and the acceptance signal stops meaning agreement — the same authors argue acceptance rate is a corrupted proxy and recommend measuring survival of the material instead (field acceptance 21.4% against 34.0% in the lab). Upward: Heander et al. define the reviewable unit by coherence rather than by display, as the smallest cross-file set that can be applied without breaking compilation or tests, which is a constraint on what may be offered separately at all. Yamashita et al. go further and treat the decomposition itself as the thing the actor negotiates, with a control that both splits and merges, on the grounds that machine-computed clusters are not the ones developers would draw. What the review shows separately is a design choice that has to answer to coherence, not a fact the decision inherits.

*The attention-narrowing force is confirmed and now has causal evidence.* Fregnan et al. observed across 219,476 pull requests that files shown earlier draw more comments than files shown later, then established it causally: with 106 participants and the position of defect-seeded files switched, participants had 64% lower odds of identifying a defect when its file appeared last. The ordering doing this is an arbitrary tool default — Gerrit and GitHub sort alphabetically. So it is not only that the unchanged surroundings escape review; where a change sits inside the presentation governs whether the change itself is examined. The effect was defect-dependent, with one of two seeded defects insensitive to position, so it is not blanket.

*And the surroundings are most of what review is actually for.* Three independent samples agree: defect-related comments were 14% of 570 at Microsoft, fourth of nine categories; 75% of findings across 32 industrial and student reviews did not affect visible functionality, rising to 77–85% once false positives are dropped; 69–81% of changes made to code under review were maintainability edits. An accept-or-reject decision framed as "is this change correct?" addresses roughly a fifth of what reviewers raise. The rest is judgement about whether the material can be understood and modified later, made against the system the diff frames out. di Biase et al. found the behavioural signature: the review pattern that stayed inside the change was the most productive *and* produced half the false positives, while patterns that opened referenced classes outside the changed hunks produced almost none — and reviewers' confidence did not track their precision.

*The force the node is missing is the absent author.* Bacchelli & Bird found that understanding the change, not judging it, was the dominant challenge, emerging unprompted in all 17 observation sessions; 91% of 873 programmers said reviewing unfamiliar files takes longer, and 82% said familiar reviewers give different feedback in kind — conceptual against naming and style. The repair developers actually use is to leave the review interface: in 20–40% of reviews they go and talk to the author. The diff-plus-comments container was never self-sufficient. It was completed by an interrogable author. Zhong et al. measured what happens when there isn't one: across 278,790 review conversations, comments that ask about intent rather than propose a fix drop from about 31% on human-written code to about 17% on AI-generated code. Heander et al. state it as their problem — the route to author intent is closed and the reasoning trace is a degraded substitute because it can confabulate. This is the pattern's actual situation, and the node does not name it.

*Reviewing an agent's change costs more, and selective rejection still discriminates.* The rubber-stamping story is not the whole story. In the same 300-project corpus, human reviewers spent 11.8% more exchange rounds on AI-generated code, and adopted AI suggestions at 16.6% against 56.5% for human ones, with a manual sample attributing 28.7% of non-adoption to incorrect code. Against that, the controlled work is consistent that a better review container buys speed and confidence without buying accuracy: HANSEL users accepted the wrong answer 32.1% of the time the agent was wrong, with a non-significant accuracy gain, while independent re-checking collapsed from 66% of tasks to 9%; Grunde-McLaughlin et al. found confidence g=0.56 against accuracy g=0.18, and g=0.85 for confidently scoring incorrect output as correct. The likeliest reconciliation is that these are different populations — maintainers disposing of an unsolicited changeset against lab participants under a five-minute limit — and nothing in the corpus tests it.

*No catalogue holds this move, including the ones that should.* Feng et al. synthesised six interaction patterns from 22 deployed agentic systems; none is a delta or change-review entry, and the analysed corpus includes Cursor Agent Mode, Copilot Agent Mode, Claude Code, Jules, Figma Make and Lovable — tools whose primary review surface is a diff with per-hunk accept and reject. The systems are in the corpus; their review surface is not in the taxonomy. Lee et al.'s writing-assistant design space codes 115 papers on 35 dimensions and has no code anywhere for change-shaped presentation: the dimension governing how AI output appears has an exhaustive four-code set, none of which shows prior state alongside proposed state, and "track changes", "redline", "strikethrough" and "diff" as a noun appear nowhere in the paper. shapeof.ai subsumes accept-in-parts inside "Inline action", a pattern named for how the AI is invoked rather than for how the change is judged, and never uses the word "diff". The seed's disclosure says the catalogues are thin here. They are thinner than that.

*The node's central promise remains untested.* Nothing retrieved compares a change-shaped presentation against an outcome-shaped or checklist-shaped presentation of the same work. That silence is verified rather than assumed. The one result that would have spoken to it — a probe in which the most process-shaped representation produced the lowest error detection — was refuted in verification: the numbers are correct but the study is exploratory and not task-balanced, with representation fully confounded with task item, and the authors say so in the sentence the claim quoted half of.

## Verdict

*Keep it as one pattern, and mature it.* No split, no merge, no retirement. Three grounds, in order of weight.

The retrospective half of the broad framing has a different container, not a different clock. Reconstructing what an agent did is a record of actions by an entity — the shape `activity-log` already owns, and the shape every retrospective system in the literature reaches for. Absorbing it here would make the node a hub over two objects that share only a moment in time.

The delta form is systematically missing from the catalogues and the design spaces, while being the primary review surface of the tools those same papers analyse. That is the gap this corpus is for.

And the node's situation, once the absent author is named, is not covered by any neighbour. `approval-gate` owns the consent moment and the accountability it concentrates. `plan-preview` owns the intention before the work. `transparent-reasoning` owns the account of how, and the literature says that account is not reliable enough to close the gap this pattern opens. Nothing owns judging a proposed change to material you did not write, proposed by an author you cannot ask.

*Four corrections to the frontmatter, two of them reversals.*

One: the initiating situation gains the reviewer's position — did not write the surroundings, did not watch the work, cannot ask who did. It currently says only that the system proposes changes to something that already exists and matters, which describes the material and not the person judging it. This is the half the evidence is densest on.

Two: the first resulting clause overstates what the delta buys. It currently claims that judgement lands on the change "rather than on re-reading the whole". The evidence says the opposite of the second half: 91% of 873 programmers reported that reviewing unfamiliar files takes longer, reviewers report that judging a small change requires reading far more code than the change contains, and di Biase et al. found that the review pattern which stayed inside the diff produced half of all false positives while context-seeking patterns produced almost none. What survives is the first half — the delta gives an approval something concrete to act on. The saving-you-from-the-whole claim moves down into the attention clause as a cost rather than staying up here as a benefit.

Three: the granularity clause is stated backwards. Not "the diff's granularity becomes the decision's granularity" but the reverse: what the review shows separately and what the actor decides separately come apart in both directions, so the reviewable unit has to be drawn by coherence rather than by display, and an acceptance does not reliably encode a judgement.

Four: the attention clause is confirmed, gains teeth, and absorbs what clause two gives up. Position within the presentation, not only the boundary of the change, governs what is examined — and reading the change without reading its surroundings is where the false positives come from.

*One new consequence worth its own clause.* The reviewer's usual repair for an illegible change is to ask its author, and here there is no one to ask. That sets up `transparent-reasoning`, which is what the situation demands and what the same literature says cannot fully answer it — the trace can confabulate, and Tang et al.'s field data shows the agent's own report of what it did is falsified by inspecting the material often enough to matter. Recording it as a `sets-up` edge is honest about both halves. Keep this clause and the initiating rewrite from restating each other: the situation states the reviewer's position, the clause states what follows from it.

Confidence is uneven and worth carrying. The attention and comprehension findings rest on stable, pre-LLM software-engineering work with large samples and one clean causal manipulation. The granularity reversal rests on one instrumented lab study, one participatory design position, and one design-space argument — enough to say the current clause is wrong, not enough to fix its replacement precisely. Nineteen claims passed three-vote adversarial verification, six were refuted, and roughly a hundred extraction-stage claims were dropped unverified when the run's search budget ran out; the note flags which findings are which.

## What executing this closes

Both of the node's standing to-dos. The attention-narrowing force gets its code-review literature, and the question of whether per-hunk acceptance granularity deserves its own force treatment is answered yes — with the force pointing the other way from how the node currently states it.

It also settles the framing question the node has carried implicitly since it was seeded: this is a pattern about a proposal, not about retrospection, and the boundary is the container rather than the clock.

## Open, and not blocking

- Whether the delta is the right container for retrospective sensemaking at all. The corpus is silent, verifiably. If this matters later it wants its own gate, not a rider on this one.
- Whether interrogating the agent substitutes for an absent author. Developers postpone rather than abandon when a human author is unavailable, and an agent can be asked — but its account of its own work is unreliable. Nobody has tested whether asking helps or compounds the problem.
- Whether showing the material improves calibration or only confidence. Rovira et al. recommend giving actors access to raw data whenever reliability is imperfect; HANSEL measured a better evidence display cutting independent checking from 66% to 9%. Both are real and they point opposite ways.
- Whether the review-queue form inherits everything the diff form does. Position bias, coherence units and the absent author were all studied on single changesets. A queue of accumulated proposals adds an ordering the literature has not examined.
- Whether `approval-gate` should carry the ability ≥ authority ≥ responsibility constraint. Siebert et al. state it and name the failure — the approver becomes a scapegoat — and Elish's moral crumple zone is the case-analysis ancestor. That belongs to the gate node rather than this one, but the gate's seed does not carry it.
