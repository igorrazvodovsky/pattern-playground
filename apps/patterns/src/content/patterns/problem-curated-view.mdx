The actor keeps returning to the same data with one question: what needs my attention, and why? The domain can answer half of it in advance — there is consensus about what counts as a problem (a lint rule, a vital-sign threshold, a policy violation) — yet a general view leaves the actor to re-derive those judgements by inference on every visit. A problem-curated view organises the projection around the detected problems instead of the data types: a checklist of candidate problems, each backed by an explicit rule. It is the design-time keying of a [purpose-keyed view](/patterns/purpose-keyed-view) taken to its limit.

## Anatomy

The checklist is the interface. Each entry is a detected problem; the list doubles as index, navigation, status display, and audit trail. Selecting a problem prunes the general view to the relevant slice, magnified and annotated with why it was flagged — the provenance is explicit, so the view can always say why it looks the way it does, and the reason travels with the actor from noticing into investigating.

The IDE problems panel is the everyday example: rule-detected problems as a list, a click showing the offending lines annotated with the rule that fired (`eslint: no-unused-vars`). Google's code-review tooling is the same anatomy at industrial scale: analyzer findings surfaced as annotations inside the review, each carrying its check and a feedback control. The anatomy works anywhere the domain can agree on detection rules.

## Commitments

Three commitments keep the form honest:

- *Annotate the judgement moment.* Flags belong where judgement is already being exercised — the review, the rounds, the scheduled check — and stay out of exploration, where they pre-empt the very judgement they are meant to support. The lesson is empirical: the same detection rules failed as ambient dashboards and succeeded attached to code review. Showing flags only when guidance is solicited is one honest implementation; the rule is the moment, not the request.
- *Keep the escape hatch structural.* The general exploratory view stays reachable alongside the curated one — required, not optional. Codifiability is a gradient: even where rules are possible, the ground truth behind them is partly heuristic, so there is always a remainder the rules don't encode. The escape hatch is where the remainder gets noticed — and where the next rule is usually discovered.
- *Watch the framing.* The same checklist reads as scaffolding when it teaches what to look for, and as fossilised process when it caps what anyone looks for.

## Consequences

- The recurring triage is answered by the checklist rather than re-derived on every visit, and provenance keeps the curation accountable.
- The rule set is a running cost. To its reader, a false positive is any flag they did not want to see — so flags need a feedback channel and a noise budget. Without them the curve is alarm fatigue, then suppression: the checklist still renders after trust has left it.
- Cases outside the rules sail past silently; the escape hatch carries them, which is why it is a commitment and not a courtesy.

## In practice

The IDE problems panel at full recurrence, lint fatigue included. Tricorder, inside Google's code-review tool: findings shown at review time, a noise budget of at most 10% "effective false positives", a not-useful button on every flag, and noisy checks removed — the upkeep consequence institutionalised. In-Sight carried the anatomy into ICU rounds, curating patient data around clinically detected problems, with the exploratory view kept structurally reachable. Triage worklists — moderation queues, fact-checking prioritisation — are the same anatomy where the detected problems are work items to allocate.
