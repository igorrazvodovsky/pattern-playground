* * *

**A newcomer facing every function at once has to work out what matters before doing anything, and the functions they misuse in early attempts can damage their work or leave the artifact in states they cannot explain.**

Carroll and Carrithers blocked the error-prone functions of a word processor for new users, and those users learned faster and understood more than users of the full system. Shneiderman proposed multi-layer designs in which first-time users start with a limited set of functions in layer 1, stay there as long as they like, and move up when they need more. McGrenere, Baecker and Booth compared a two-interface word processor, a personal interface with only chosen features beside the full default, against adaptive menus, and participants felt better able to navigate, control and learn the software with the two interfaces. That study was of experienced users cutting the interface down to what they already used, and it measured experience, not speed. In a follow-up lab study, Findlater and McGrenere found a menu the actor arranged as fast as an ideal fixed one when people did arrange it, and faster than an adaptive one, but some people did not arrange it at all without guidance. Gajos and colleagues traced the annoyance of adaptive interfaces to lost spatial stability: the one adaptive design people accepted copied commands into a separate, marked area and left the familiar parts untouched. The two bodies of evidence point in different directions over time. For newcomers it was the designer who chose the reduced layer, and it worked; for experienced users, choosing for themselves gave more satisfaction and control. The costs are that experienced actors are slowed if they cannot leave the reduced layer, functions in higher layers are harder to discover, and the layers assume an order of learning that not every actor follows.

Therefore,

**Offer a first layer with a small set of functions that is complete for the simple version of the work, and make each further layer contain everything in the one before. Choose the first layer for the newcomer, show them that more exists, and from then on let them make each move between layers, with the artifact proposing a move rather than making it.**

Do not trap the actor in a layer, and do not move them between layers or reorder what a layer shows without telling them. Where the artifact adapts on its own, keep the familiar parts in place and confine the changes to a marked area.
