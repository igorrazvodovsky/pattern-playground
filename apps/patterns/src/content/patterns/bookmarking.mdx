* * *

**When an actor reaches an item or view they will need again later, the system cannot tell that it matters. Even where they could [bookmark](/concepts/bookmarking) it, they often repeat the search or leave tabs open instead.**

Long-term returns are a small share of revisits but the ones automatic history serves worst (Obendorf et al. 2007), and people keep things by whichever method costs least at that moment, so a save that asks for anything loses to an open tab (Jones, Bruce and Dumais 2001). Collections that do get used are organised only in occasional bursts (Abrams, Baecker and Chignell 1998), so filing can wait until the list needs it.

Therefore,

**Let the actor save any addressable item or view with a single action, and make their saved list reachable in one step from wherever navigation starts. Save immediately without prompting for a name, folder, or tag; let the actor rename or organise entries later through [incremental formalisation](/patterns/incremental-formalisation). Keep saved links resolving when targets are renamed or moved, and mark broken or deleted targets directly in the list rather than failing only after the actor clicks. Keep one saved list per system, however many places offer the save.**

Keep the save private to the actor. Where an item can also be followed for [notifications](/patterns/notification), offer [following](/concepts/following) as a separate action so neither implies the other.

## In practice

The variants below are ways into the same list, not separate stores.

- *Saved list.* Ordered by when items were saved or arranged manually, showing enough context—or a [link preview](/patterns/link-preview)—so the actor can recognise each target before opening it.
- *Pin.* Shows saved items in a persistent spatial slot, such as the top of a sidebar, tab bar, or message thread. Because screen space bounds the set to a handful of items, pinning suits active working items rather than long-term archives.
- *Favourite or star.* Marks a saved item in place so the saved set can also act as a filter or sort key inside its home collection.

## Research on this pattern

- Revisiting accounts for most browser navigation: [Tauscher and Greenberg (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1997.0125) measured a 58% revisit rate, and [Cockburn and McKenzie (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2000.0459) found 81% over longer observation windows. Most revisits are to pages seen minutes ago.
- [Obendorf, Weinreich, Herder and Mayer (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240719) grouped revisits into short-term (under an hour), medium-term (within a day), and long-term; long-term revisits are a small share of traffic but the hardest for automatic history tools to support.
- [Abrams, Baecker and Chignell (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1145/274644.274651) found that bookmark collections grow steadily, are organised in occasional bursts when lists become unmanageable, and contain many entries that are never visited again.
- [Jones, Bruce and Dumais (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/502585.502599) observed that people rely on multiple keeping methods at once—bookmarks, open tabs, self-addressed emails, and re-searching—choosing whichever has the lowest friction at the moment.
- [Kaasten and Greenberg (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/634067.634209) tested merging back, history, and bookmarks into a single recency-ordered list where bookmarks appear as explicitly marked history entries.
