**The actor reaches a place they will need again, and the system has no way to know it matters. Finding it took moves, or a search whose terms they will have forgotten, and next time they will pay the same cost or fail to find it at all. Leaving the tab open, emailing the link to themselves and taking a screenshot are what people do instead, and each is a bookmark made with the wrong tool.**

Returning is most of use. Over half of the pages people visit in a browser are pages they have visited before, and in longer studies the share rises past four fifths (Tauscher and Greenberg 1997; Cockburn and McKenzie 2001). Most of those returns are to something seen minutes ago, which the back button and [recents](/patterns/recents) handle. Bookmarks serve the remainder: the return after a week or a month, which is rare, and which the available tools serve worst (Obendorf et al. 2007).

What is known about the mark itself is mostly about its failure. Collections grow steadily and are organised in bursts, when the list has become unmanageable, and a large share of what is kept is never used again (Abrams, Baecker and Chignell 1998). People keep found things by many methods at once, and choose among them each time by what the moment costs: a bookmark competes with re-searching, with an open tab, with an email to oneself, and often loses, because it asks for an act now against a need that may never come (Jones, Bruce and Dumais 2001). One study argued the opposite of this page's split, folding bookmarks into a recency-ordered history as marks the actor adds to it (Kaasten and Greenberg 2001); that is the evidence that the two moves share a substrate, and it stands against keeping them apart.

The costs follow from the act. A bookmark is something the actor has to remember to do, at the one moment they are least inclined to, since they are busy with the thing they found. Filing it is a second act, and naming it a third, and any of them asked at the moment of marking is a reason not to mark. The place behind the mark moves or is deleted, and the list does not know. And a set made inside one system stays there, while the actor's places are spread across many.

Therefore,

**Give any state the actor can reach a single act that keeps it, and one place of their own where what they kept is listed and reached in a move from anywhere. Ask for nothing at the moment of marking: no name, no folder. Let naming and filing come later, when the set has grown enough to need them. Keep a mark resolving when the place behind it moves, and when it cannot, say so in the list rather than failing on the way. Show which marks the actor uses and which they never have, so that pruning is a choice they can make rather than a chore they avoid.**

The star that also ranks, the pin that also fixes a position and the follow that also subscribes each give the mark a second meaning. Add the second meaning only where the actor wants the second thing, since a mark that means two things is harder to remove.

## In practice

### Forms of the mark

- *Bookmark.* A row in a list the actor owns. The plain form, and the one the rest are measured against.
- *Pin.* The mark fixes a position in the interface: a pinned tab, chat or message stays at the top or the edge. The way back is spatial rather than listed, and the set is small by construction, since positions run out.
- *Favourite.* A mark that also ranks. The star doubles as a filter and a sort key, so it serves browsing as much as return, and it is read by other people as an opinion.
- *Follow.* A mark that also subscribes: the place comes to the actor through a [notification](/patterns/notification) when it changes. The way back is no longer needed, because the place keeps arriving.
- *Saved view.* A mark on a framing rather than an item: a query, an arrangement and a set of visible attributes kept together. [Data view](/patterns/data-view) carries this form.

### Where the marks live

A sidebar, a start page, the command menu before a query is typed. Whichever it is, the mark should be one move away from anywhere, and the list should show what each mark points at well enough that the actor need not open it to know; a [link preview](/patterns/link-preview) does this for a mark on a place elsewhere.

### Rot

Places move and are deleted, and the list is the last to know. Keep the address resolving where the system controls both ends. Where it does not, check the marks and say in the list which no longer resolve, instead of letting the actor find out on the way.

## Research on this pattern

- Revisiting is most of browser use: [Tauscher and Greenberg (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1997.0125) found a revisit rate of 58 percent, and [Cockburn and McKenzie (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2000.0459) found 81 percent. Most revisits are to pages seen minutes ago.
- [Obendorf, Weinreich, Herder and Mayer (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240719): revisits split into short-term (within an hour), medium-term (within a day) and long-term, and the long-term ones, though a small share, are the ones existing tools serve worst.
- [Abrams, Baecker and Chignell (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1145/274644.274651): bookmark collections grow steadily, are organised in bursts, and hold a large share of entries that are never used again.
- [Jones, Bruce and Dumais (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/502585.502599): people keep found things by many methods, bookmarks among them, chosen each time by the cost at that moment.
- [Kaasten and Greenberg (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/634067.634209): a proposal to merge back, history and bookmarks into one recency-ordered list, the evidence for treating bookmarking and recents as one move.
- Untested here: whether asking for nothing at the moment of marking raises the rate of marking, and whether showing use lets people prune.
