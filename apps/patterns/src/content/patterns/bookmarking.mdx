**When an actor reaches an item or view they will need again weeks or months later, the system has no way to know it matters. Without a direct way to save it, they must repeat the search or navigation steps next time, or fall back on workarounds such as leaving tabs open, messaging links to themselves, or taking screenshots.**

Browser studies show that while most revisits happen within minutes or hours and are served by the back button and [recents](/patterns/recents), long-term returns after days or weeks are poorly served by automatic history (Tauscher and Greenberg 1997; Cockburn and McKenzie 2001; Obendorf et al. 2007). Bookmarks fill that gap, but they carry a timing mismatch: they require an explicit action now, while the actor is focused on their current task, for a future return that may never happen (Jones, Bruce and Dumais 2001).

Because of that upfront cost, any extra prompt at the moment of saving—asking for a title, a folder, or tags—discourages the actor from bookmarking at all. Collections that do get used grow steadily, are organised only in occasional bursts once the list becomes unwieldy, and accumulate entries that are never opened again or whose targets have moved or been deleted (Abrams, Baecker and Chignell 1998).

Therefore,

**Let the actor save any addressable item or view with a single action, and make their saved list reachable in one step from wherever navigation starts. Save immediately without prompting for a name, folder, or tag; let the actor rename or organise entries later through [incremental formalisation](/patterns/incremental-formalisation). Keep saved links resolving when targets are renamed or moved, and mark broken or deleted targets directly in the list rather than failing only after the actor clicks.**

Avoid overloading the bookmark action with secondary behaviours unless the actor expects them together. A star that also acts as a public endorsement, a pin that consumes limited layout space, or a follow that subscribes to [notifications](/patterns/notification) makes the mark mean two things at once and makes the actor hesitate to add or remove it.

## In practice

- *Bookmark.* An entry in a personal list owned by the actor, ordered by when it was saved or arranged manually. Show enough context—or a [link preview](/patterns/link-preview)—so the actor can recognise the target before opening it.
- *Pin.* Fixes an item to a persistent spatial slot, such as the top of a sidebar, tab bar, or message thread. Because screen space bounds the set to a handful of items, pinning suits active working items rather than long-term archives.
- *Favourite or star.* Doubles as a filter and sort key across a collection, and in shared workspaces is often visible to others as a rating or endorsement.
- *Follow or watch.* Subscribes the actor to [notifications](/patterns/notification) when the item changes, bringing updates to the actor rather than waiting for them to return.
- *Saved view.* Saves a collection's query, sort order, grouping, and visible attributes as a reusable perspective in a [data view](/patterns/data-view#saved-views).

## Research on this pattern

- Revisiting accounts for most browser navigation: [Tauscher and Greenberg (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1997.0125) measured a 58% revisit rate, and [Cockburn and McKenzie (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2000.0459) found 81% over longer observation windows. Most revisits are to pages seen minutes ago.
- [Obendorf, Weinreich, Herder and Mayer (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240719) grouped revisits into short-term (under an hour), medium-term (within a day), and long-term; long-term revisits are a small share of traffic but the hardest for automatic history tools to support.
- [Abrams, Baecker and Chignell (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1145/274644.274651) found that bookmark collections grow steadily, are organised in occasional bursts when lists become unmanageable, and contain many entries that are never visited again.
- [Jones, Bruce and Dumais (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/502585.502599) observed that people rely on multiple keeping methods at once—bookmarks, open tabs, self-addressed emails, and re-searching—choosing whichever has the lowest friction at the moment.
- [Kaasten and Greenberg (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/634067.634209) tested merging back, history, and bookmarks into a single recency-ordered list where bookmarks appear as explicitly marked history entries.
