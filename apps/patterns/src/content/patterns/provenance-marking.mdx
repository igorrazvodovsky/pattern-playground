* * *

**When machine-generated or machine-revised content sits in the same document as human writing or travels outside the tool that made it, an actor meeting a passage cannot tell whether they are reading a person's own words, a light machine edit, or an autonomous draft. Without a legible sign of machine involvement and origin, they cannot tell how to approach it.**

Where a surface already shows that a model is speaking, such as the assistant's turn in a dedicated chat thread, an extra badge adds noise without adding information. Provenance marking earns its place when machine output mixes with human writing on one surface, or when content leaves the artefact that made it. Once generated text is edited by hand or merged into a draft, what came from the model and what came from a person cannot be reconstructed after the fact unless it was stamped when written. Marking origin also changes what the unmarked means: the moment any part of a document or feed is labelled as machine-made, everything left unlabelled is read as a claim of human origin.

Therefore,

**Attach a mark of machine involvement and origin at the moment content is generated, revised, or exported. Keep the mark quiet where the surface already shows that the model is speaking, distinguish assisted revision from autonomous generation rather than stamping both with a binary badge, and bind the mark to the content itself wherever the output mixes with human writing or leaves the tool.**

## In practice

### Degrees of involvement

A single binary mark fails where collaboration is subtle. Stamping an entire document or passage with a generic machine badge collapses light editing into autonomous generation. When a model only polishes phrasing, recipients treat machine involvement as routine; when it generates the substantive argument, scrutiny rises. Collapsing these degrees into one mark misrepresents authorship and penalises the human contributor without clarifying what the machine produced.

### Co-writing traces during drafting

During composition, provenance marking serves the writer before it serves the recipient. Showing a granular trace of which spans were generated, which were shaped by a prompt, and which the author rewrote in their own words helps them regulate their own reliance before deciding how the finished piece is disclosed outward. Where a tool tracks span-level involvement during drafting, let the author toggle those marks while writing and correct attributions where they rewrote a suggestion in their own words.

### Forms of the mark

Across tools, the mark takes four recurring forms: disclosure labels on generated blocks and features, visual conventions reserved for machine output, span-level co-writing traces that distinguish generated from human-edited text, and watermarks or embedded file metadata readable by people or software after export. Interface chrome is enough while content stays in the frame that produced it; when a passage is copied, exported, or shared as a file, the mark has to travel with the content or file payload rather than stay in the surrounding chrome.

## Research on this pattern

- The disclosure paradox and third-party exposure: actors routinely state that machine involvement should be disclosed, yet they often rate content lower in authenticity, trustworthiness, and quality when a disclosure mark is present ([Sahebi, Formosa & Bankins, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108253)). However, being exposed after the fact by a third party or automated classifier incurs a significantly steeper trust penalty than proactive disclosure by the author ([Dykes, 2026](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/disclose-ai-paced/); [Schilke & Reimann, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01967-y)).
- Degree and role of involvement: reader reactions depend on the extent and character of the machine's contribution. Disclosing that a model edited text has negligible impact on perceived quality in informational writing, whereas disclosing autonomous generation lowers quality ratings ([Li, Liang, Peng & Yin, 2024](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.279)). Ratings of an author's sincerity and trustworthiness fall progressively as the disclosed proportion of machine involvement rises ([Nakano et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3742413.3789076)).
- Context and relational expectations: penalties for machine involvement concentrate in communications where personal commitment, sincerity, or human connection is expected, such as interpersonal correspondence or creative writing, while routine or transactional tasks show minimal penalty ([Nakano et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3742413.3789076); [Purcell et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.114092)).
- Writer and reader asymmetry: authors are significantly less likely than recipients to consider machine disclosure necessary, demonstrating a divergence in expectations across roles ([Fang, Wen & Lee, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3805689.3806724)). Making span-level provenance visible to writers during composition reduced the share of unedited AI text kept from 13.7% to 3.5% and increased writers' sense of ownership ([Hoque et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3641895)).
