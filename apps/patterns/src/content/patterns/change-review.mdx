* * *

**When the actor is asked to accept changes to content they are responsible for and sees only the changed version, they either re-read all of it and may still miss what changed, or accept it without checking. They need to see the change on its own: what was there, what would replace it, and in parts small enough to accept or reject one at a time. Without that, the decision is in effect made by whoever proposed the change.**

Two needs pull against each other here. The change is recorded in the units of the mechanism that produced it, such as lines of text, files or fields, but the actor judges it in units of meaning, and pieces that only make sense together are hard to accept or reject one at a time. Checking is only manageable if the review shows just what changed, but whether a change is right often depends on content around it that did not change.

The same arrangement appears wherever one person is responsible for a piece of work and another proposes changes to it. Copy-editors return manuscripts with each edit marked and questions in the margin, and the author accepts or rejects each one. Lawyers exchange marked-up versions of a contract. UK government departments publish Keeling schedules that show an existing act as it would read after a bill amends it. Architects mark the altered areas of a drawing with revision clouds. Wikipedia holds some edits back until a reviewer has checked the difference. In software it appears as tracked changes, suggestion mode, pull-request review, and accepting or rejecting individual changes in AI editors and coding agents.

Who proposed the change affects what the review is for. When the system made the change, the actor did not see the work being done, and the review is their main chance to check it. When a colleague made it, the review is where the two versions are reconciled.

Code review is where this situation has been studied most. Understanding the change takes most of a reviewer's effort, and changes split into related parts are easier to understand than changes split by file or line.

Therefore,

**Show the proposed change separately from the content it would alter: what is there now next to what would replace it, side by side or inline. Split it into parts the actor can judge on their own, show enough of the unchanged content around each part to judge it, and let the actor accept or reject each part separately.**

Some problems are outside what any view of the change can show. The faults code reviewers most often miss are omissions: places that should have changed but did not. Whatever the review leaves out, the actor will not check, so where omissions matter, the review needs a check over the whole content alongside it. An "accept all" control is useful once the actor has looked through the change, but if it is the first option offered, people use it instead of reviewing.
