The extent to which an artifact modifies its behaviour, presentation, or output on its own in response to the actor and their context.

Where [malleability](/patterns/malleability) describes how easily the actor can reshape the artifact themselves, adaptivity describes how the artifact reshapes itself for them. Both concern fit between actor and tool, approached from opposite sides of initiative.

## Trigger

What signal causes the artifact to change:

- *Stated*: explicit declarations such as locale, role, permissions, or preferences the actor set directly.
- *Observed*: actions the actor actually took during use, such as items they opened, views they configured, or suggestions they accepted or dismissed.
- *Inferred*: unstated intent, context, or patterns estimated from indirect signals and prior behaviour.

Moving from stated to inferred triggers lowers the effort required to configure the artifact, while raising the risk of misreading the actor's situation and making the cause of a change harder to trace.

## Scope

Which part of the artifact shifts when adaptation fires:

- *Presentation*: what is foregrounded, hidden, reordered, or formatted differently, such as layout, density, or locale conventions.
- *Behaviour*: how the artifact acts, including starting defaults, shortcuts, and where it pauses or proceeds on its own.
- *Output*: what the artifact produces, recommends, or generates in response to the current situation.

Presentation changes affect spatial memory and recognition. Behaviour changes affect predictability and control. Output changes affect relevance and trust.

## Legibility and reversibility

How readily the actor can read and govern what the artifact changed:

- *Visible*: the actor can tell that the artifact adapted, rather than mistaking an adapted state for a fixed default or missing content.
- *Predictable*: the actor can anticipate when a change will happen and whether repeating an action will produce the same result.
- *Scrutable*: the actor can inspect what signal, history, or assumption drove the adaptation.
- *Reversible*: the actor can undo, override, or lock a part of the artifact when an adaptation gets in the way.

Adaptation that is silent and irreversible forces the actor to work around a moving target. Treating adaptive shifts as inspectable and overridable preserves the actor's mental model of the tool.

## Stability over time

How adaptive behaviour holds across repeated sessions:

- *Responsiveness versus stability*: adapting too eagerly breaks muscle memory and established habits; adapting too slowly leaves the artifact stuck on outdated assumptions.
- *Drift and lock-in*: tailoring tightly around past behaviour can narrow what the artifact shows or offers, trapping the actor in a bubble and handling new or unusual tasks poorly.

An adaptive artifact needs room for the actor's needs to change without penalising deviation from past patterns.
