**People keep unrelated work apart by habit, in separate folders, desks, windows and accounts. A tool that holds all of their work in one space makes them do that separation by attention every time, and exposes half-finished or private work beside work that others can see.**

The boundaries that matter follow how work is already divided. By ownership, a personal workspace holds one actor's private work, a team workspace holds what a group shares, and an organisational workspace holds what everyone may read. These usually nest, and work moves outward through them as it goes from private drafting to team work to something the whole organisation uses. By topic, workspaces hold projects, areas or subjects. By activity, they separate active work from reference and archive. A workspace may be assembled for one project and disbanded when it ends, kept for years while it accumulates shared knowledge, or made for an afternoon's exploration and thrown away. Across all of these the workspace does the same three things: it decides what is present when the actor enters, who can see it, and what everything made inside it starts with.

The second of these is why a workspace boundary enacts [privacy](/patterns/privacy): personal workspaces are private by default, team workspaces admit their members, and organisational ones grant reading broadly and editing by role. The actor's [agency](/patterns/agency) is in deciding what belongs together, and in [collaboration](/patterns/collaboration-foundation) the workspace is where shared and individual work zones are drawn. The third is easy to overlook. A new file in a shared folder takes the folder's sharing settings; a new issue in a tracker project gets the project's fields and workflow; a file opened in a code editor follows the workspace's formatting rules, and completion suggests from the project's own names; a message posted in a channel falls under the channel's retention. Where a model works inside the workspace, the instructions and documents given to it at the workspace level reach every conversation started there in the same way.

What the actor inherits differs in how visible its effect is. A permission or a formatting rule is deterministic: it can be checked, and its effect shows as a refusal or a change the actor can see. An instruction or a document given to a model steers the result without leaving a trace, so the actor cannot tell from the output whether it applied. The costs are the costs of any boundary: each one is a switch the actor has to make, and work behind it is out of sight. The literature on co-located and shared work (Arvola's privacy gradients, Tidwell's arrangement of contexts) supports the division by ownership; the inheritance of defaults is observed across products rather than studied.

Therefore,

**Divide the work into workspaces along the lines where attention and access already divide: who owns it, what it is about, and whether it is active. Let each workspace decide what is present in it, who can see it, and what everything made inside it starts with, and let the actor switch between workspaces without losing the state of the one they left.**

Draw as few boundaries as the work needs. A workspace drawn along the organisation chart rather than along the work is crossed so often that it costs more than one shared space would, and the actor ends up working across boundaries instead of inside them. Inherited defaults also go stale: a rule or instruction set once at the workspace level keeps shaping what is made inside it after the actor has forgotten setting it, so show at the point of use what the workspace is applying and where it comes from, as permission screens do with "inherited from".

## In practice

### States

- *Empty:* a new workspace with nothing in it. Offer templates, recent items or starting points suited to the workspace's scope.
- *Single context:* one item fills the available space, and the workspace's own navigation may hide to give it room. Moving to several items should take one move.
- *Several contexts:* tabs, panels or windows are open, navigation and organisation controls come forward, and the active context is marked among the others.
- *Overflow:* more contexts are open than fit. Scrolling tabs, grouping or search reach the rest; overflow is often a sign that something should be archived, closed or reorganised.

{/* **Tabbed workspaces**: Discrete contexts accessible through labelled tabs. Only one context visible at a time. Optimised for limited sets of contexts (typically 5–7) where full attention on single context is primary mode. Tab labels provide peripheral awareness of other available contexts. */}
{/* **Panelled workspaces**: Multiple contexts visible simultaneously through spatial arrangement (side-by-side, tiled, or resizable regions). Enables comparison, reference, and parallel work across contexts. Fixed or user-configurable layouts. */}
{/* **Windowed workspaces**: Overlapping or free-floating containers users position and layer. Maximum flexibility for personal organisation. May create visual clutter; requires active window management. */}
{/* **Multi-device workspaces**: Contexts distributed across personal and shared devices.
