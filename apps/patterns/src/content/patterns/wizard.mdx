* * *

**Wizards are built far more often than the evidence supports. The case they serve is narrow: an unfamiliar goal whose later steps genuinely depend on earlier answers.**

The practitioner literature is nearly unanimous in their favour. Nielsen Norman Group recommends them for infrequent, complex tasks where the actor lacks a model of the work; the Windows guidelines, which gave the pattern its name, give page-level rules for building them; the design systems that ship one describe when to prefer it to a progressive form. Installers, setup assistants, tax interviews, checkout flows, and the create-a-resource pages of cloud consoles are all built this way, and people who would never finish the equivalent paper form do finish the interview.

The research below points the other way, and it is worth reading before building one. It does not say the pattern is wrong. It says the pattern is right in a smaller region than it is used in, and it helps to say where that region begins. In our experience it begins at two conditions together. The first is dependency: a later step genuinely cannot be asked until an earlier one is answered, because the answer decides which questions exist. A multi-page form without this is a long form with pagination, and it gives up the form's overview and buys nothing back. The second is unfamiliarity: the actor does the task rarely enough that they do not hold its structure, so showing one decision at a time is a relief rather than a confinement. Remove either condition and the single page is better. Add repetition, and even a wizard that was right the first time becomes the thing people complain about by name, which is why the cloud consoles that provision through five gated pages also offer a one-page form and a command line to anyone who has done it before.

Therefore,

**When one goal is made of dependent steps and the actor does not hold its structure, lay the task out as a corridor: one step at a time, each gated on the last and each addressing a single purpose, with the branching pruning what follows so the path walked is shorter than the whole. Show the position and the remaining length, validate at each step, keep what was entered through back and forward, and show the whole before the final commitment. Do not build one where the actor could see everything at once instead, and build a way around it for anyone who has walked it before.**

## Components

- Step indicator: shows the current position, the steps completed, and how much remains. Not knowing the length of the corridor is what makes people leave it.
- Step validation: inline feedback that confirms a step's input before the next step opens, so an early error does not cascade.
- Navigation controls: a primary action advances ("Continue", "Next"), a secondary action retreats ("Back"), a tertiary one abandons ("Cancel"). "Back" undoes the previous "Next" rather than merely browsing, so what was entered survives the round trip.
- State persistence: entered data is kept across steps and, where it can be, across sessions.
- Review step: everything collected is shown before the final commitment.
- Branching logic: later steps change according to earlier answers. This is the defining component; without it the wizard is a paginated form.

## Constraints

- Step integrity: each step addresses a single purpose. A step that needs tabs or an accordion to manage its own content, what the Windows guidelines call a burrito wizard, has failed to chunk.
- Bounded branching: roughly two decision points in the whole wizard and at most one nested branch within any of them. More than that and the corridor becomes a maze.

## Related components

- [Conversational form](/patterns/form#forms-and-conversation): Embeds form inputs within <ComponentRef id="components-messaging--docs">messaging</ComponentRef> interface, allowing flexible dialogue whilst collecting structured data. Better for actors who benefit from clarifying questions.

## Research on this pattern

- [The same health-care form as a single page beat its multi-page version on every measure](https://doi.org/10.2196/25787): a usability score of 76 against 67, faster completion, medium-severity errors in 31 per cent of single-page sessions against 69 per cent of multi-page ones, and a stronger preference, with participants saying they valued seeing all the information at once.
- [Dividing a form into sub-forms raised mental workload rather than lowering it](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858236), measured with near-infrared imaging while people filled the forms in.
- Suchman's account of situated action supplies the theoretical objection: "action is organized not simply by rule following but by the contingencies and improvisations of applying rules". A wizard fixes one sequence in advance and assumes the system knows it. Where the actor knows the task better than the system does, the sequence privileges the wrong party's model, and the actor experiences it as being marched.
- What remains untested: both studies compared paginations of one fixed form, not sequences where later steps depend on earlier answers, so they measure the pagination case rather than the one the pattern is reserved for.
