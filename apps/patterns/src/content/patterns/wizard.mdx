**People cannot fill in a page of fields whose later questions depend on answers they have not yet given, and when they meet such a task rarely they have no model to fill it by: they guess at which questions apply, answer ones that will be removed, and give up.**

A wizard works when two conditions are met:

1. *Conditional dependencies*: later steps depend directly on choices made in earlier steps. What the actor selects first decides which questions or configurations are relevant downstream.
2. *Task unfamiliarity*: actors perform the task infrequently (account setup, compliance configuration, complex provisioning) and have no established mental model. Presenting one decision at a time gives them clarity and reduces anxiety.

The sequence takes one of a few forms depending on how the dependencies hold: fixed, branching, grouped under milestones, or with optional steps at the end (each is described under In practice). What stays constant across them is the gate between steps and the one purpose per step.

Whatever its form, a wizard costs more than a single page. The costs are the same whether or not the wizard is justified:

- *Higher interaction cost*: every step adds navigation clicks, validation delays and page transitions.
- *Loss of context and visibility*: what was entered on earlier steps is hidden, so comparing, cross-checking and copying across steps is hard.
- *Interruption risk*: an actor who leaves partway loses their progress unless the system keeps it.
- *Rigid sequence*: the interface dictates the order. Experienced actors who know their inputs cannot jump straight to the fields that matter.
- *Poor fit for exploratory tasks*: open-ended, iterative work, such as editing media or composing a document, cannot be forced into a sequence.

Where neither condition holds, these costs buy nothing: paginating a long form without real dependencies hides the total effort, stops the actor scanning ahead or checking earlier inputs, and adds clicks and page transitions for nothing.

Therefore,

**Structure the task as a gated sequence of focused steps, each with one purpose and everything it needs on the screen, and let earlier answers remove the questions that no longer apply, with no more than one or two points at which the sequence branches. Show where the actor is and what remains; in each step pre-fill what the system can guess, put guidance beside the fields, and check the inputs before letting the actor advance; keep everything entered when they go back or leave; and end with a review of every answer, each linked back to its step, before the final commitment.**

Use a wizard only where a single page would not do. If a step needs tabs, accordions or long scrolling to hold its contents, it has more than one purpose and should be split. Beyond two branch points the step count jumps with each answer, and the actor loses track of how far they are. The pacing that reassures on first use slows the actor down by the fifth, so give returning actors a faster path than the steps.

## In practice

### Forms

- *Fixed*: the same steps in the same order for everyone. Justified only when a technical prerequisite forces the order, such as uploading a file before mapping its fields; otherwise a single-page form is better.
- *Branching*: earlier answers add, remove or change later steps, so the actor sees only the steps that apply. When the step count is not known up front, the progress indicator shows milestones rather than a count.
- *Grouped*: a long step splits into sub-steps under a milestone, and finished milestones collapse.
- *Optional steps*: non-essential configuration placed at the end, with a way to skip straight to the review once the required steps are done.

### Faster paths for returning actors

- Previous choices remembered and offered as the defaults.
- A single-page or expert mode that shows every field at once.
- A template or clone of an earlier run to start from.
- In technical systems, a command line or API for the same task.

## Research on this pattern

- *Single-page vs. multi-page performance*: In a controlled clinical trial comparing single-page, multi-page, and conversational forms ([Schnall et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/25787)), the single-page form significantly outperformed the multi-page version across all usability metrics: higher System Usability Scale scores (76 vs. 67), faster completion times, lower error rates (31% of users experienced medium-severity errors vs. 69% in the multi-page version), and higher user preference. Participants emphasized the value of seeing all information at once.
- *Cognitive workload of form chunking*: Neuroimaging research ([Pušnik et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858236)) using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) found that dividing a form into sub-forms actually increased mental workload compared to a unified layout.
- *Implications for practitioners*: These empirical findings highlight that arbitrarily chunking forms into wizards does not automatically reduce cognitive load. A wizard is only justified when real conditional dependencies or extreme unfamiliarity outweigh the usability benefits of full-page visibility.
