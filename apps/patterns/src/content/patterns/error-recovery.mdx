* * *

**The system has failed to do something the actor asked, and the actor did not cause it. Left unmarked, the failure reads as success, or as a wait that never ends. Marked badly, it reads as the actor's fault, in words written for the people who built the system. Either way the actor cannot tell what happened to their work, whether trying again is safe, or who to turn to.**

The guidance on this is old and agrees with itself. Nielsen's ninth heuristic asks that an error be stated in plain language, say precisely what went wrong, and suggest a solution (Nielsen 1994). Shneiderman's golden rules ask for simple error handling and easy reversal, so that a failure is never a dead end. Lewis and Norman (1986) widened the aim from preventing errors to designing for them: a system will fail, and the design question is whether the actor can see the failure and get back from it. The current design systems restate the same three parts, what happened, why, and what to do, and a reader can set Material, Polaris, Carbon, Atlassian and the Apple guidelines side by side and find them agreeing on the message while differing on the surface it arrives on: a banner, a whole page, a dialog, a line beside the control. Agreement on the rule and variety in the form is what marks a pattern rather than a component.

One distinction the design systems draw is the one this page rests on. GOV.UK keeps validation as its own pattern and writes separate pages for a service that is unavailable, a destination that is not found, and a request the service could not complete. The split is between a fault in what the actor gave and a fault in what the system did, and the two ask for different messages: the first says what to change, the second says what happened to the work.

The costs are in the claims the report makes. A report that names a cause the system has not established lies the way a progress bar stuck at ninety percent lies, and the actor learns to disbelieve the next one. A report for every passing hiccup is noise, and the actor learns to dismiss the one that matters along with the rest. A retry offered on a request that may already have taken doubles the damage: the double submission that charges a card twice or posts a message twice is a recovery path that was not thought through. And the question the actor asks first, whether the thing was saved, is often one the system cannot answer after a timeout; a report that guesses either way is worse than one that says it does not know and shows how to find out.

Therefore,

**Report the failure where the actor is looking, in their words, and in three parts: that the request did not take, what did and did not change, and what they can do now. Keep whatever they entered within reach. Offer the next move that fits the failure: try again where retrying is safe, wait where the fault is passing, go back where the destination is gone, and a way to reach a person where none of those will do. Say what the system does not know rather than guessing. Do not let the report stand in the way of the work that did not fail.**

Where the system can put the failure right on its own in a moment, let it, and report only what it could not. A report on every hiccup is the fastest way to teach the actor that reports can be ignored.

## In practice

### By what failed

- *The request failed and nothing changed.* The report goes beside the control that made the request, and retry is the next move. The commonest case and the cheapest to get right.
- *The request half took.* Say which parts did. The committed part is a change the actor did not want, so the way back is [undo](/patterns/undo), or a view of the two states where the parts cannot simply be reversed.
- *The request timed out and the outcome is unknown.* Say so. Offer the way to check, a refresh or the record of what was sent, rather than a retry that may double the effect or a claim of failure that may be false.
- *The destination is unavailable.* Not found, not permitted, or the service is down. These are whole-page reports, and each wants a different next move: a search or the way back for a missing page, a request for access where it is a matter of permission, the time to try again and where to watch for it where the service is out.
- *The connection is lost.* Keep what the actor is doing where the work can continue without the connection, queue what cannot, and show the state; the report is a standing one until the connection returns.

### The message

What happened, in the actor's terms and not the system's. Why, where the cause is known, and nothing where it is not. What to do, as an action the actor can take from where they are. Codes and identifiers come last, kept for the person who will be asked about the failure, not first as the thing the actor must read past. No blame, since the actor did nothing, and no joke, since the actor has just lost something.

### Where it lands

Beside the control, as a callout, when the actor is still looking at it. In the actor's path, as a dialog, only when they must choose before anything else can happen. As the whole page when the whole destination failed. As a [notification](/patterns/notification) when the actor was told they could leave and has. A transient message fits only a failure the system has already recovered from, which is a confirmation and not this move.

## Research on this pattern

- Nielsen (1994), [Ten usability heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/), the ninth: help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors. Restated with examples in [Error-message guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines/).
- Shneiderman, [Eight golden rules of interface design](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/goldenrules.html): offer simple error handling, and permit easy reversal of actions.
- Lewis and Norman (1986), "Designing for error", in Norman and Draper (eds), *User Centered System Design*. The case for treating recovery as a design aim beside prevention.
- Untested here: whether a report that states the scope of what took effect changes what the actor does next, compared with one that only names the failure; and how far the double-submission problem is understood by the actors who meet it.
