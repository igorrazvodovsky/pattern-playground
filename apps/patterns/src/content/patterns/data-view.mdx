The actor faces a collection too large to absorb whole, with a purpose that fixes what matters in it right now. A data view is a named projection of the collection — the same items shown from one angle, nothing copied and nothing altered — built from a *query* (which items), a *representation* (rendered how), and an *arrangement* (ordered and grouped by what). The familiar toolbar is that anatomy made visible: filter is the query, the view switcher is the representation, sort and group are the arrangement, and the attribute selector is the projection's ceiling put in the actor's hands.

The demo's `controls` prop is the designer-side switch over each piece of that anatomy: disabling a control enforces that part of the framing. An enforced framing is still a framing — just one the actor no longer negotiates.

## Representation switching

One collection as list, board, table, calendar — the move Notion, Airtable, and Obsidian Bases ship as their core product. The items don't change; the rung and geometry they are rendered at does, and each representation makes different comparisons effortless (a board makes status visible, a calendar makes time visible, a table makes attributes comparable). Representation switching keyed to container geometry rather than an explicit control — wide hosts get tabs, small ones a pile — is [semantic zoom](/patterns/semantic-zoom)'s resize-driven cousin.

## Attribute selection

Which attributes the compressed rung carries decides what the actor can scan, compare, sort, and filter by — and in the wild this ceiling is almost always fixed: in a sample of 156 sites, fewer than one in ten offered any customisation at all. Given the choice, actors split into recognisable temperaments — *hoarders* who surface attributes and never hide any, *minimalists* who prune the overview down to exactly their criteria — which is an argument for making the selection malleable rather than guessing a middle.

## Saved views

A framing worth constructing is worth keeping. Saved views persist the whole framing — query, representation, arrangement, visible attributes — as a named, shareable object, with three authorship poles: developer-defined defaults, role views an administrator curates for a team, and personal views the actor tunes for their own workflow. The lineage is old — Shneiderman's forgotten *extract* task, saving the sub-collection or the widget settings themselves — and the consequence is a lifecycle: saved framings go stale and accumulate, so pruning is part of the pattern, not an afterthought.

## Writable views

The collection's product core is edited *through* its projections: dragging a card between board columns is a write executed through the framing — the column move is an attribute change wearing spatial clothes. Two design decisions follow. Which edits are legal through which representation: the calendar accepts a date drag, the board a status drag, the table almost anything cell-by-cell. And how refused or ambiguous writes surface: an edit the view cannot translate back to the model (dragging a card into a column the query would immediately exclude it from) needs an honest refusal or a visible consequence, not a silent success. The database literature has owned this problem for decades as the view-update problem; product UI mostly ships it unnamed.

## Time

Time is a framing axis like any other: the same collection as current snapshot, trend, or full history — [temporality](/patterns/temporality) decides which the task needs.
