The actor holds two or more things — versions of one item, instances of a kind, subsets of a collection — and the question is the difference. Most tools answer by rendering each thing fully and leaving the comparison to the eye: two windows, two printouts, back and forth, the distinction assembled in the actor's head and lost when attention moves.

Comparing gives the difference its own representation. Shared features recede, distinctions stay salient, and the magnitude of each discrepancy is readable — the comparison is read, not performed.

## Three ways to show a difference

The visual-comparison literature names three families, combinable:

- *Juxtaposition* — the things side by side, alignment doing the work. The cheapest form and the weakest: the actor still carries the difference between glances.
- *Superposition* — the things overlaid in one space, difference read as displacement. Works where the compared things share a coordinate system.
- *Explicit encoding* — the difference computed and drawn as its own object: a diff, a delta column, a changed-region highlight. The strongest form and the most editorial, since something decided what counts as a change.
