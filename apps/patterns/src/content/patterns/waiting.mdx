* * *

**When an operation takes more than a moment and the interface shows nothing, the actor cannot tell whether the system is working, stalled, or broken. They may wait on a failed request, trigger a duplicate action by clicking again, or stay watching the screen when they could have switched to other work.**

### Forces

- *Flicker vs confirmation*: showing an indicator for sub-second pauses creates a disruptive flash, while withholding it past a second leaves the actor unsure whether their action registered.
- *Predictability vs false precision*: actors prefer a bounded wait to an open-ended one, but a progress bar or countdown that stalls at 90% because the system cannot measure remaining work erodes trust in future indicators.
- *Visible effort vs captured attention*: showing intermediate steps makes a wait feel shorter and increases perceived value, but live updates also invite the actor to stay and watch when a long task should free them to work elsewhere.

Therefore,

**Match the indicator to the duration of the wait and to what the system can measure, state when the actor is free to leave, and replace the indicator as soon as the request finishes or fails.**

Delay the indicator by 200–500 ms so sub-second operations show nothing. Use an indeterminate spinner or skeleton screen for waits of a few seconds, and show determinate progress in domain units ("3 of 12 files", or a stage checklist) once the wait passes ten seconds, reserving time estimates for operations whose remaining duration can be predicted reliably. When a task runs long enough for the actor to leave, move its status to a persistent surface such as a task tray, queue row, or [activity log](/patterns/activity-log).

## In practice

Search and generative systems often display current steps during a wait ("searching the web", "reading 3 sources"). Keep these labels tied to real system stages rather than decorative cycling text. Where the text exposes the model's reasoning rather than status labels, use [transparent reasoning](/patterns/transparent-reasoning); the test is whether the content remains useful to read after the result arrives.

## Research on this pattern

- [Miller (1968)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1476589.1476628) and [Nielsen (1993)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) establish the 0.1-second, 1-second, and 10-second response-time limits: under 0.1s feels instantaneous, up to 1s keeps the actor's train of thought, and past 10s attention shifts away from the task.
- [Myers (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1145/317456.317459) found that participants preferred a percent-done progress bar for waits over a few seconds, and preferred a longer, predictable wait to a shorter, unpredictable one.
- [Harrison et al. (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1294211.1294231) and [Harrison et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753556) showed that perceived duration depends on bar motion: progress bars that accelerate toward the end, or use backward-moving ribbing, are judged shorter than uniform bars of the same clock duration.
- [Maister (1985)](https://davidmaister.com/articles/the-psychology-of-waiting-lines/) proposed that occupied, explained, and finite waits feel shorter than unoccupied, unexplained, and open-ended ones.
- [Buell and Norton (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376) termed the preference for visible effort *operational transparency*: participants valued outcomes more and tolerated longer waits when shown the steps being performed on their behalf.
