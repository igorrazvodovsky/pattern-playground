**When generated output is nearly right and the actor can name what is off, re-running the request draws again without knowing what was wrong, restating the whole prompt repeats everything to change one thing, and editing by hand does the rewriting the system was meant to do.**

Unlike a new [prompt](/patterns/prompt), a revision instruction states a difference—"shorter", "warmer", "drop the second point"—against the output already in front of the actor. Describing a change in words pays off when one short instruction reaches across many sentences at once, such as shifting tone, length, reading level, or structure; when the actor already knows the exact word or figure they want at a single spot, describing the edit takes more effort than typing it directly. Because an instruction names a direction rather than the exact wording—many different rewrites satisfy "shorter" or "more formal"—the system has to choose what to cut or rephrase, and the actor still has to inspect how that direction was carried out.

Therefore,

**Let the actor state what to change about existing output—in their own words or through a short set of named presets—and apply that change to the output in place rather than generating again from the request. When the actor has selected part of the output, revise only that [selection](/patterns/selection) and leave the rest untouched.**

Reserve preset revisions for passage-wide adjustments such as shorter, longer, simpler, or more formal, and leave literal wording edits to [editing in place](/patterns/editing-in-place).

## In practice

### Presets, continuous controls, and open instructions

Preset verbs (shorter, longer, simpler, fix grammar) and continuous controls (length or reading-level sliders, or touch gestures that preview length as the actor pinches or spreads) save typing when the adjustment is a common dimension. A plain "rewrite" preset can sit with them: it names no direction, but it keeps what the passage says and changes the wording, so the system still works from the passage. When revision chips appear beneath a reply before the actor has judged it, they steer what the actor decides is wrong with the text, so keep an open instruction input beside them—and where actors type the same instruction repeatedly, let them save it as a reusable button.

### Applying the revision

In a [conversation](/patterns/conversation), a follow-up instruction returns a new turn and leaves the actor to carry the revised text back to their work. In a document or code editor, overwriting the selected passage in place is quickest when paired with [undo](/patterns/undo), while proposing the rewrite as an inline diff through [change review](/patterns/change-review) costs an acceptance step in exchange for showing what was cut, added, or rephrased before it enters the text.

## Research on this pattern

- Frequency in practice: follow-ups that adjust style or expand generated text appear in [18–30% of assistant writing sessions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.16023) across Bing Copilot and WildChat logs.
- Unrequested changes and drift: on revision instructions for research answers, even the strongest model [fulfilled only 59% of instructions and was weakest at adding specified wording, with unrequested changes (31%) and ignored instructions (21%) the commonest failures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03866). Subjective instructions such as "witty" or "enthusiastic" [frequently shifted tone toward harassing or demeaning phrasing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04113), and doctors refining drafted patient replies found [instructing less effortful than direct editing but prone to introducing errors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19940).
- In-place comparison and direct controls: keeping rewrites in place and letting writers save recurring instructions as buttons [reduced workload and improved revision ratings over chat-beside-editor workflows](https://doi.org/10.1145/3613904.3641899), inline edit proposals with factuality warnings [helped writers catch unsupported additions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15337), and on touch screens, pinching to shorten and spreading to lengthen text [worked when the gesture previewed the length change in flight](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713554).
