Several people work on the same thing behind separate screens. Each can scroll to a different part — that relaxation is what makes the shared workspace usable at all — so the workspace shows everyone everything except each other. What a shared desk answered in a glance now costs an interruption to ask, and people who cannot glance step on each other's work or stop trusting the shared copy.

Presence wears the others in the work as graded marks: an avatar stack says who is here, a named cursor and selection say where, an action signal says what, and the trail says who was here and did what. Each grade is added only where the surface's tempo needs it — a live canvas wants the cursor, an asynchronous tracker wants the trail — and every grade is captured as a by-product of the work itself, never authored as a report.

## Grades

- *The stack* — who has the document open now. The cheapest mark, and often enough.
- *The cursor and selection* — where each person is and what they hold, named and coloured. The grade that makes live co-editing feel occupied rather than haunted.
- *The action signal* — someone is typing here, someone is editing the same thing you are. The collision-prevention grade.
- *The trail* — who viewed, who changed what, when. The asynchronous face; on slow surfaces it is most of presence.

The directed forms — following another's view, pulling everyone to yours — are the same marks turned into a channel, and they border on shared-view territory rather than pure awareness.

- Dourish & Bellotti (1992) [Awareness and coordination in shared workspaces](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/143457.143468) — passive awareness through the workspace vs separate reporting mechanisms
- Erickson & Kellogg (2000) [Social translucence](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/344949.345004) — visibility's consequence chain, and translucence rather than transparence
- [Figma: multiplayer editing](https://www.figma.com/blog/multiplayer-editing-in-figma/) — cursor and selection as shipped context
