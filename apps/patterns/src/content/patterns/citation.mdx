There is a piece of information the actor needs to use. They did not write it, and they cannot see where it came from. Checking it properly means re-researching it, cost of which might be too big. Also, fluent wrong output is accepted exactly as readily as right output, because the surface gives nothing to tell them apart.

## Forces

- *Mark vs. follow.* The mark works on trust whether or not it is followed.
- *Document vs. passage.* A pointer to a whole document is cheap to produce and expensive to use: the actor has to find the supporting sentence themselves, and citation links "offer little guidance about what evidence they contain". A pointer to the passage is what makes the check short.
- *Staying vs. leaving.* Professional fact-checkers verify by leaving the page and reading laterally; hover cards and in-place excerpts keep the actor inside the answer.
- *Coverage vs. correctness.* Citing during generation buys precision and loses coverage; attaching citations afterwards covers more and supports less.
- *Every claim vs. the claims that need it.* Marking everything makes the marks noise; marking nothing leaves the contentious claim indistinguishable from the banal one.

## Solution

Citation attaches a pointer to each claim — a marker, a chip, a footnote — that resolves to the material the claim rests on. Render the marker so it can be read at a glance, previewed without leaving the answer, and opened into the source at the cited span. Place the sources where the actor meets them before any explanation of the answer, since a convincing explanation lowers the chance they look. Where a claim has nothing to point at, say so.

## Variants

Ordered by how far the pointer sits from the evidence.

### Passage at the claim

The marker resolves to the exact span, highlighted in the source, with the quoted text previewed on hover. NotebookLM's numbered markers open the document scrolled to the highlighted paragraph; Anthropic's Citations API makes the span the unit of the contract — each claim carries the cited text and its character range in a named document. Adobe Acrobat's inline highlights and Granola's transcript quotations are the same form on PDFs and recordings. The cost of following is lowest here; the risk is premature convergence on one source.

### Chip with popover

A domain chip at the end of the claim ("northjersey +3"), opening an anchored card with title, snippet, favicon, and 1/N paging where several sources back one claim. Perplexity and ChatGPT both use it. The "+N" collapses multiplicity without cluttering the prose; the snippet is scent, not the evidence.

## Research on this pattern

- Citation marks raise trust in generated answers even when the citations are [random](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01303) or [hallucinated](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06435), and trust falls when participants check them — an example of the [authority heuristic](https://www.issuelab.org/resources/875/875.pdf). 
- Following is rare: [most participants never open a source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08554), [answer engines draw a fraction of the source engagement search engines do](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22349), [links inside Google's AI summaries are clicked on 1% of visits](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/), and [whether an actor verifies at all is set more by prior disposition than by the answer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28498).
- [Unfolding evidence in place deepened what readers took from sources](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00361), though it measured reading depth, not reliance. Separately, [sources reduced overreliance where explanations increased it](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08554) — the basis for placing sources before explanations in the solution.
