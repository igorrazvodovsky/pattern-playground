* * *

**Most of what an actor opens is something they had open recently. Without a direct way back, they have to recall where it lived, what it was called, or which search terms found it, and otherwise search or navigate the hierarchy again.**

Browser studies consistently show that 58–81% of page visits are revisits, and that most returns happen within the same hour or day rather than weeks later. A short, deduplicated list of recently visited items covers a large share of those returns at zero upfront cost to the actor.

That lack of manual effort also creates the pattern's main failure modes. If the system logs every hover, preview, or notification glance, noise quickly crowds out intentional work. Conversely, during a busy task, rapid navigation can push an important item off the list before the actor returns to it. And because the list records behaviour automatically, it can expose sensitive items on a shared screen or during a screen share.

Therefore,

**Record the items, views, and searches the actor intentionally opens or edits, ordered by most recent use, and surface the list where navigation starts: in [empty workspaces](/patterns/state-empty), [command menus](/patterns/command-menu), and [search fields](/patterns/searching) before typing. Deduplicate entries so a repeatedly opened item appears once, keep the list short enough to scan, and let the actor remove individual items or clear the history.**

Keep recents separate from bookmarks and pinned items. Mixing automatic history with manually saved items makes the list unpredictable and hides which items the actor explicitly chose to keep.

## Research on this pattern

- Revisiting accounts for most browser navigation: [Tauscher and Greenberg (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1997.0125) measured a 58% revisit rate and showed that a short recency-ordered list of distinct pages predicts a large share of next visits; [Cockburn and McKenzie (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.2000.0459) found an 81% revisit rate over longer observation windows.
- [Obendorf, Weinreich, Herder and Mayer (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1240624.1240719) grouped revisits into short-term (under an hour, served by the back button), medium-term (within a day, served by recents), and long-term (served by bookmarks or search).
- [Adar, Teevan and Dumais (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357241) showed that revisitation rhythms vary by page type, meaning a single expiry rule does not fit all content equally well.
- [Kaasten and Greenberg (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1145/634067.634209) tested combining back, history, and bookmarks into a single recency-ordered list.
- [Findlater and McGrenere (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985704) and [Todi, Bailly, Leiva and Oulasvirta (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445497) showed that dynamically reordering primary navigation harms spatial memory and slows selection; keeping recents in a dedicated section preserves the stability of the main navigation structure.
