The system will act on cases the actor is not there to see — files it will move, messages it will
hide, suggestions it will stop making. Correcting each case as it appears works until the same
correction comes round for the third time, at which point what the actor wants to change is not the
case but the condition. Rule composition is the move of writing that condition down: assembling
clauses over fields, operators and values into a statement the system will run, and doing it in a
surface where the statement can be read back and checked. Practitioners call the surface a rule
builder, a filter builder, a query builder or a segment builder, after whichever object it composes.

The condition is the hard part. Ur and colleagues surveyed 318 people on what they wanted a smart
home to do and found 78% of the answers fitted a single trigger paired with a single action — the
work is not in chaining steps, which is [workflow](/patterns/workflow)'s territory, but in saying
precisely enough when the one step should happen.

## Forces

- *Combination against legible grouping* — conditions need and, or and not, and the trouble is not
  the operators but where the grouping sits. Comparing a graphical query builder against SQL over
  the same tasks, Young and Shneiderman recorded fifty parenthesis errors in SQL against two
  ordering errors in the graphical equivalent, with composition accuracy more than doubling. A
  surface that punctuates precedence reproduces the SQL result; one that shows grouping structurally
  does not.
- *Exactness against the actor's vocabulary* — the rule must resolve to fields the system holds, and
  the actor's reason for wanting it usually does not. "Stop suggesting things I have already read"
  becomes a clause over a read-state flag, which is close but not the same, and the difference only
  shows up in the cases that fall between.
- *The rule against the set* — a rule composed in isolation runs among the rules already in force.
  Actors hold specific wrong models of how it joins them: that two rules with opposite actions
  cancel out, that rules fire in the order they are listed, that a condition attached to one rule
  constrains the others. Each of those produced debugging failures in Zhang and colleagues' study.
- *Foreseeing effect* — the hard part of authoring a rule is not writing it but estimating what it
  will do to live cases before it runs. Brackenbury and colleagues showed 153 participants
  pre-written rules, half carrying one of ten bug classes, and found that for most classes the bug
  made participants worse at predicting what the rule would do. A rule does not tell its reader what
  it does, so the surface has to.

## Anatomy

The literature that studies automation rules settles on three parts, and the distinction between the
first two is where actors go wrong:

- *Trigger* — a statement over events, which happen at a moment. "The file is added to the folder."
- *Condition* — a proposition over states, which hold across an interval. "While the project is
  archived."
- *Action* — what the system does when the trigger fires and the conditions hold.

Huang and Cakmak identified the event/state confusion in 2015 and argued it is made by the
interface: the plain "if this, then that" form gives an instant and an interval the same wording.
Seven years later Zhang and colleagues found the confusion in 21 of 50 unaided debugging sessions,
and every one of those sessions ended in failure. Grouping and naming states separately from events,
and using different conjunctions for each, is an interface fix for a problem that reads like a
comprehension one.

Two further parts belong to the composed rule where the domain has them: *scope*, which bounds what
the rule may touch, and *exceptions*, the named cases that override it.

## Showing the effect before committing

Because the text does not carry the behaviour, the surface has to show effects rather than the rule.
Three forms, in descending order of how well they are evidenced:

- *Replay against the record* — apply the changed rule to what actually happened and show what would
  have come out differently. Zhang and colleagues built exactly this and it was the component that
  moved participants' success rates; it is the strongest form because the cases are real and the
  actor already has opinions about them.
- *Simulate against the standing set* — step the rule through against the rules already in force, so
  interactions and loops surface before the rule is saved. This is what Corno and colleagues'
  EUDebug checks, and it is the only form that addresses the rule-against-the-set force.
- *Sample the boundary* — show the cases that newly match, the ones that match but only just, and
  the ones that fail but only just. Noessel proposes this for tuning an agent's triggers, on the
  grounds that a random sample of results will not contain the cases that decide whether the rule is
  right. It is a designer's proposal rather than a studied one, and it is the form to reach for when
  there is no record to replay against.

Where the rule's object is a visible collection, the preview collapses into the composition itself:
a [filter](/patterns/filtering) that updates its result count as each clause lands is
effect-preview running continuously, which is the oldest form of the move.

## Entry points

Composition rarely starts from an empty clause list, and should not.

- *From the case that went wrong.* The strongest entry is the misfire itself — the message that was
  hidden, the suggestion that should not have been made — with the rule opened beside it and the
  clauses that matched it highlighted. The actor arrives already knowing which part is wrong.
- *From a generated draft.* The system proposes a rule and the actor edits it, from a stated
  intention, from the corrections it has been given repeatedly, or from watching what the actor does
  by hand. Ur and colleagues found more than 100,000 people sharing rules on IFTTT and duplicating
  each other's freely, which is the same preference expressed socially. Selecting among proposed
  drafts is its own difficulty: in Zhang and colleagues' study the synthesiser produced the right
  patch, and only half to three-fifths of participants picked it.
- *From a scratch space.* Comparing query builders for professional search, Russell-Rose and
  colleagues found a temporary holding area — somewhere to park a clause that is not yet in the
  rule — was one of the useful things a canvas gave that a line-by-line form did not.

## Exceptions beside rules

Not every correction should become a rule. "Never show me this one again" settles the case without
touching the condition, and for a low-stakes domain that is the right cost. The list of exceptions
then becomes an object in its own right: it needs reviewing, clearing, and starting over, or it
accumulates until the composed rule no longer describes what the system does. The exception and the rule
are two rungs of the same escalation, and a surface that offers only the second makes small
corrections expensive.
