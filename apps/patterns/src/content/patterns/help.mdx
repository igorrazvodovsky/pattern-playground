* * *

**The actor needs an answer in the middle of their work, and the artifact can say only so much on its surface. Answers kept anywhere else are a second telling of the artifact. They go out of date when it changes, use names that differ from the ones on screen, describe features this actor does not have, and are hard to find with the words the actor has.**

What the actor came for decides what the answer has to look like. Four needs recur, and each needs a differently shaped answer:

- *Doing a task*: steps toward a goal the actor already has, in the order they will take them, with the branches real work runs into. The actor wants to get back to work, not to read about it.
- *Looking something up*: what a setting, field, value, limit or shortcut is, laid out the way the artifact itself is laid out, so the actor can find the entry from where they stand.
- *Understanding why*: how the artifact or the domain works and why it is built that way. This is read when the actor steps back from the task; mixed into steps, it slows both down.
- *Learning the basics*: a guided first experience with a known outcome. Inside the artifact this is the first run that onboarding designs, and help keeps it available to replay.

Each question also concerns either the interface (where to click, what a field accepts) or the domain (business rules, regulations, how the data relates), and any of the four needs can be about either. Finding where something lives is not a fifth need: it is how the actor reaches any of the four, through search, an index or a command menu. Recovering from an error is not one either. It begins with the report of what failed and turns into a task or an explanation.

Where an answer lives is the main design decision. Answers placed in the interface are found at the moment of need and change with it, but they fix the guidance to one path and take space on every view. Tasks and facts move into the interface well: steps become controls, a wizard or an agent's actions, and facts become hints beside the fields they describe. Reasons mostly stay outside, since they are read away from the work. Answers kept outside have room for detail, but the actor has to leave their work to reach them, and they go out of date without anyone noticing.

Help is pulled. It reaches the actors who think to ask, and says nothing to those who do not know there is something to ask about. Newcomers often cannot name the feature or the term their problem involves, so they search with the wrong words, and only a minority of their attempts to find help succeed.

Agents now read help too. The artifact's own agent answers questions from it, and an [outside agent](/patterns/outside-agent) the actor brings reads it to learn what the artifact holds and what it can do. A page that has gone out of date then misleads more than one reader, and can turn into a wrong action taken for the actor.

The words are usually written by someone other than the person designing the interface, but the design decides what help has to exist and what it can rely on: names that stay stable, places it can link to, and a way to know what applies to this actor.

Therefore,

**Answer each question where it comes up, in the shape of what the actor came for: steps for a task, an entry for a fact, a reasoned account for a why. Move tasks and facts into the interface where they fit, and keep reasons and longer material in a place outside it that the artifact links to from the point of need. Give both places the artifact's own names, let each link to the other, and show the actor the version that applies to their plan, role and release.**

Every page kept outside the interface has to be kept true as the artifact changes, or it goes on teaching the old one. Give each page an owner and say which release it describes. Where a control needs a page to be understood, fixing the control usually serves the actor better than writing the page.

## In practice

### Where each kind of answer lives

| Need | In the interface | Beside it | Outside it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doing a task | [wizard](/patterns/wizard), [template](/patterns/template), an agent doing the task | contextual help panel | help-centre article |
| Looking something up | [input hints](/patterns/form#input-hints), tooltips, shortcut hints | glossary, shortcut sheet | reference pages |
| Understanding why | [explanation](/patterns/explanation) of one event, [validation](/patterns/validation) messages that say why | "learn more" sidebar | conceptual articles |
| Learning the basics | first run, [empty states](/patterns/state-empty) that teach, [starter examples](/patterns/starter-example) | replayable first-run guidance | courses, walkthroughs |

### Documentation outside the interface

The help centre or documentation site is a separate place, but the interface decides most of what it can do:

- *Names*: pages use the labels the actor sees on screen. A renamed label is usually the first thing that makes a page wrong, so renames go through the pages that mention them.
- *Addresses in both directions*: the interface links to the page for the control at hand, and the page links back into the interface, to open the setting it describes or the view it explains.
- *What applies*: a page that describes a feature this actor's plan, role, platform or release does not have sends them looking for something that is not there. Let the actor pick the version, and pre-select it from what the artifact knows about them.
- *Agents as readers*: publish the pages in a form a program can read, such as plain text or a documentation server an agent can query, laid out the way the artifact is laid out.

## Research on this pattern

- Newcomers rarely find help with their own words: in an observational study of people learning a complex design application, [Kiani et al. (2019)](https://hci.cs.sfu.ca/KianiCHI2019.pdf) found that only 38.2% of help-seeking attempts succeeded, largely because participants could not identify which feature or term applied to their problem.
- Pull help fails on awareness: [Grossman, Fitzmaurice and Attar (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1518701.1518803) separate knowing that a capability exists from knowing how to use it, and help that waits to be asked serves only the second.
- People act before they read: [the paradox of the active user](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/paradox-of-the-active-user/) describes people starting their task at once instead of learning the system first, which is why answers placed at the point of need get read and manuals do not.
