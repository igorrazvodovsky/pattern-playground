* * *

**The system does most things well enough, and a few things wrong in the same way every time. Each time, the actor corrects it. The correction works, and the next session starts from the same defaults, so the actor makes it again. Nobody notices the cost of one correction; the cost is that it never stops.**

The defaults cannot be right for everyone, and the preferences that differ are mostly not the kind a settings screen anticipates. An actor who wants British spelling, no headings, one tool rather than another, or a house rule about how tests are run has no axis to set; they have a sentence. Writing that sentence once is cheap. What is not cheap is knowing it needs writing: people often cannot name a preference until they have corrected the same output three times, and a correction made in the flow of work does not announce itself as a candidate for keeping. Where the system writes its own account of the actor, the actor's handle on it is partial and the words are not theirs; where the actor writes, the words are theirs and the system's reading of them is what varies.

Therefore,

**Give the actor a place to write, in plain language, what should hold across sessions, and read it at the start of every run. Keep it as the text they wrote, where they can find and change it, rather than as an inference about them. Scope it by where it lives, so a rule for one project or one kind of file does not reach the rest. Make it easy to put a correction there from the place the correction was made.**

The instruction is read by a system that interprets, so it holds in the way a note to a colleague holds, not the way a setting holds. Say what it is for as well as what to do, keep each entry short, and do not let it stand in for a rule that has to be exact.

## In practice

### Promoting correction to an instruction

- The actor promotes it by command from the place they made it: a slash command, a phrase ("add this to the instructions"), or a tool that appends what they said to the file. 
- The system saves on its own and shows a receipt ("Saved 2 memories"), which is [memory's](/patterns/selective-memory) territory unless the actor can read and edit what was saved as text. 
- The system drafts an entry and the actor approves, edits or dismisses it before it takes effect

## Research on this pattern

Vaithilingam et al. call the artefact an intent specification ("cursorrules, CLAUDE.md files, user directives, AI memory of user intent"), a human-authored document that grounds the system's decisions, and show that as it grows new entries conflict with old ones and need something like impact analysis before they are committed. Neumann, Pi and Singh find that the actor's instructions sit at the bottom of a hierarchy they cannot inspect, and that people "consistently desire greater transparency and control" over it. Li et al.'s walkthrough of six assistants finds the same verb (delete, forget) producing local or global effects depending on which layer it reaches, and model customisation records complicating control further. Wang et al. compare labelling examples, writing rules and writing prompts as ways to teach a personal classifier: prompting performs best at first, all three stall at refinement, and people blend them, which is the evidence that instruction, composed rule and demonstration are three moves rather than one. Gao et al. show the system inferring a preference description from repeated edits and showing it for correction, the system-written counterpart of the actor's text.
