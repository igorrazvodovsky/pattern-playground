**People faced with an empty input box rarely have their intent already formulated in words: they know what they want to accomplish in rough terms, but leave critical criteria, boundaries, and contextual details unstated because an open field offers no cues to help them externalise tacit thought.**

An open text field offers complete freedom of expression, but that freedom assumes the actor already has their intent formulated and merely needs to transcribe it. In practice, intent is often tacit, fragmented, or incomplete until external questions force a choice. Staring at a blank prompt, an actor struggles to anticipate which dimensions need deciding—such as the target audience, boundary conditions, governing standards, or reference sources—and leaves them out.

One common case occurs when the task-required parameters and constraints are known in advance. In repetitive, high-constraint work—such as drafting compliance memos, extracting structured data, or adhering to standardised project rubrics—the questions that need answering can be predicted before the actor begins. In these cases, turning those questions into labelled slots, bounded controls, or pre-submission checks relieves the actor of having to remember and articulate the structure from scratch, preventing critical omissions before the request runs.

However, static slots impose their own costs. Slot-based scaffolding pays off only when the structure of the problem is genuinely known ahead of time. On open reasoning, exploratory writing, or argumentative tasks, rigid slots constrain thinking and lead to worse results than free-form prose. Furthermore, pre-articulated suggestions can anchor actors prematurely, reducing the variety of solutions without improving quality. Structure also calcifies: a scaffold reflects its author's past assumptions about what an inquiry should consider, and it continues to enforce those conventions until redesigned.

Therefore,

**Break the prompt into visible, labelled slots for the questions and parameters known in advance, let the actor supply or select each part independently, and inspect the completeness of the request before it runs.**

Apply scaffolding when the parameters of the task can be anticipated beforehand. Avoid rewriting or expanding the actor's prompt invisibly behind their back: if the system elaborates a request, it should present the additions as inspectable changes the actor can review or reject. Where the task is open-ended or the relevant questions cannot be predicted in advance, abandon static slots and let an [open request](/patterns/open-request) or [agent inquiry](/patterns/inquiry-agent) draw out missing details in conversation instead.

## In practice

### Pre-submission quality feedback

Rather than segmenting the entire input into separate form fields, an interface can preserve an open prompt composer while evaluating the text against known criteria as the actor types. Quality feedback inspects the draft for completeness, missing context, and task constraints, offering non-blocking suggestions or warnings before the request runs.

<Demo spec="prompt-quality-feedback" label="Prompt quality feedback" />

### Configurable controls

When specific task parameters recur across prompts—such as audience, scope, output format, or reference sources—bind them to dedicated controls beside the input rather than asking the actor to reconstruct them in prose every time.

## Research on this pattern

- *Intent reification and task boundaries*: [Xu, Ma & Chen (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19114) (*OOPrompt*) found that reifying intents into structured, slot-based prompt artifacts improves performance on repetitive, highly constrained, and hierarchical tasks, but fails on open reasoning or argumentative tasks where rigid slots restrict problem framing.
- *Error reduction via pre-generation elicitation*: [Scott-Jackson (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09593) showed that structured pre-generation elicitation rescued underspecified prompts, reducing off-target model outputs from 34% to 5% at the cost of modest additional preparation time.
- *Suggestions versus active inquiry*: [Maier, Schneider & Feuerriegel (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23324) demonstrated that while conversational model inquiry significantly raised the quality and diversity of user specifications, pre-articulated prompt suggestions reduced diversity without raising overall quality.
