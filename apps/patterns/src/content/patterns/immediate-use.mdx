* * *

**People want to explore, evaluate, or use an artifact without committing personal details or creating an account up front. Forcing registration before demonstrating value turns curiosity into an administrative hurdle and drives actors away.**

### Forces

- *Evaluation vs commitment*: actors want to determine whether an artifact solves their problem before trading credentials, attention, and privacy; systems often demand an account before revealing any utility.
- *State vs identity*: an artifact needs memory to be useful—keeping drafts, settings, and work in flight—yet continuity does not require a named identity record.
- *Utility vs abuse*: open access invites automation, spam, and rate-limit exhaustion, which tempts makers to gate the front entrance rather than protecting specific expensive operations.

Therefore,

**Allow full or substantial interaction with the artifact without requiring an account or proof of identity, storing work in client-side state, address parameters, or temporary sessions until the actor asks for something that needs an identity.**

Protect abuse-sensitive boundaries (such as public messaging, heavy model execution, or resource-intensive exports) with targeted friction or rate limits rather than demanding an account for simple exploration.

## In practice

### One-off actions and checkout

For transaction-oriented artifacts or discrete services, allow the transaction to proceed with minimal contact details required for fulfilment, without forcing the creation of a password or profile.

### Temporary session boundaries

Communicate clearly when state is ephemeral. If clearing browser data or switching devices will cause work to be lost, present a discreet reminder indicating that the current session is temporary.
