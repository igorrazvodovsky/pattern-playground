The work is stored continuously or many times over, and at some point the actor needs a state it no longer has. It may be the state before a mistake noticed days later, the version that went to the client, the one that was approved, or a direction they abandoned and now want back. Undo cannot reach any of these: the session has ended, or too many changes and too many people came between.

Version history keeps past states of the work as states, not as a list of actions, and lets the actor open one to inspect it, copy from it, or restore it. Most states are captured automatically, because people rarely save a version on purpose just before they break something. Naming keeps the history usable as it grows. Some moments name themselves, such as a publish or a release, and others are named by the actor when they reach something worth keeping. Restoring is recorded as a change of its own, so nothing after the restored state is lost.

## Naming and retention

An automatic history grows faster than anyone can read, and a list of timestamps gives no sign of which states mattered. Naming is the main answer, and it raises three questions for the design:

- *Which moments name themselves.* Publishing, sending, releasing, and approving are already deliberate acts, so they can mark the history without asking anything more of the actor.
- *Who names the rest.* Leaving all naming to the actor means the important states often go unnamed, because their importance is only clear later. Letting the actor name a past state after the fact helps.
- *What happens to unnamed states as they age.* Many systems keep every state for a short period and fewer as they get older, while keeping every named state. How far back the history goes is a policy decision with privacy and storage costs, not only a convenience.

## More than recovery

Interviews with creative practitioners describe version histories used in ways a recovery feature does not anticipate. People treat past versions as a collection of parts to draw on, rely on the history to give them confidence to try risky directions, keep low-fidelity captures rather than complete ones, and revisit versions over long periods. A design that offers only "restore this version" serves the first case in this page's situation and misses the others. Copying part of an old state into the current one, and opening an old state beside the current one, are as much a part of the pattern as restoring.

Where a history branches, so that several lines of work continue from one state, bringing them back together is a separate problem, covered by [reconciliation](/patterns/reconciliation).

## Where it is practised

- Document editors with a version history that groups automatic snapshots, lets the actor name a version, and restores or copies any of them.
- Design tools that add a named version on publish and let the actor add their own.
- Version control, where every commit is kept and tags and releases name a few of them.
- Operating system backups that keep hourly snapshots for a day, daily ones for a month, and weekly ones after that, and restore a single file or the whole set.
