# Charts

Chart selection is multi-dimensional. The right chart depends on what data you have, how much of it there is, what you want to reveal, and which visual encoding you choose. These four axes are independent — you can enter the decision from any of them.

## What data do you have?

The dataset structure constrains which chart families are viable before any other consideration.

### Dataset type

<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Type</th><th>Description</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Tables</td><td>Rows and columns; the most common case</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Tree</td><td>Parent–child hierarchy with a single root</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Network</td><td>Nodes and edges; no single root</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Spatial data</td><td>Geographic or positional coordinates</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

### Data types within the dataset

Combinations of categorical, ordinal, and quantitative variables determine how many meaningful chart types remain.

- *Cat* — pure categories, no inherent order
- *Quant* — continuous numeric values
- *Cat, Quant* — the most common combination; unlocks bar, scatter, line families
- *Cat, Ord, Quant* — adds ranked categories; enables parallel coordinates and heatmaps
- *Quant, Quant* — two numeric dimensions; scatter and bubble territory

## How much data?

Scale is an independent constraint that eliminates candidates regardless of data type or analytical goal.

<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Dimension</th><th>Values</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Variables</td><td>1 / 2 / 3 / 4 or more</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Categories</td><td>under 10 / 10–99 / 100–999 / 1,000+</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Data values</td><td>under 20 / dozens / hundreds / thousands / effectively infinite</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

A bar chart works under 10 categories; beyond that it degrades. A choropleth needs a fixed region set. A scatter plot tolerates thousands of points; a pie chart does not tolerate more than five.

## Why are you building it?

Two parallel frameworks for the analytical goal — Munzner's target taxonomy and a domain-usage taxonomy. Both are valid entry points.

### Munzner: target of interest × analysis aim × query

<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Target</th><th>Analysis aim</th><th>Analytic query</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Trends — overall shape or direction</td><td>Explain — communicate a finding</td><td>Compare — magnitudes, ranks, change</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Features — specific patterns or peaks</td><td>Discover — explore for unknowns</td><td>Identify — locate a specific value or outlier</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Outliers — anomalies and exceptions</td><td>Discover and Explain — both</td><td>Summarise — aggregate, describe distribution</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

### Alternative: domain usage

A more intuitive entry point for most design contexts — what the chart *is about* rather than what cognitive operation it supports.

<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Usage</th><th>What it shows</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Time</td><td>Change across a sequence; temporal order on the x-axis</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Distribution</td><td>How values spread; shape, spread, skew, outliers</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Association</td><td>Relationship between two or more continuous variables</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Comparison</td><td>Relative magnitude across discrete categories</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Flow</td><td>Volume moving between states or nodes</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Hierarchy</td><td>Containment and parent–child structure</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Network</td><td>Connections with no implicit hierarchy</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Spatial</td><td>Values tied to geographic or positional coordinates</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

## How are you encoding it?

Marks and channels are the elementary vocabulary. Some chart families are defined by their mark; within a family, channel choice is a design decision.

### Marks

<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Mark</th><th>Typical chart families</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Points</td><td>Scatter, bubble, dot plot, strip plot</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Lines</td><td>Line chart, area, parallel coordinates, step chart</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Areas</td><td>Area chart, treemap, choropleth, streamgraph</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Connection</td><td>Network, chord, arc, Sankey</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Containment</td><td>Treemap, circle packing, Euler/Venn</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

### Channels

<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Channel</th><th>What it encodes well</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Position</td><td>Quantitative values; highest accuracy for readers</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Colour</td><td>Category membership or magnitude (use sparingly for magnitude)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Size</td><td>Quantitative values; works for magnitude, not fine differences</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Shape</td><td>Category membership; limited to ~5–6 distinguishable shapes</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
